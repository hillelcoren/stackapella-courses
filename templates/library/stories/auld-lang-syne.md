Auld Lang Syne, also printed as Auld Langsyne and known by its first line, Should Auld Acquaintance Be Forgot, is a Scots song by Robert Burns. Its title means "old long since": the days long ago [^hh4][^dick]. Its question is whether old friends should be forgotten, and its answer is a drink together, "a cup o' kindness", for the sake of those days [^smm5]. Burns sent it to a friend in 1788 as an old song he admired, and its earliest known printing came in Edinburgh in 1796, a few months after he died, to a slow old tune [^gebbie3][^dick][^smm5]. The tune sung at New Year all over the world joined the words in 1799, in George Thomson's collection of Scottish airs [^thomson][^dick][^mccue].

## Where it comes from

**The letter of 1788.** On 17 December 1788 Burns wrote to his friend Frances Dunlop: "Apropos, is not the Scotch phrase, 'Auld lang syne,' exceedingly expressive? There is an old song and tune which has often thrilled through my soul." He copied the verses on another sheet and added: "Light be the turf on the breast of the heaven-inspired poet who composed this glorious fragment!" [^currie][^gebbie3][^dick][^loc]. That copy differs from the printed song: its first verse and chorus call for "a waught o' Malaga", a long drink of Malaga wine [^dick][^wallace]. That sheet later crossed the Atlantic to Albany, New York [^dick][^loc][^wallace].

**The old man's singing.** In September 1793 Burns sent the song to George Thomson, an Edinburgh collector of Scottish airs: "The air is but mediocre; but the following song, the old song of the olden times, and which has never been in print, nor even in manuscript, until I took it down from an old man's singing, is enough to recommend any air" [^gebbie5][^dick][^stenhouse]. No two of his copies match: James C. Dick, who printed Burns's songs with their tunes in 1903, knew at least four in his hand, and the Library of Congress counted six in 2013 [^dick][^loc].

**The Museum, 1796.** Burns also gave the song to James Johnson, an Edinburgh engraver, for The Scots Musical Museum [^dick][^smm5]. Its fifth volume is undated; the National Library of Scotland dates it 1796. Its preface mourns "the late celebrated Scottish Bard" and prints a letter he wrote "a few weeks before his death", so it appeared after Burns died on 21 July 1796; Dick puts it at the end of that year, and thought it very probable that Burns had seen the song's engraved page [^smm5][^rpo][^dick]. On page 426, No. 413, "Auld lang syne" is set in D to the old tune, with the chorus "For auld lang syne my jo" [^smm5][^stenhouse]. No author is named; the song is signed Z, which the Museum's index explains as "old verses, with corrections or additions" [^smm5][^stenhouse][^hh3].

![[2]]

**The tune we sing.** In 1799 Thomson printed the song in the second volume of his Select Collection of Original Scottish Airs, No. 68, headed "From an old MS. in the Editor's possession"; Dick points out the manuscript was Burns's own, not five years old [^thomson][^dick][^gla]. His lively tune, marked "Allegretto", was arranged by the Bohemian composer Leopold Kozeluch [^thomson][^mccue]. It came from a strathspey, The Miller's Wedding, in Bremner's collection of reels of 1759, later also called The Miller's Daughter [^glen][^dick][^stenhouse]. Burns had already written "O can ye labour lea" to a form of it, printed in the Museum in 1792 [^glen][^mccue][^dick]. In November 1794 he told Thomson that "the other tune you may hear as a common Scots country dance", which Dick reads as this one [^gebbie5][^dick].

![[3]]

**The older songs.** The opening line was old. The Bannatyne Manuscript, written in 1568, has a poem on forgotten friendship, "Auld kyndnes foryett" [^dick][^stenhouse][^mccue]. James Watson printed a love song, "Old-Long-syne", in 1711, beginning "Should old Acquaintance be forgot, And never thought upon" [^watson][^dick][^hh3]. Allan Ramsay's "Should auld acquaintance be forgot, Tho' they return with scars" followed in 1720, and the Museum's first volume set it to the old tune in 1787 [^glen][^dick][^smm1]. Henry Playford had printed that tune in London in 1700 as "For Old long Gine my Joe" [^glen][^dick].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Burns only wrote down what an old man sang. What the record shows: no such song has been found, and Thomson came to think Burns said it "in a playful humour" [^dick][^hh3]. Stenhouse, after the editor Cromek, said Burns admitted only three stanzas were old; Dick calls this "a mere repetition of Cromek", who gave no authority for it [^stenhouse][^dick]. Often told too: the English composer William Shield wrote today's tune. He did use it to end the overture to his opera Rosina, staged in London in the early 1780s [^stenhouse][^glen]. Henley and Henderson's 1897 edition calls the tune Shield's, but Glen and Dick found that the strathspey came first and that Shield never claimed it [^hh3][^glen][^dick].

## What the words mean

The song is in Scots, the language of Lowland Scotland. Auld is old, lang is long and syne is since or ago [^hh4][^dick][^dsl-syne]. "For auld lang syne" means for the sake of old times.

**Verse 1.** Should old friends be forgotten and never brought to mind, and the old days too? The Museum ends "And auld lang syne!"; Thomson asks "And days o' lang syne?" [^smm5][^thomson].

**Chorus.** For the old days, my dear, we will still take a cup of kindness. Jo is a sweetheart or a dear friend, and tak is take [^hh4][^dick][^gebbie6].

**Verse 2.** Surely you will have your pint-stoup, and I mine: a stoup, or stowp, is a tall jug with a handle [^gebbie6][^dick].

**Verse 3.** We two (twa) have (hae) run about the braes, the hillsides, and pulled (pou'd) the gowans, the daisies; but we have wandered many (mony) a weary foot (fitt) since (sin) then [^hh4][^dick].

**Verse 4.** We two paddled (paidl'd) in the burn, the stream, from the morning sun till dine, dinner-time at midday [^dsl-dine][^gebbie6][^hh4][^dick]. But broad (braid) seas have roared between us since [^hh4][^dick].

**Verse 5.** Here is my hand, my trusty fiere, my friend, and give me a hand of yours [^hh4][^dick]. We will take a right gude-willie-waught: a waught is a long drink, and gude-willie means hearty, given with goodwill, so a deep drink of friendship [^dick][^hh4][^gebbie6].

The Museum puts the pint-stoup verse second; Thomson moved it to the end, as most later books do, and has "my dear" for "my jo" [^smm5][^thomson][^dick].

### The full words

These are Burns's words as The Scots Musical Museum printed them in 1796, No. 413, with spelling and punctuation as printed. The first verse and chorus stand under the notes, and we set them out as lines; after each later verse the page prints "For auld &c.", calling for the chorus again. The footnote on "cup" reads "Some Sing Kiss in place of Cup" [^smm5].

**Verse 1**
Should auld acquaintance be forgot
And never brought to mind?
Should auld acquaintance be forgot,
And auld lang syne!

**Chorus**
For auld lang syne my jo,
For auld lang syne,
We'll tak a cup o' kindness yet
for auld lang syne.

**Verse 2**
And surely ye'll be your pint stowp!
And surely I'll be mine!
And we'll tak a cup o' kindness yet,
For auld lang syne.

**Verse 3**
We twa hae run about the braes,
And pou'd the gowans fine;
But we've wander'd mony a weary fitt,
Sin auld lang syne.

**Verse 4**
We twa hae paidl'd in the burn,
Frae morning sun till dine;
But seas between us braid hae roar'd,
Sin auld lang syne.

**Verse 5**
And there's a hand, my trusty fiere!
And gie's a hand o' thine!
And we'll tak a right gude-willie-waught,
For auld lang syne.

## How it is sung

In Scotland it is sung at midnight on Hogmanay, New Year's Eve, in a circle holding hands; on the last verse everyone crosses their arms and takes the hands on either side [^edinburgh][^scotorg]. It is sung at partings too; a Glasgow Burns scholar calls it "the global song of parting" [^gla][^dick]. In the 19th century it spread through social gatherings, Masonic ones among them, and the theatre, and in Scotland it took the place of "Good night, and joy be wi' you a'" as the song that ends a festive night [^mccue][^dick].

Our arrangement takes the tune from Thomson's page, in F, written in 4/4 with every note value doubled, and Burns's words from the Museum, with its "and auld lang syne" [^thomson][^smm5]. We sing the first verse for 2 to 4 singers: the soprano sings the tune, the alto and tenor sing the words with it, and the bass holds the roots on "oo". Three held notes, "mind" among them, are shortened for breaths.

## Versions and other words

In the same 1799 volume Thomson set Burns's "Now Spring has clad the grove in green" to an "Air, The Hopeless Lover", which Dick shows is the old Auld Lang Syne tune renamed [^thomson][^dick].

## Did you know?

- The Museum's page has a footnote that "Some Sing Kiss in place of Cup", and Henley and Henderson thought the note was probably Burns's own [^smm5][^hh3].
- Thomson's page prints the chorus for two voices: a second singer, written in the bass clef, sings the same words under the tune [^thomson].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/auld-lang-syne/soprano): you sing the tune, from middle C up to the D a ninth above it. The second line climbs A, C, D on "brought to mind?", and the D is your top note, held; breathe in the rest, then start the third line on that same D. On "lang" you step D to C before a long F on "syne?".
- [Alto](/songs/auld-lang-syne/alto): five low notes, from A below middle C up to E. You open on A for the first four syllables, then rise through C to E on "be" and rock D, E on "forgot". "Never brought to" sits on C, and your long notes end the lines: B flat on "mind?", A on "syne?".
- [Tenor](/songs/auld-lang-syne/tenor): your home note is F. You start just below it on E for "Should", step up to G on "be forgot" and to A on "-ver brought to", and come back to F each time. The moment to practise is the last word: from F you drop a fourth to low C and hold it for "syne?".
- [Bass](/songs/auld-lang-syne/bass): ten notes on "oo", from low F to the F an octave above. After a short A pick-up you hold F, C, F and B flat, three or four beats each. The third line leaps up to high F. At the end the chords quicken: B flat, C, then F.

## Questions

### Why do we sing Auld Lang Syne at New Year?

Because in Scotland it is the Hogmanay song, sung at midnight in a circle of joined hands, and its words, old friends drinking to old times, suit the end of a year [^edinburgh][^scotorg]. Guy Lombardo's Canadian dance band played it at the stroke of midnight in its yearly New Year's Eve broadcasts, heard across North America [^tce][^natgeo]. Just why it became a world song is, a Glasgow Burns scholar says, still something of a mystery [^mccue].

### What is the original tune of Auld Lang Syne?

The earliest known printing of Burns's words, in 1796, uses an older, slower tune also called Auld Lang Syne, which Henry Playford printed in 1700 [^smm5][^mccue][^glen][^dick]. The tune sung today is a Scottish dance tune, printed as the strathspey The Miller's Wedding in 1759, which George Thomson set to the words in 1799 [^glen][^dick][^thomson]. The old tune is the more reflective [^mccue][^gla].

### How do you pronounce Auld Lang Syne?

Say "syne" like the English word "sign", with an s, not "zyne" [^dsl-syne][^npr]. The Dictionary of the Scots Language gives it as [səin], and in 1991 the head of music at BBC Radio Scotland told American public radio that most people get it wrong [^dsl-syne][^npr].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, from middle C up to the D a ninth above [^thomson]. With two singers the alto sings beneath it; the bass on "oo" makes three and the tenor four. All but the bass sing the words together, so it is a good first song for holding your own line.
