Now Is the Month of Maying, spelled "Now is the moneth of Maying" in the book's table of contents, is an English part-song for May by Thomas Morley [^morley1595]. It is a ballett, a light dancing song in which every half ends in "fa la" [^morley1597][^fellowes1920]. Morley printed it in London in 1595 as No. 3 of The First Booke of Balletts to Five Voyces [^morley1595][^imslp-balletts]. The same year his printer issued the book with Italian words, and there the song begins "So ben mi c'ha bon tempo", the first line of an Italian song by Orazio Vecchi printed in Venice in 1590 [^morley1595it][^imslp-vecchi][^chilesotti]. On May Morning in Oxford, the choir of Magdalen College sings it from the top of the college's tower [^magdalen2024][^oxmag].

## Where it comes from

**The composer.** Thomas Morley was born in 1557 or 1558 and was a pupil of William Byrd [^fellowes1921][^dnb][^concise]. He took his music degree at Oxford in 1588, was organist of St Paul's Cathedral in London, and in 1592 became a Gentleman of the Chapel Royal [^fellowes1921][^dnb][^concise]. After books of canzonets in 1593 and madrigals in 1594, he "struck out a new line" in 1595, as Fellowes put it, with a set of balletts [^dnb][^fellowes1921]. The Chapel Royal filled his place in the autumn of 1602, and he died about then. The Concise Oxford Dictionary of Music gives 1602; Fellowes, in 1921, gave 1603; and the historians Hawkins and Burney gave 1604, as the Dictionary of National Biography noted in 1894, adding that the date is uncertain [^concise][^fellowes1921][^dnb].

**The book of 1595.** The title page of the top voice's book reads "Cantus. Of Thomas Morley the first booke of Balletts to five voyces. In London by Thomas Este", with the year in Roman numerals [^morley1595]. There is no score: each of the five singers had a separate book, Cantus, Altus, Tenor, Bassus or Quintus, holding only that voice's notes [^morley1595]. Morley dedicated the set to Sir Robert Cecil, one of the queen's Privy Council, and signed the dedication in London on 12 October 1595 [^morley1595][^fellowes1921]. He likens his "simple Compositions" to the "Milke" that people of the old world offered their gods when they had no incense [^morley1595]. A short poem headed "M. M. D. to the Author" follows: Morley lacks the charm of Orpheus, who drew stones and beasts, but need not envy him; let him "Draw thou the Shepherds still and Bonny-lasses" [^morley1595].

Now is the month of Maying is No. III, on one page of each book [^morley1595]. In the Cantus book the page opens with a large capital N. The first stanza runs under the notes, which are diamond-headed, as music was printed then, and each half of the song is written out twice. The other two stanzas are printed below as verse [^morley1595].

![[2]]

**The Italian twin.** In the same year Thomas Este printed the whole book again with Italian words, as Il primo libro delle ballette a cinque voci, dedicated in Italian to Cecil [^morley1595it][^imslp-balletts]. Its No. III carries the same music, but the words begin "So ben mi c'ha bon tempo" [^morley1595it][^imslp-balletts]. That is the opening of an aria for four voices with lute by Orazio Vecchi, printed in Venice by Angelo Gardano in 1590 in Vecchi's Selva di varia ricreatione, a book of madrigals, arias, dances, dialogues and more [^chilesotti][^imslp-vecchi]. Morley's ballett is not Vecchi's music with new words. Set beside Chilesotti's transcription, Vecchi's aria is for four voices and lute and Morley's for five, and their tunes run differently: Vecchi's top voice climbs D, E, F, G, A in quick notes, while Morley's repeats its G and steps up to B [^chilesotti][^morley1595]. The music theorist Megan Kaes Long describes Morley's balletts as new pieces loosely modeled on Italian ones, where earlier English editors had put English words to unchanged Italian pieces [^long2015]. In 1597 Morley named Vecchi among the composers a student of madrigals should take as guides [^morley1597].

![[3]]

**The English words** are not a translation. The Italian words keep a teasing secret, while the English is an invitation to go out and dance in May. No poet is named in the book [^morley1595]. Fellowes noted that the poet Michael Drayton had been suggested as the compiler of the set's poems, without naming any evidence, and IMSLP lists him as their attributed author [^fellowes1920][^imslp-balletts]. Fellowes listed ten of the set's balletts as translated from Gastoldi; Now is the month of Maying is not among them [^fellowes1920].

**What a ballett was.** Two years later, in A Plaine and Easie Introduction to Practicall Musicke, Morley described "an other kind of Ballets, commonlie called fa las" [^morley1597][^olemiss]. He called it "a slight kind of musick" and wrote that "as I take it" it was "deuised to be daunced to voices" [^morley1597][^olemiss]. Fellowes later explained the fa la as a purely musical interlude, the kind instruments might play, which unaccompanied voices had to sing on some syllables [^fellowes1920].

![[4]]

**What happened next.** The set was printed again in English in 1600 [^dnb][^fellowes1921]. In 1921 Fellowes wrote that it had become better known than any other English madrigal book, and named Now is the month of Maying first among its favourites [^fellowes1921].

### Timeline

- 1590: Orazio Vecchi prints So ben mi ch'a bon tempo, an aria for four voices and lute, in his Selva di varia ricreatione (Venice: Angelo Gardano) [^chilesotti][^imslp-vecchi]
- 1595: On 12 October Morley signs the dedication of The First Booke of Balletts to Five Voyces, printed in London by Thomas Este; No. III is Now is the month of Maying [^morley1595][^fellowes1921]
- 1595: Este prints the same book with Italian words; its No. III begins "So ben mi c'ha bon tempo" [^morley1595it][^dnb][^imslp-balletts]

- 1600: The Balletts are printed again in English [^dnb][^fellowes1921]
- 1602: Cesare Negri's Milan dance book Le gratie d'amore includes a couple dance to Vecchi's song; it is reissued in 1604 as Nuove inventioni di balli [^chilesotti][^negri1604]

## What the words mean

Morley printed three stanzas; our arrangement sings the first [^morley1595]. Stanza by stanza, in our own words:

**Stanza 1.** It is May, the month of "Maying": going out early to the woods to bring back green branches and flowers, then dancing round a maypole. In London, the parishes held their own "mayings" during the month [^strutt]. The young men ("merry lads") are out enjoying themselves, each with his "bonny lass", his pretty girl, on the "greeny grass".

**Stanza 2.** Spring, dressed ("clad") in gladness, laughs at winter's gloom. To the sound of the bagpipes (printed "Bagpips") the "nymphs", the poem's pastoral word for the young women, dance their steps ("tread out their ground").

**Stanza 3.** "Fie then": come on, for shame. Why do we sit here lost in thought ("musing"), turning down the sweet pleasures of being young? Speak up, pretty nymphs: shall we play barley-break? Barley-break was a country chasing game for three couples, each a man and a woman holding hands. One couple stood in the middle ground, called "hell", and had to catch the others [^fellowes1920][^nares][^strutt]. An old epigram quoted in Nares's glossary jokes that the last couple left in hell passed the time kissing [^nares]. So the last line is a flirtation as well as a game.

**The Italian words.** In our own plain words, and roughly, since the lines are in dialect, the Italian words of No. III in Morley's Italian book say: I know well who is having a good time, I know it, but enough said. Its second stanza: I know who is the favourite, but alas, I cannot say [^morley1595it]. Morley printed only that one extra stanza; Vecchi's own song goes on for more [^chilesotti].

### The full words

These are the three stanzas of Morley's 1595 Cantus book, No. III, with the long s written as s [^morley1595]. The first stanza stands under the notes there, so its line breaks, its capital on "Vpon" and leaving out its sung fa las are ours; the other two are printed as verse, each with its "Fa la la."

Now is the month of Maying,
When merry lads are playing,
Each with his bonny lasse,
Vpon the greeny grasse.

The spring clad all in gladnesse,
Doth laugh at winters sadnesse. Fa la la.
And to the Bagpips sound,
The Nimphs tread out their ground. Fa la la.

Fye then why sit wee musing,
Youths sweet delight refusing. Fa la la.
Say daintie Nimphs and speake,
Shall wee play barly breake. Fa la la la.

## How it is sung

Morley described balletts as songs that could be danced to as well as sung [^morley1597][^olemiss]. It was sung from part-books, each singer reading only a single line [^morley1595].

It belongs to May Day. In Oxford on May Morning, the choir of Magdalen College climbs the Great Tower and at 6 a.m. sings the Hymnus Eucharisticus, a hymn from the 17th century [^magdalen2013][^oxmag]. Then it sings madrigals, Now is the month of Maying among them, before the bells ring out [^magdalen2024][^oxmag].

Our arrangement is read from E. H. Fellowes's edition of 1913, its accidentals checked against the 1595 part-books [^morley1595]. We sing the first stanza and its fa las, each half twice, as Morley wrote them out [^morley1595]. Morley's five voices become four: the soprano sings the tune at the top, and his two tenor parts are joined into one.

## Did you know?

- Vecchi's song was danced as well as sung. Cesare Negri, a Milan dancing master, printed a "Balletto So ben mi chi ha buon tempo" for a man and a woman, with its tune and a lute tablature, in his dance book of 1602, reissued in 1604 [^negri1604][^chilesotti].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/now-is-the-month-of-maying/soprano): you carry Morley's dancing tune, up on the top D on "playing", on "his bon-" and "lass" and in the fa las. Listen for the C sharp on "are" just before "playing"; it pulls up into the D. In the second half's fa las you run up the scale from the low D, one note to each half beat.
- [Alto](/songs/now-is-the-month-of-maying/alto): your tune sits close under the soprano, mostly on G and F sharp. Watch the F naturals: the first "Fa la", "with" and "up-" are a half step lower than your usual F sharp. You reach A only in the second half, on "his bon-" and in the fa las, leaping up to it from E after "grass".
- [Tenor](/songs/now-is-the-month-of-maying/tenor): you sing Morley's two tenor parts in one line, most often on D, up to F on "his". On the "-y" of "greeny" and on "grass" you have G sharp, then C sharp, the bright notes of those chords. In the second half's fa las you walk down to your low E while the soprano runs up.
- [Bass](/songs/now-is-the-month-of-maying/bass): you are the foundation, mostly D and G, from the low G on "Now", "Maying" and "When" up to A on "bonny". After "grass" you start the fa las first, with the tenor, and then lead the rising scale, G, A, B, C, D, that the alto and soprano take up a beat apart.

## Questions

### What does Now Is the Month of Maying mean?

It is an invitation to celebrate May. It is the month of "Maying", when young people went out to bring in green branches and dance [^strutt]. The lads are out with their girls on the grass, spring laughs at winter, and the young women dance to bagpipes. The last stanza teases those sitting still: why refuse youth's pleasures, so shall we play barley-break, a chasing game for couples [^morley1595][^fellowes1920][^nares]?

### Who wrote Now Is the Month of Maying?

Thomas Morley composed the music and printed it in London in 1595 [^morley1595][^fellowes1921]. Nobody knows who wrote the English words: the book names no poet, and Michael Drayton's name has been suggested, though Fellowes gave no evidence for it [^morley1595][^fellowes1920]. Morley's Italian edition sets the same music to the opening lines of a song by Orazio Vecchi, printed in Venice in 1590 [^morley1595it][^chilesotti].

### Is Now Is the Month of Maying a madrigal?

In the broad sense, yes. Morley called it a ballett: among the kinds of light music he counted the madrigal the best, and the ballett "another kind more light", made for dancing, with its "fa la" refrains [^morley1597][^olemiss]. But balletts have always been printed and sung among the English madrigals, and Fellowes called Morley's set the best-known English madrigal book [^fellowes1921]. It is polyphonic: five voices, each in its own part-book with a line of its own [^morley1595].

### Is Now Is the Month of Maying for SATB?

Morley wrote it for five voices: the title of his book says "to five voyces", and the song appears in all five part-books [^morley1595]. Our arrangement is SATB. It keeps three of Morley's parts as they are and joins his two tenor parts into one, so four singers can sing all of it. It needs all four parts, as the tenor holds notes that complete the chords.

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, the top line of the song [^morley1595]. But in a ballett every voice has a line of its own, and in the second half's fa las the bass leads, starting a rising scale that the alto and then the soprano take up.
