Who Will Bow and Bend Like a Willow, often shortened to Who Will Bow and Bend, is a brisk worship song of the Shakers, a community that "worshiped with their own unique songs and dances" [^shakermuseum][^andrews]. Its first strain asks who will bow and bend like a willow and turn and twist and reel in the wind of "simple freedom". The second asks who will drink "the wine of power", which it calls "Mother's wine", and the third answers: "O ho I will have it" [^hazzard][^andrews]. Its maker is unknown: Mary Hazzard copied it into her songbook unsigned and undated, near songs of early 1847 [^hazzard][^hazzardbook]. Edward Deming Andrews printed its words in 1937 and its tune in 1940, placing its start at Canterbury, New Hampshire, about 1843 [^andrews1937][^andrews].

## Where it comes from

**The revival.** The song belongs to the great Shaker revival that broke out in the fall of 1837, which the Shakers called Mother Ann's Work after their founder, Mother Ann Lee. In it worshippers received "gift songs" they believed came from the spirit world [^andrews][^wintcoll]. Andrews put its most fantastic years between 1842 and 1845. At the outdoor feasts of those years, he tells, worshippers shouted, staggered and bowed low, and in many songs the singers acted the words out, some of them reeling "as if intoxicated" [^andrews]. Andrews counted union, "freedom from bondage" and humility among the themes the songs of the 1840s kept coming back to, and this song names union and bondage outright and acts out the humility [^andrews].

![[1]]

**Canterbury.** Andrews's note on the song says it originated "about 1843 at the Canterbury community" and appears to have spread widely, and that several New Lebanon songbooks copy it, sometimes as "Laughing John's Interrogatory" [^andrews]. Canterbury was a Shaker village in New Hampshire [^andrews][^shakers-canterbury]. In the same note, which quotes Henry C. Blinn's account of the revival, "Laughing John" was a "simple-minded" spirit "manifested in a medium". His laugh, Blinn wrote, "was passed from one to another, till the whole body, young and old, would burst out with one merry peal of laughter" [^andrews][^blinn].

**The songbook.** The copy we sing from is in the songbook of Mary Hazzard, a sister of the Church Family at New Lebanon, New York, the village at the head of the Shaker world [^andrews][^shakermuseum]. Her title page dates its start to 7 February 1847 and calls it "A Collection of Extra Songs of Various Kinds Written and Pricked For the Purpose of Retaining them": pricked, that is, with the tunes set down as well as the words [^hazzardbook][^wintcat]. Many songs in it carry the name of the village or family they came from, and the Winterthur Library in Delaware keeps it as ASC 893 [^wintcat][^wintcoll][^hazzardbook]. The same book holds [Simple Gifts](/songs/simple-gifts), whose story says more about it [^hazzardbook].

**The page.** This song fills the book's page 18 and the top of page 19 [^hazzard]. Nothing heads it, and nothing at its end says where it came from, who gave it or when. Each line of words has its tune written under it in Shaker letteral notation, the pitches as the letters a to g, and strokes and ties on the letters giving the rhythm; this tune keeps mostly to c, d, e and g, with a run of a's in the last strain [^hazzard][^wintcoll]. A small "3", the Shakers' mark for the third of their four speeds, stands above a crossed double bar at the head of the first line [^hazzard][^andrews]. Each of the three strains ends with repeat dots, and the last line, "Shake out all the starch & stiffening.", runs over to the top of page 19, above a march headed "Advent's March" [^hazzard].

**Its neighbours.** The song on pages 16 and 17 is signed "Second Family Caterbury Feb 22nd 1847", with Canterbury spelled short of its first n, and "Eternal Praise" on page 20 is signed "Second Family Canterbury. April 1847." [^hazzardbook]. Just before our song, page 17 has a short unsigned song, "Simple Intercourse"; above that, the Canterbury song of 22 February ends "Well well I'm willing to reel reel reel reel into freedom" [^hazzardbook]. So the copy sits two songs from each of these Second Family songs. That fits Andrews's Canterbury, though the page itself names no place [^hazzard][^hazzardbook][^andrews].

## What the words mean

**First strain.** "Who will bow and bend like a willow, who will turn and twist and reel?" Each verb is a movement of the revival's worship [^andrews]. A willow bends low in the wind and springs back. To "reel" is to whirl, or to stagger as if drunk; Hazzard spells it "real" [^hazzard]. The wind here is a "gale of simple freedom", blowing from "the bower of union". A gale is a strong wind and a bower a leafy shelter. The Shakers' Summary View of 1848 says humility is needed to keep "the union and harmony of the church of God" [^summaryview]. And "simple" names a virtue, since the same book counts simplicity among its twelve Christian virtues [^summaryview][^andrews].

![[2]]

**Second strain.** "Who will drink the wine of power, dropping down like a shower?" In the revival, spirits brought gifts that existed only in vision: fruit, robes, and "the wine of Mothers love", Mother being Mother Ann [^andrews][^andrews1937]. A Shaker account quoted by Andrews says the gift of spiritual wine made those who drank it "stagger and reel like drunken people" [^andrews]. A "drinking" song from New Gloucester, Maine, that Andrews prints sings almost the same picture: "Ho, ho I've been a-drinking out of Mothers good wine", which "sets me turning, twisting, reeling" [^andrews]. So "Mother's wine is freely working" pictures that wine at work in the singers, as new wine works, or ferments, and with it comes "pride and bondage all forgetting". A Canterbury hymn printed in 1852, "Precious Counsel", asks the same of its singers: "O brethren and sisters, be humble and low, O live in sweet union" [^sacredrep].

**Third strain.** The answer is joyful: "O ho I will have it, I will bow and bend to get it", reeling, turning and twisting. "Shake out all the starch and stiffening": starch stiffens cloth, and in Shaker songs stiffness goes with pride. Another song Andrews prints, from a Maine hymnal, sings of brushing off "pride and stiff" until "limber as a withe", a bendy willow twig [^andrews].

### The full words

Hazzard's two pages give the song as below, her spelling kept ("real", "Mothers", "&"), in lines that follow the music's phrases [^hazzard]. Her repeat signs have each strain sung twice. Andrews's printing of 1940 has "From the bower of union flowing" where Hazzard wrote "blowing"; his earlier printing, in 1937, has "blowing" too [^andrews][^andrews1937][^hazzard].

Who will bow and bend like a willow
Who will turn and twist and real
In the gale of simple freedom
From the bower of union blowing,

Who will drink the wine of power
Dropping down like a shower
Pride and bondage all forgetting
Mothers wine is freely working

O ho I will have it
I will bow and bend to get it
I'll be reeling, turning, twisting
Shake out all the starch & stiffening.

## How it is sung

Its home was the Shaker meeting of the revival years, where a song like this was acted out as it was sung [^andrews]. In 1937 Andrews set it among the "drinking" songs, whose singers, he wrote, often acted out receiving and draining cups [^andrews1937]. Andrews marked it "fairly brisk, sub-allegro" in the Shaker way of naming speeds, at 106 quarter notes a minute [^andrews]. Our recording takes it at about half that speed, for learning.

We take tune and words straight from Hazzard's two pages, in C, with each of her letters as written [^hazzard]. Shaker letters show how high a note is only by where the letter sits on the page, so the octave is a reading [^wintcoll]. The crossed double bar is the Shaker sign for the measure, and the 3 above it marks the third speed, which the Shakers' rules gave to quick dance tunes [^andrews]. We read the measure as 2/4, as Andrews printed it, since every bar holds two quarter notes [^andrews]. Our score doubles every value to write it in 4/4, and follows her repeat dots, so each strain comes round twice. Only spelling changes: "reel" for "real", "Mother's" for "Mothers", "and" for "&". Her notes give "bower" one syllable and "union" two. One word could be read "gate" as easily as "gale"; we follow Andrews's "gale" [^hazzard][^andrews]. Beginners get marked places to breathe. The arrangement is for 2 to 4 singers, and since the page carries only the tune, the alto, tenor and bass parts are our own.

## Versions and other words

Andrews printed the words in 1937 with no tune, place or date, as "also a quick song" [^andrews1937]. In *The Gift to Be Simple* (1940), Andrews gives the song as No. 30, "Who Will Bow and Bend Like a Willow", set in E flat with two beats to the bar [^andrews]. Besides "flowing" for "blowing", his words have "Oh ho!" and "stiff'ning!" [^andrews]. He also reads the octaves differently, setting parts of the second and third strains an octave below ours, so his tune never climbs past the C above middle C (in our key) and ends on middle C [^andrews]. He names a second title, "Laughing John's Interrogatory", from New Lebanon songbooks; an interrogatory is a set of questions, and the song asks "Who will ...?" three times before it answers [^hazzard][^andrews].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/who-will-bow-and-bend/soprano): the tune is yours, most often on E. The first strain sits low, between middle C and G, and "Who will turn" and "In the gale" each start on two middle Cs. The second leaps up: "will drink the wine" is on the C above middle C, and "of" and the "pow-" of "power" reach your top E. Then, after "shower", you drop an octave to middle C on "Pride". The third strain stays high, with "O ho" held on that upper C, and ends on it on "stiffening".
- [Alto](/songs/who-will-bow-and-bend/alto): you sing the words a third or a sixth below the tune almost all the time, mostly a third, starting on the A below middle C, your lowest note. In the first strain you join the tune on its middle C, on "will" in "Who will turn" and on both notes of "In the". In the second strain you sing most of "Dropping down like a shower" on B, your top note, five times running, then drop back to that low A on "Pride".
- [Tenor](/songs/who-will-bow-and-bend/tenor): your first strain stays low, mostly on G and E, and dips to your lowest note, the C an octave below middle C, only on "bow" in its first line. On "will" in "Who will turn", where the soprano and alto share middle C, you sing the E below them. In the second strain you climb step by step, G, A, B, C, on "the wine of power" and sing "Dropping down like a show-" on your top D, dropping to A for "-er". The third strain sits mostly on middle C, rising to D on "Shake out all the".
- [Bass](/songs/who-will-bow-and-bend/bass): you sing "oo", starting on a low A, so the song opens on an A minor chord before it settles in C. You hold a low G for five beats under "bow and bend like a wil-" and for seven under "Dropping down like a show-", and reach your lowest note, F, under the second "Who" of the first strain and under "reeling" in the third.

## Questions

### Who wrote Who Will Bow and Bend Like a Willow?

Nobody knows. Hazzard's copy gives no maker or giver, no place and no date [^hazzard]. Andrews printed its words in 1937 with no place or date, and in 1940 put its beginning at Canterbury, New Hampshire, about 1843, but named no author [^andrews1937][^andrews].

### When was Who Will Bow and Bend Like a Willow printed?

Its earliest known printings are both by Edward Deming Andrews: the words in 1937 and the tune in 1940. The 1937 printing, in his article "Shaker Songs" in The Musical Quarterly, gives all twelve lines with no tune [^andrews1937]. His book The Gift to Be Simple added the tune, as song No. 30 [^andrews].

### What does "bow and bend like a willow" mean?

It means to be humble and yielding, like a willow that bends low in the wind and springs back. In the revival's meetings the singers also did it, bowing, turning and reeling as they sang [^andrews]. Simple Gifts, copied into the same songbook, says the same: "To bow and to bend we shan't be asham'd" [^hazzardbook].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano has the tune from first note to last, the line Hazzard wrote down [^hazzard]. With two singers, the alto joins the soprano, singing the same words mostly a third below. A third singer takes the bass, singing mostly the chord roots on "oo". With four, the tenor adds the words in the middle of the chord.
