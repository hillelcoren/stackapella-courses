Shchedryk is the Ukrainian song behind Carol of the Bells: a shchedrivka, a song for the New Year that wishes a household well. In its words a swallow calls the master of the house outside to see his new lambs and promises him a year of money [^eu-carols][^america][^kyivind]. The composer Mykola Leontovych worked, by Ukrainian scholars' count, for close to twenty years on a short choral setting built on its four-note tune [^makarenko][^vn-bib]. In 1922 Oleksandr Koshyts's Ukrainian National Chorus sang that setting at Carnegie Hall in New York [^america][^slate]. In 1936 the American choral director Peter J. Wilhousky gave the music new English words, about bells, and as Carol of the Bells it joined America's Christmas carols [^musicanet][^slate][^eu-carols].

## Where it comes from

**The words.** Ukrainian folk carols are of two kinds: koliadky, for Christmas, and shchedrivky, for New Year's Eve [^eu-carols]. The evening was called Shchedryi vechir, the Generous Evening. By the old Julian calendar it falls on what is now 13 January, and many Ukrainians still keep it as the old New Year [^eu-malanka][^risu]. Since the churches' change of calendar, many now keep it on 31 December [^kyivpost-malanka][^zaxid-malanka]. Carols of both kinds wish good things on a household, and the Encyclopedia of Ukraine finds in them traces of older beliefs, from before Christianity, that the church later took in [^eu-carols]. A Ukrainian musicologist places Shchedryk's tune among the oldest layers of the country's ritual songs: one small figure repeated like a spell [^makarenko].

No one can date the words. The swallow is the clue most writers point to. Before Rus' took Christianity in 988, its year began on 1 March, by the Encyclopedia of Ukraine's account, and the swallow, back from the south, is a bird of spring [^eu-newyear][^america]. On this reading the song once greeted a spring New Year. Writers differ on the month: one says March, another April [^ukrainer][^kyivind]. Folk singers had more than one version of the words. Yurii Masiutyn (Ya. Yurmas) noted that Leontovych took his words from the version from Krasnopil, in Volhynia, printed in Kyiv in 1913, and a historian of the song says the same [^nbuv][^ukrainer][^zbirnychok]. Others say he first heard it in his wife's village, Pidlistsi, or that his version is closer to Podillia's [^vn-bib].

**The setting.** Mykola Leontovych was born on 13 December 1877 in Monastyrok, in the Podillia region, now part of Vinnytsia province [^eu-leon][^esu][^makarenko]. He trained for the priesthood but taught music and singing in village and town schools [^eu-leon][^america]. He later settled in Tulchyn, in Podillia, his home for most of the years that followed [^vn-bib][^kyivind].

Leontovych kept coming back to Shchedryk. Ukrainian scholars count five versions of his setting, the first from about 1901 to 1902 and the last from 1919 [^makarenko][^vn-bib]. One historian of the song dates the start of his work to 1910 instead [^ukrainer]. Its 1916 version was the first heard in public: Koshyts conducted it with a students' choir at a Christmas concert in the hall of Kyiv's Merchants' Assembly, on 29 December 1916 by the old calendar [^ukrainer][^zn][^vn-bib]. Many books give 25 December, but recent research gives the 29th [^zn][^vn-bib][^ukrainer]. The concerts made Leontovych famous [^esu]. An edition of the setting was printed in 1918 [^ukrainer][^zn]. In 1921, the state publisher in Kharkiv printed Shchedryk as the first of Leontovych's Try pisni (Three songs), beside Dudaryk and Prala, under his name alone [^trypisni]. We sing its tune and words.

![[1]]

**The chorus.** In early 1919, at Symon Petliura's order, the Ukrainian People's Republic formed a choir to sing abroad: a musical embassy to show Europe that Ukraine was a nation [^makarenko][^ukrhec-europe]. The Encyclopedia of Ukraine dates its founding to 1918 [^eu-koshyts]. Koshyts led it as the Ukrainian Republic Capella. With the Bolsheviks threatening Kyiv, the singers left, and in 1919 began their tour in Czechoslovakia [^ukrhec-europe][^ukrainer]. They went on to France, Belgium, the Netherlands, England, Spain and more, with Shchedryk at the heart of the programme [^ukrhec-europe][^makarenko][^america]. While they toured, the Bolsheviks took Ukraine, and the state that had sent them ceased to exist; the choir, unable to go home, was renamed the Ukrainian National Chorus [^ukrhec-europe][^makarenko][^eu-koshyts][^ukrhec-nw].

**Leontovych's death.** On 23 January 1921 Leontovych was shot dead at his parents' home in the village of Markivka by an agent of the Cheka, the Soviet secret police [^eu-leon][^esu][^america][^slate]. He was 43.

**New York.** The chorus reached New York on 26 September 1922 [^ukrhec-nw][^ukrainer]. On Thursday 5 October 1922 it sang at Carnegie Hall, and Shchedryk was heard in the United States for the first time [^ukrhec-nw][^kyivind][^america][^slate].

![[2]]

**The English.** Peter J. Wilhousky (1902 to 1978) was an American choral director, born in Passaic, New Jersey, to a family that writers call Ukrainian or Rusyn, from the Prešov region of today's Slovakia [^musicanet][^america][^ukrainer][^kyivind][^ukrhec-nw]. In 1936 Carl Fischer of New York published his Carol of the Bells, with Leontovych's music and Wilhousky's own English words [^musicanet][^slate][^america]. His words are about bells, not the swallow, and are not a translation [^america][^ukrainer]. They are still under copyright, so we name them and don't quote them [^musicanet][^slate].

**Myth and record.** Often told: the young Wilhousky sat in the audience at Carnegie Hall in 1922 and went home to write Carol of the Bells [^america][^slate]. What the record shows: the sources disagree. An archive's exhibition places him at the chorus's first New York concerts [^ukrhec-nw]; Slate reports that archivists and historians have not been able to confirm it [^slate]; and in a letter to a Ukrainian musicologist, Wilhousky said he had only recently heard Shchedryk performed when he came to arrange it [^america]. His English words were published in 1936, fourteen years after the concert [^musicanet][^slate].

## What the words mean

The words are a folk text with no known author. We sing twelve lines and then the first two again to close.

**"Shchedryk, shchedryk, shchedrivochka".** These are calling words more than a sentence. A Ukrainian dictionary gives "shchedryk", stressed on its first syllable, as another name for the Generous Evening itself [^sum-shchedryk]. So the title is said SHCHED-rik: "shch" is "sh" and "ch" run together, as in "fresh cherries", the "e" is the "e" of "bed", and the "y" is close to the "i" of "bit" [^ua-lessons][^jenkala]. Shchedrik and Schedryk are other spellings of the same word. "Shchedrivochka" is a fond, small form of "shchedrivka", a New Year song [^eu-carols].

**The swallow arrives.** A little swallow has flown in. It starts to twitter and calls the master of the house to come out.

**What she tells him.** Come out, master, and look at your "koshara", your sheepfold [^sum-koshara]. The ewes there have "pokotylys", a farm word for giving birth, used of sheep [^sum-kotytysia], and lambs have been born.

**The promise.** All your "tovar", your livestock, mostly cattle in the old sense, is good [^sum-tovar]. You will have a "mirka" of money: a mirka was a household measure for grain, and the vessel that held it [^sum-mirka]. If not money, then "polova", the chaff left from threshing grain [^sum-polova], which seems to promise grain enough to thresh. And you have a dark-browed wife.

### The full words

These are the words as a Kyiv songbook printed them in 1913, six numbered couplets from Krasnopil, in Volhynia, with its spelling and punctuation [^zbirnychok]. We sing them as Leontovych printed them in 1921 [^trypisni]. Beyond spelling, his print has "lastivochka", as the 1913 page has under its notes, where its first couplet prints the dialect "hlastivochka"; "Khoch ne hroshei" for the page's "Khoch ne hroshi"; and a different last line, "V tebe zhinka chornobrova", you have a dark-browed wife. Like his print, we close with the first two lines again, which the 1913 page leaves off. The transliteration is Ukraine's national system of 2010.

*Pryletila hlastivochka.*
a little swallow has flown in.

*Stala sobi shchebetaty*
She began to twitter away,
> Господаря викликати:
*Hospodaria vyklykaty:*
calling out the master of the house:

> „Вийди, вийди господарю,
*"Vyidy, vyidy hospodariu,*
"Come out, come out, master,
> Подивися на кошару;
*Podyvysia na kosharu;*
take a look at the sheepfold;

> „Там овечки покотились,
*"Tam ovechky pokotylys,*
the ewes there have given birth,

*A yahnychky narodylys.*
and little lambs have been born.

If not money, then chaff from the threshing:
> Твоя жінка черноброва“...
*Tvoia zhinka chernobrova"...*
your wife is dark-browed."

## How it is sung

A shchedrivka is sung to a household on New Year's Eve to wish it a good year; different carols are addressed to different members of the household [^eu-carols]. By one Ukrainian newspaper's account, shchedrivky are sung again on the eve of Epiphany [^zaxid-epiphany]; the Encyclopedia of Ukraine notes that in Podillia that evening is also called Shchedryi vechir [^eu-epiphany]. The Encyclopedia of Ukraine, on Leontovych, glosses the title as 'Epiphany Carol' [^eu-leon].

Our arrangement is new, written for Stackapella. It takes the folk tune and words as Leontovych printed them in 1921, but not his setting: our four parts are our own. It is in G minor, in 3/4, and quick. It builds part by part, from the soprano alone to all four, and ends as the opening two lines return and all four hold the final chord. It is for 2 to 4 singers: soprano and alto, then bass, then tenor.

- Koshyts wrote in his memoirs that Shchedryk was the greatest success of the chorus's repertoire. [^america][^ukrainer]

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/shchedryk/soprano): the tune is the same four quick notes in every bar, B-flat, A, B-flat, G, on just three pitches. The challenge is keeping it light for 29 bars: let the two short notes in the middle of each bar stay even, and save the long note for the last "lastivochka".
- [Alto](/songs/shchedryk/alto): joins in bar 5 with the soprano's rhythm and words, but its own shape: on "Stala sobi" it circles D, C, D, D, and on "Hospodaria" it moves G, F, G, E-flat, a third below. It ends on its lowest note, a long B-flat under the tune's G.
- [Bass](/songs/shchedryk/bass): enters at "Vyidy, vyidy" with the word "Shchedryk": "Shched-" holds a whole bar and "-ryk" half a bar, then a rest. It starts on a high G and drops to the low G at "Tam ovechky". Count the rests as carefully as the notes, and hold the last low G for two full bars.
- [Tenor](/songs/shchedryk/tenor): comes in last, at "Tam ovechky", with one "Shchedryk" a bar, mostly on B-flat. The trap is the last bar of each of the first three four-bar phrases (bars 16, 20 and 24), where "shched-ryk" turns into two short, even notes against the tune's rhythm. It finishes on a long low D.

## Questions

### What does Shchedryk mean in English?

Shchedryk has no exact English word. It comes from "shchedryi", generous, and a Ukrainian dictionary gives it as another name for Shchedryi vechir, the Generous Evening, New Year's Eve by the old calendar [^sum-shchedryk][^eu-malanka]. Its words tell of a swallow that promises the master of the house lambs, money and a good year [^america][^kyivind].

### Who wrote Shchedryk?

Nobody knows: the words and the four-note tune are a Ukrainian folk song with no known author [^makarenko][^zbirnychok]. The famous choral setting is Mykola Leontovych's, which he reworked from about 1901 to 1919 [^makarenko][^vn-bib]. We sing the folk tune and words as he printed them in 1921, in four parts of our own [^trypisni].

### Is Carol of the Bells a translation of Shchedryk?

No. Carol of the Bells uses Leontovych's music, but Peter J. Wilhousky's English words, published in 1936 and still under copyright, are new: they are about bells, while the Ukrainian words are about a swallow promising a good New Year [^america][^ukrainer][^musicanet][^slate].

### Is Shchedryk a Christmas song?

Not at first: it is a shchedrivka, a song for New Year's Eve, 31 December by the new church calendar and 13 January by the old [^eu-carols][^eu-malanka][^risu]. Its first public performance, in Kyiv in 1916, was at a Christmas concert [^ukrainer], and by one Ukrainian newspaper's account shchedrivky are also sung on the eve of Epiphany [^zaxid-epiphany]. America came to know it as a Christmas carol through the English Carol of the Bells [^eu-carols].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, the four-note figure, from the first bar to the last. It also starts alone for four bars, so two singers have the tune and the alto, and the bass and tenor add the slow "Shchedryk" underneath.
