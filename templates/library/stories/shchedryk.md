Shchedryk is a Ukrainian shchedrivka, a song for the New Year that wishes a household well. In its words a swallow flies in and calls the master of the house outside to see his new lambs, and tells him the year will bring him money [^eu-carols][^america][^kyivind]. The composer Mykola Leontovych worked, by Ukrainian scholars' count, for close to twenty years on a short choral setting built on its four-note tune [^makarenko][^vn-bib]. In 1922 Oleksandr Koshyts's Ukrainian National Chorus sang that setting at Carnegie Hall in New York [^america][^slate]. In 1936 the American choral director Peter J. Wilhousky gave the music new English words, about bells, and as Carol of the Bells it joined America's Christmas carols [^musicanet][^slate][^eu-carols].

## Where it comes from

**The words.** Ukrainian folk carols are of two kinds: koliadky, for Christmas, and shchedrivky, for New Year's Eve [^eu-carols]. The evening was called Shchedryi vechir, the Generous Evening. By the old Julian calendar it falls on what is now 13 January, and many Ukrainians still keep it as the old New Year [^eu-malanka][^risu]. Since the churches' change of calendar, many now keep it on 31 December [^kyivpost-malanka][^zaxid-malanka]. Carols of both kinds wish good things on a household, and the Encyclopedia of Ukraine finds in them traces of older beliefs, from before Christianity, that the church later took in [^eu-carols]. A Ukrainian musicologist places Shchedryk's tune among the oldest layers of the country's ritual songs: one small figure repeated over and over, like a spell [^makarenko].

No one can date the words. The swallow is the clue most writers point to. Before Rus' took Christianity in 988, its year began on 1 March, by the Encyclopedia of Ukraine's account, and the swallow, back from the south, is a bird of spring [^eu-newyear][^america]. On this reading the song once greeted a spring New Year. Writers differ on the month: one says March, another April [^ukrainer][^kyivind]. Folk singers had more than one version of the words, and which village's version Leontovych heard is disputed [^vn-bib][^ukrainer].

**The setting.** Mykola Leontovych was born on 13 December 1877 in Monastyrok, in the Podillia region, now part of Vinnytsia province [^eu-leon][^esu][^makarenko]. He trained for the priesthood, but made his life teaching music and singing in village and town schools [^eu-leon][^america]. From 1904 to 1908 he taught at a railway school in Hryshyne, in the Donbas, until a workers' choir he led got him into trouble. He then settled in Tulchyn, in Podillia, his home for most of the years that followed [^vn-bib][^kyivind][^america].

Leontovych kept coming back to Shchedryk. Ukrainian scholars count five versions of his setting, the first from about 1901 to 1902 and the last from 1919 [^makarenko][^vn-bib]. One historian of the song dates the start of his work to 1910 instead [^ukrainer]. It was the 1916 version that was first heard in public. Koshyts conducted it with a choir of Kyiv's university students and of its women's higher courses at a Christmas concert in the hall of Kyiv's Merchants' Assembly, on 29 December 1916 by the old calendar [^ukrainer][^zn][^vn-bib]. Many books give 25 December, Christmas Day by that calendar, but recent research gives the 29th [^zn][^vn-bib][^ukrainer]. The concerts made Leontovych famous [^esu]. An edition of the setting was printed in 1918 [^ukrainer][^zn].

**The chorus.** In early 1919, at Symon Petliura's order, the Ukrainian People's Republic formed a choir to sing abroad: a musical embassy to show Europe that Ukraine was a nation [^makarenko][^ukrhec-europe]. The Encyclopedia of Ukraine dates its founding to 1918 [^eu-koshyts]. Koshyts led it as the Ukrainian Republic Capella. With the Bolsheviks threatening Kyiv, the singers left, and in 1919 began their tour in Czechoslovakia [^ukrhec-europe][^ukrainer]. Over the next years they sang in France, Belgium, the Netherlands, England, Spain and more, with Shchedryk at the heart of the programme [^ukrhec-europe][^makarenko][^america]. While they toured, the Bolsheviks took Ukraine and the state that had sent them ceased to exist, so the singers could not go home; the choir was renamed the Ukrainian National Chorus [^ukrhec-europe][^makarenko][^eu-koshyts][^ukrhec-nw].

**Leontovych's death.** Leontovych did not live to see where his setting went. On 23 January 1921 he was shot dead at his parents' home in the village of Markivka by an agent of the Cheka, the Soviet secret police [^eu-leon][^esu][^america][^slate]. He was 43.

**New York.** The chorus sailed from Hamburg on the Caronia and reached New York on 26 September 1922 [^ukrhec-nw][^ukrainer]. On Thursday 5 October 1922 it sang at Carnegie Hall, and Shchedryk was heard in the United States for the first time [^ukrhec-nw][^kyivind][^america][^slate].

**The English.** Peter J. Wilhousky (1902 to 1978) was an American choral director, born in Passaic, New Jersey, to an Eastern European family [^musicanet][^america][^ukrhec-nw]. Writers differ on its roots: some call him of Ukrainian descent, while the Ukrainian History and Education Center calls the family Rusyn, from the Prešov region of today's Slovakia [^ukrainer][^kyivind][^ukrhec-nw]. He later arranged choral music for the NBC Symphony Orchestra's broadcasts [^america][^slate][^ukrhec-nw]. In 1936 Carl Fischer of New York published his Carol of the Bells, with Leontovych's music and Wilhousky's own English words [^musicanet][^slate][^america]. His words are about bells, not the swallow, and are not a translation [^america][^ukrainer]. They are still under copyright, so we name them and don't quote them [^musicanet][^slate].

**Myth and record.** Often told: the young Wilhousky sat in the audience at Carnegie Hall in 1922 and went home to write Carol of the Bells [^america][^slate]. What the record shows: the sources disagree. An archive's exhibition places him at the chorus's first New York concerts [^ukrhec-nw]; Slate reports that archivists and historians have not been able to confirm it [^slate]; and in a letter to a Ukrainian musicologist, Wilhousky said he had only recently heard Shchedryk performed when he came to arrange it [^america]. His English words were published in 1936, fourteen years after the concert [^musicanet][^slate].

## What the words mean

The words are a folk text with no known author. We sing twelve lines and then the first two again to close. Here is their meaning in our own words.

**"Shchedryk, shchedryk, shchedrivochka".** These are calling words more than a sentence. A Ukrainian dictionary gives "shchedryk", stressed on its first syllable, as another name for the Generous Evening itself [^sum-shchedryk]. "Shchedrivochka" is a fond, small form of "shchedrivka", a New Year song [^eu-carols].

**The swallow arrives.** A little swallow has flown in. It starts to twitter and calls the master of the house to come out.

**What she tells him.** Come out, master, and look at your "koshara", your sheepfold [^sum-koshara]. The ewes there have "pokotylys", a farm word for giving birth, used of sheep [^sum-kotytysia], and lambs have been born.

**The promise.** All your "tovar", your livestock, mostly cattle in the old sense, is good [^sum-tovar]. You will have a "mirka" of money: a mirka was a household measure for grain, and the vessel that held it [^sum-mirka]. If not money, then "polova", the chaff left from threshing grain [^sum-polova], which seems to promise grain enough to thresh. And you have a dark-browed wife.

**The close.** The opening lines come back: the generous evening, and the swallow that has flown in.

## How it is sung

A shchedrivka belongs to New Year's Eve by the old calendar, sung to a household to wish it a good year; different carols are addressed to different members of the household [^eu-carols]. Shchedrivky are heard again on the eve of Epiphany, and in Podillia, Leontovych's region, that evening is also called Shchedryi vechir [^eu-epiphany][^zaxid-epiphany]. The Encyclopedia of Ukraine's entry on Leontovych glosses the title as 'Epiphany Carol' [^eu-leon]. In America, through Wilhousky's English, the music became a Christmas carol [^eu-carols][^america].

Our arrangement is new, written for Stackapella. It uses the traditional tune and words and does not use Leontovych's setting. It is in G minor, in 3/4, and quick. It builds part by part. The soprano sings the first four bars alone. The alto joins at "Stala sobi shchebetaty". The bass comes in at "Vyidy, vyidy", singing only the word "Shchedryk" on long notes, and the tenor joins at "Tam ovechky", also on "Shchedryk". All four sing to the end, where the opening two lines return and all four parts hold the final chord together. It is for 2 to 4 singers: soprano and alto; then add the bass; then the tenor.

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/shchedryk/soprano): the tune is the same four quick notes in every bar, B-flat, A, B-flat, G, all the way through, on just three pitches. The challenge is keeping it light and even for 29 bars: let the two short notes in the middle of each bar stay level, and save the long note for the last "lastivochka".
- [Alto](/songs/shchedryk/alto): joins in bar 5 in the soprano's rhythm, with the same words. Its shape changes under the tune: on "Stala sobi" it circles D, C, D, D, and on "Hospodaria" it moves G, F, G, E-flat, a third below. It ends on its lowest note, a long B-flat under the tune's G.
- [Bass](/songs/shchedryk/bass): enters at "Vyidy, vyidy" with the word "Shchedryk": "Shched-" holds a whole bar and "-ryk" half a bar, then a rest. It starts on a high G and drops to the low G at "Tam ovechky". Count the rests as carefully as the notes, and hold the last low G for two full bars.
- [Tenor](/songs/shchedryk/tenor): comes in last, at "Tam ovechky", with one "Shchedryk" a bar, mostly on B-flat. The trap is the last bar of each of the first three four-bar phrases (bars 16, 20 and 24), where "shched-ryk" turns into two short, even notes against the tune's rhythm. It finishes on a long low D.

## Questions

### What does Shchedryk mean in English?

Shchedryk has no exact English word. It comes from "shchedryi", generous, and a Ukrainian dictionary gives it as another name for Shchedryi vechir, the Generous Evening, New Year's Eve by the old calendar [^sum-shchedryk][^eu-malanka]. The song is named for the evening it belongs to. Its words tell of a swallow that flies in, tells the master of the house his lambs are born, and promises him money and a good year [^america][^kyivind].

### Is Carol of the Bells a translation of Shchedryk?

No. Carol of the Bells uses Leontovych's music, but Peter J. Wilhousky's English words, published in 1936 and still under copyright, are new: they are about bells, while the Ukrainian words are about a swallow promising a good New Year [^america][^ukrainer][^musicanet][^slate].

### Is Shchedryk a Christmas song?

Not at first. It is a shchedrivka, a song for New Year's Eve. That evening is 31 December by the new church calendar and 13 January by the old [^eu-carols][^eu-malanka][^risu]. Its first public performance, in Kyiv in 1916, was at a Christmas concert, and shchedrivky are also sung on the eve of Epiphany [^ukrainer][^eu-epiphany][^zaxid-epiphany]. America came to know it as a Christmas carol through the English Carol of the Bells [^eu-carols].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, the four-note figure, from the first bar to the last. In our arrangement the soprano also starts alone for four bars, so with two singers you have the tune and the alto, and the bass and tenor add the slow "Shchedryk" underneath.
