And the Glory of the Lord is the first chorus of Messiah, George Frideric Handel's oratorio of 1741, and the first time the choir sings in it, after the tenor's "Comfort ye" and "Ev'ry valley" [^wordbook1750][^prout1902]. Its words are a single verse of the prophet Isaiah, 40:5, as the King James Bible gives it: "And the glory of the LORD shall be revealed, and all flesh shall see it together: for the mouth of the LORD hath spoken it" [^kjv]. Handel wrote it in London in the last week of August 1741 [^grove1880][^vickers2023]. It is a quick dance in three time, in A major, and each phrase of the verse has its own tune [^prout1902]. We sing the whole chorus in Handel's own four parts, in his key, for four singers.

## Where it comes from

**The words.** Messiah's words are verses from the Bible, chosen and ordered by Charles Jennens, a rich amateur of music born in 1700 into a family of Gopsall, in Leicestershire [^dnb-jennens][^dnb-handel][^vickers2023]. On 10 July 1741 Jennens wrote to his friend Edward Holdsworth that he hoped to persuade Handel to set "another Scripture Collection I have made for him": "The Subject is Messiah" [^vickers2023][^jhcorr]. He opened it with the first verses of Isaiah 40, the prophet's message of comfort to Jerusalem: "Comfort ye, comfort ye my people", then "Every valley shall be exalted", and then this chorus, the next verse [^wordbook1750][^kjv]. Part One goes on from Isaiah's prophecies to the birth of Christ and the shepherds [^vickers2023].

**The music.** Handel's score, now in the British Library, is headed in his hand "Messiah, an Oratorio. Part the First", and its first page notes the day he began, 22 August 1741 [^autograph][^grove1880]. He finished Part One, this chorus with it, on 28 August, and the whole oratorio by 14 September [^grove1880][^vickers2023].

**Dublin and London.** Handel took Messiah to Dublin, where it was first performed in the Musick Hall in Fishamble Street at noon on Tuesday 13 April 1742, for three charities: the relief of prisoners in the city's jails, Mercer's Hospital and the Charitable Infirmary [^townsend1852][^grove1880]. Faulkner's Dublin Journal reported that above 700 people heard it and that about £400 was raised, and found that "Words are wanting to express the exquisite Delight it afforded" [^townsend1852][^schoelcher1857][^vickers2023]. London first heard it at the Covent Garden theatre on 23 March 1743, as "A New Sacred Oratorio" [^grove1880][^vickers2023][^tobin]. The London wordbook, which Jennens saw through the press, carried the motto "Majora canamus", "let us sing of greater things"; the one of 1750 that we read still prints it, and gives this chorus on page 4 [^vickers2023][^wordbook1750].

**The Foundling Hospital.** From 1750 Handel gave Messiah every spring in the chapel of the Foundling Hospital, London's home for abandoned babies, for its funds [^grove1880][^dnb-handel][^tobin]. When he died, on 14 April 1759, he left the hospital "a fair copy of the score, and all the parts" [^brownlow1847][^dnb-handel].

**In print, and Mozart's version.** The first complete printed score came out in London in 1767, from Randall and Abell, the successors of Handel's publisher Walsh [^prout1902][^vickers2023]. In March 1789 Mozart rescored Messiah for Baron Gottfried van Swieten's concerts in Vienna; it was first heard at Count Johann Esterházy's on 6 March 1789 and printed in Leipzig in 1803 [^koechel1862][^holschneider][^imslp-k572]. That printed score included changes by Johann Adam Hiller, and Ebenezer Prout suspected that the harmony under the alto's first entry in this chorus was Hiller's, "quite contrary to the spirit of the music"; in his edition of 1902 he put back the plainer chords that Handel's own bass line suggests [^prout1902].

**Handel's way with the words.** Handel sets the first phrase as "the glo-ry of the Lord", with the last syllable of "glory" and "of" sung on the same note [^prout1902]. Prout explains this as the Italian habit of joining two vowels on one note, and says most editions of his day "spoil the passage" by splitting the note; he restored Handel's reading throughout [^prout1902]. Our parts sing it that way.

## What the words mean

Isaiah 40 begins a new message in the book of the prophet: God tells him to comfort his people and to speak kindly to Jerusalem, for her time of hardship is over [^kjv]. A voice cries out to prepare a road for God through the desert, valleys raised and mountains brought low [^kjv]. This verse says what will happen when that road is ready.

**"And the glory of the Lord shall be revealed."** God's glory, his presence and splendour, will be shown openly [^kjv].

**"And all flesh shall see it together."** "Flesh" here means people, mankind in general [^webster-flesh]: everyone will see it, all at once. The Gospel of Luke quotes this passage when John the Baptist begins to preach, ending "And all flesh shall see the salvation of God" [^kjv]. So Christians read it as pointing to Christ, and that is how Jennens uses it, before the prophecies of his birth [^vickers2023].

**"For the mouth of the Lord hath spoken it."** God himself has said it, so it will surely happen. "Hath" is the old form of "has".

### The full words

This is the chorus as the London wordbook of 1750 prints it, with its capitals and the long s made modern [^wordbook1750]. It is the King James text of Isaiah 40:5, word for word [^kjv]. Handel repeats each phrase many times; the page shows the words as we sing them.

And the Glory of the Lord shall be revealed,
and all Flesh shall see it together,
for the Mouth of the Lord hath spoken it.

## How it is sung

It is No. 4 in Prout's edition of Messiah, after the overture, "Comfort ye" and "Ev'ry valley", and the first chorus of Part One [^prout1902][^wordbook1750]. Handel gave Messiah in spring, but it is now just as often a Christmas work, since Part One tells of the prophecies and the birth: in Boston, the Handel and Haydn Society has sung it "every year since 1854 at Christmastide without interruption", by its own account [^hh][^vickers2023].

Handel gives each phrase of the verse its own tune [^prout1902]. "And the glory of the Lord" dips, leaps up and then climbs step by step to "Lord", first sung by the alto alone. "Shall be revealed" runs in quick notes. "And all flesh shall see it together" falls in short steps. "For the mouth of the Lord hath spoken it" is sung on one note, in long held values. The four tunes come back in different parts and keys and are sung against each other. Near the end all four parts stop together for a bar, then sing the last "hath spoken it" slowly, marked Adagio [^prout1902].

Our arrangement is read from Prout's full score of 1902, No. 4, "Allegro", crotchet = 100, in A major and three beats to the bar [^prout1902]. We sing the whole chorus in Handel's four voice parts, in his key, at his tempo, with every note and word as Prout prints them. The orchestra is left out: the alto starts the song alone, as Handel has it, the orchestra's ten bars of introduction are not played, and its two short interludes become a bar's rest each. The slow ending is written out half as slow again, because a song keeps one tempo. We have written in breaths for beginners where a line ran long. It lasts about three and three quarter minutes, for four singers only, because the tunes pass from part to part.

## Did you know?

- Charles Burney, who saw Handel in Chester on his way to Ireland in 1741, believed Messiah had been performed in London that year and badly received [^burney1785]. Horatio Townsend showed in 1852 that the London papers had no notice of any such performance, and Handel's own letter from Dublin says only that he had set it "before I left England" [^townsend1852].
- In two stretches of this chorus the Mozart score gave the choir no accompaniment but the bass instruments; Prout, writing for the big choral societies of his day, doubled the voices there on the organ or the wind [^prout1902].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/and-the-glory-of-the-lord/soprano): you sing the top line, most often on E, then B, C-sharp and A. Your one top A comes on "Lord" at the peak of "the glory of the Lord" near the end, and your low E only once, on "the" of your second "and the glory", early on. On "for the mouth of the Lord" you hold E for long notes before rising to F-sharp on "spo-ken": keep the long notes steady.
- [Alto](/songs/and-the-glory-of-the-lord/alto): you start the song alone with Handel's opening tune, and your one low A comes straight away, on the very first "the". You sing most often on A, then F-sharp, B and E, and reach your top B on many notes, among them "glory" in "and the glory", "see it together" and "for the mouth of the Lord".
- [Tenor](/songs/and-the-glory-of-the-lord/tenor): you sing the words in the middle of the chord, most often on B and E, then C-sharp. Your one top G-sharp comes on the "the" of your third "and the glory", near the end of the first part. Your low E comes twice, on the "the" of your second "And the glory" and on "the" in "together, the glory" in the last part.
- [Bass](/songs/and-the-glory-of-the-lord/bass): you sing the bass line, most often on A, then E and B. You and the tenor are the first to sing "For the mouth of the Lord", and you sing it on one A, mostly three beats to a syllable. Your top E comes once, on "Lord" in your second "the glory of the Lord", and your low G-sharp once, on the "the" before your second "glory" in "the glory, the glory of the Lord" in the last part.

## Questions

### What Bible verse is And the Glory of the Lord?

Isaiah 40:5, in the King James Version: "And the glory of the LORD shall be revealed, and all flesh shall see it together: for the mouth of the LORD hath spoken it" [^kjv]. Charles Jennens placed it in Messiah straight after verses 1 to 4 of the same chapter, which the tenor sings in "Comfort ye" and "Ev'ry valley", so the chorus completes the passage [^wordbook1750][^vickers2023].

### What key is And the Glory of the Lord in?

A major, in three beats to the bar, marked Allegro, with a crotchet at 100, in Ebenezer Prout's edition of 1902, which follows Handel's own score [^prout1902]. We sing it in that key and at that speed, in Handel's four voice parts [^prout1902].

### Which part starts And the Glory of the Lord?

The alto, alone. After the orchestra's introduction the altos sing "And the glory, the glory of the Lord" by themselves, and then the other three parts come in together with the same words [^prout1902]. In our song there is no introduction, so the alto sings the very first note.
