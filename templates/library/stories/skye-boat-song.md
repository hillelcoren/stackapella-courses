The Skye Boat Song, often called by its first line, Speed, Bonnie Boat, or by its refrain, Over the Sea to Skye, is a Scottish song about a real escape. In June 1746, two months after his army was beaten at Culloden, Prince Charles Edward Stuart, Bonnie Prince Charlie, crossed by night from Benbecula to the Isle of Skye in a small boat, dressed as Flora MacDonald's maid.[^lyon][^blaikie][^dnb-flora] The song came more than a century later. Harold Boulton wrote the words, and the air, its book says, was four bars taken down from Hebridean boatmen and four added by Anne Campbell Macleod.[^sotn] Their Songs of the North printed it in the 1880s,[^smith1888][^blackie1889] and Robert Louis Stevenson later wrote his own words to the same tune.[^students][^balladindex]

## Where it comes from

**The escape.** Charles Edward Stuart, born in Rome in 1720, landed in Scotland in 1745 to win back the British throne for his father, and his rising ended on Culloden Moor on 16 April 1746.[^dnb-charles][^blaikie] In late June, hunted through the islands, he met Flora MacDonald, a young woman of Milton in South Uist, who agreed to take him to Skye.[^blaikie][^dnb-flora] He went as "Betty Burke", an Irish spinning maid.[^blaikie][^dnb-flora] Flora's own account, taken down by Dr Burton of York and copied by Bishop Robert Forbes into his collection The Lyon in Mourning, says they set sail "at eight o'clock, June 28th, Saturday, 1746", and that a league from shore the sea grew rough, "and at last tempestuous".[^lyon] Walter Blaikie's itinerary (1897) agrees; the Dictionary of National Biography put it at ten on the evening of the 27th.[^blaikie][^dnb-flora] Fired on by soldiers off Waternish, they rowed on and landed at Kilbride in Trotternish, near Monkstadt House.[^lyon][^blaikie][^dnb-flora]

**The book.** Songs of the North was edited by A. C. Macleod and Harold Boulton, with the music arranged by Malcolm Lawson, and published in London by Field and Tuer at the Leadenhall Press.[^edwards1887][^smith1888] Its first volume held 46 songs and was dedicated, by permission, to Queen Victoria.[^edwards1887][^imslp][^sotn] We have not seen the first edition; our copy, a later Cramer printing, carries no date. The Ballad Index gives 1884 as the song's earliest date, and IMSLP puts "(1884)" on a Cramer copy of the twentieth edition.[^balladindex][^imslp] Two notices of July 1885, in the Illustrated London News and Chambers's Journal, treat it as a handsome new volume from Field and Tuer.[^iln1885][^chambers1885] By August 1887 the anthologist D. H. Edwards could note that many of its songs were printed there for the first time.[^edwards1887]

**The air and its maker.** A. C. Macleod was Anne Campbell Macleod, whom Edwards calls Annie, second daughter of Dr Norman Macleod of Glasgow.[^edwards1887][^whoswho1923] Who's Who for 1923 records her marriage in 1888 to James Wilson, later Sir James, which made her Lady Wilson, and her death in 1921.[^whoswho1923][^clanmacleod] The song's page is headed "Skye Boat Song" and, in bold, "(Jacobite)". Over the music, "Words by Harold Boulton" faces "Four bars taken down from Hebridean boatmen, four bars added by A. C. Macleod", with Malcolm Lawson's name below.[^sotn] In 1923 Boulton's later collection, Our National Songs, called it an "Air (founded upon an old 'Chanty') Composed by A. C. Macleod".[^ons1923] Neither book names the boatmen's song.

![[1]]

**The words.** Harold Boulton lived from 1859 to 1935.[^imslp][^ons1923] In 1910 the writer Harold Simpson set down Boulton's story of how the song began. A Professor Blackburn told him of Highland boatmen rowing him and Mrs Blackburn across to Roshven, chanting a refrain: "Row us along, Donald and John, Over the sea to Roshven." On this, Simpson writes, Boulton founded the Skye Boat Song, which Boulton counted among his three most successful lyrics.[^simpson1910] In 1888 Edwards printed the song, all four verses, among Boulton's poems, and Laura Alexandrine Smith quoted it with its tune in The Music of the Waters, her book of songs of the sea.[^edwards1888][^smith1888]

**Myth and record.** Often told: Macleod heard the air from oarsmen rowing her from Soay to Loch Coruisk on Skye, in 1879 by one biographer's account.[^balladindex][^standrews] What the record shows: the book names no place or year for the boatmen, and Boulton's own story is of a refrain heard on the way to Roshven. The words are Boulton's, of the 1880s; "(Jacobite)" names the subject, not the age.[^sotn][^simpson1910][^balladindex] The book's footnote put the escape in "the winter of 1745-6";[^sotn][^smith1888] it was in June 1746,[^lyon][^blaikie] and by 1923 the note read "after Culloden, in 1746".[^ons1923]

## What the words mean

The chorus urges the boat on like a bird. "Bonnie" means fine or handsome. "The lad that's born to be king" is Charles himself, whom the Jacobites held to be the rightful heir to the throne.[^dnb-charles] Skye is the large island off the west coast; the Gaelic version printed with the song calls it Tìr a' Cheò.[^sotn]

![[2]]

The first verse is the storm. The winds howl and thunder breaks overhead, while the enemy, "baffled", stands on the shore and dares not follow. The book's footnote says the pursuers, though they had boats, did not venture out;[^sotn] Flora says soldiers fired at them.[^lyon] The 1888 and 1889 printings we have read say "Thunder-clouds rend the air"; the Cramer reprint and Our National Songs of 1923 say "Thunderclaps", as sung today.[^edwards1888][^smith1888][^blackie1889][^sotn][^ons1923]

![[3]]

The second verse is a lullaby: sleep softly, for the ocean is a bed fit for a king, and Flora will keep watch by your head. The third looks back to Culloden, where "many's the lad" who could wield a claymore, a Highland broadsword, lay dead by nightfall. The fourth tells of burned homes, and of exile and death scattering the loyal men, and ends in hope: before the sword is cool in its sheath ("ere" means before), Charlie will come again.

### The full words

Harold Boulton's words as printed in the undated Cramer reprint of Songs of the North, vol. I, p. 20, which we read. The chorus is sung to begin and after each verse.

**Chorus**
Speed bonnie boat like a bird on the wing,
"Onward" the sailors cry;
Carry the lad that's born to be king
Over the sea to Skye.

**Verse 1**
Loud the winds howl, loud the waves roar,
Thunderclaps rend the air;
Baffled our foes stand by the shore,
Follow they will not dare.

**Verse 2**
Though the waves leap, soft shall ye sleep,
Ocean's a royal bed.
Rocked in the deep Flora will keep
Watch by your weary head.

**Verse 3**
Many's the lad fought on that day
Well the claymore could wield
When the night came silently lay
Dead on Culloden's field

**Verse 4**
Burned are our homes, exile and death
Scatter the loyal men;
Yet, ere the sword cool in the sheath,
Charlie will come again.

## How it is sung

It was made for one voice with piano, in 6/8: Songs of the North asks for it "With animation and well accented", Our National Songs "With a strong swing".[^sotn][^ons1923] Smith's printing of 1888 marks the chorus "Chorus" and the verse "Solo".[^sotn][^smith1888]

Our arrangement takes the melody, words and harmony from Songs of the North, in G, for 2 to 4 singers: chorus, verse 1, chorus, verse 2, chorus. The soprano sings the tune. The other voices keep Lawson's piano chords bar by bar, his chorus drone becoming a sung bass line. The chorus sits in G major, and each verse turns to E minor before the chorus brings it home. Breaths are written in for beginners, with a short rest after "cry", "Skye", "air", "dare", "bed" and "head".

## Versions and other words

**Robert Louis Stevenson's words.** Stevenson (1850 to 1894) wrote a different song to the same air, beginning "Sing me a song of a lad that is gone".[^dnb-rls][^rpo-rls][^songsoftravel] It keeps Boulton's refrain, "Over the sea to Skye", but turns it inward: the singer remembers a lad who sailed merrily over the sea to Skye, and asks, "Say, could that lad be I?" It was printed among his Songs of Travel, verses he sent home for publication some months before he died.[^songsoftravel][^rpo] The Scottish Students' Song Book printed it as "Over the Sea to Skye", noting that Boulton, as owner of the air's copyright, allowed it to be used for the words Stevenson "was afterwards inspired to write".[^students] Stevenson's words are in the public domain everywhere. Boulton died in 1935, so his are in the public domain in Britain, the United States, Spain and most other countries, but not yet in a few with longer terms, such as Mexico, where they are free from 2036. Where they are not yet free, this site withholds the song.

## Did you know?

- Flora MacDonald's own account turns the song's second verse around: on the crossing she slept, and the Prince sang songs to entertain the company and kept watch over her, so that no one would tread on her in the dark. [^lyon][^balladindex]
- On 19 September 2022, the day of Queen Elizabeth II's funeral, the massed pipes and drums of the Scottish and Irish regiments played it as her coffin neared Windsor Castle, after the service in Westminster Abbey. The reports give no reason for the choice. [^classicfm2022][^sdx2022]

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/skye-boat-song/soprano): you carry the tune, from the D above middle C to the D an octave higher. The chorus has a lilting dotted rhythm ("Speed bon-nie") and climbs to your top D, held, on "wing" and "king", then drops a fifth, B to E, into "sai-lors" and "sea to". The verse moves mostly in even notes, each half line a step lower than the last: "howl" on B, "roar" on A, "rend the" on G, then down a third to E on "air".
- [Alto](/songs/skye-boat-song/alto): you move in long notes under the tune, mostly one note for each bar or half bar. In the chorus you start on B and hold it through "boat like a", then sit on F-sharp for "bird on the wing" while the tune climbs above you. In the verse you go from your top G on "winds howl" and "waves leap" down to your low G on "air", "dare", "bed" and "head".
- [Tenor](/songs/skye-boat-song/tenor): you stay mostly on G, the home note. In the chorus you lift to C on "bird on the" and "born to be" and settle on A for "wing" and "king". In the verse you dip to your low E on "Thunderclaps", "stand by the shore" and "Flora will keep"; solo it in the player to tune those drops.
- [Bass](/songs/skye-boat-song/bass): you sing Lawson's drone as a moving line: low G, up to D on "bird on the", then C on "wing", B on "onward the" and C again before home to G on "cry". The verse starts high on E for "Loud the winds howl", and each half ends with a climb, B, then D-sharp on "rend the", then E on "air". That D-sharp, the song's only note outside the key, leans up into E; tune it high.

## Questions

### Who wrote the Skye Boat Song lyrics?

Harold Boulton wrote the familiar words, "Speed bonnie boat", for Songs of the North in the 1880s. Robert Louis Stevenson later wrote different words to the same air, "Sing me a song of a lad that is gone".[^sotn][^students][^songsoftravel]

### What are the original lyrics of the Skye Boat Song?

The original words are Boulton's: a chorus and four verses, on the storm, Flora's watch over the sleeping prince, the dead of Culloden and the burned homes. The printings of 1888 and 1889 have "Thunder-clouds rend the air" where "Thunderclaps" is sung today. All the words are given above.[^edwards1888][^smith1888][^blackie1889][^sotn]

### Are there Gaelic lyrics to the Skye Boat Song?

Yes, but they translate Boulton's English; they are not the boatmen's song. Smith noted in 1888 that Songs of the North also gave a Gaelic version. The later reprint we read, and Our National Songs of 1923, print "Iorram Sgitheanach", a Skye iorram or boat song, as Neil Shaw's "only authorised translation", and the reprint advertises a volume whose "Gaelic Text" Shaw edited. His dates are unknown, so it is not given here. No early source prints or names the boatmen's own song.[^sotn][^smith1888][^ons1923][^standrews]

### What is the Outlander version of the Skye Boat Song?

It is Robert Louis Stevenson's words, not Boulton's, sung to the same air as the theme of the Outlander television series, which began in 2014. The series' composer turned to Stevenson's "Sing me a song of a lad that is gone" and changed "lad" to "lass" for the story's heroine, which is why it differs from the "Speed, bonnie boat" most people know.[^mccreary2014][^variety2025][^songsoftravel]

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, as Songs of the North prints it. Two singers take the tune and the alto; three add the bass, Lawson's drone as a line; the tenor makes four.[^sotn]
