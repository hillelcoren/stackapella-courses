There Is a Balm in Gilead, often called Balm in Gilead, is an African American spiritual of comfort [^canterbury][^dett1927]. In the Bible the prophet Jeremiah asks, "Is there no balm in Gilead; is there no physician there?" The spiritual's refrain answers that there is: a balm "To make the wounded whole" and "To heal the sin-sick soul" [^kjv][^canterbury][^dett1927]. Between refrains, one voice sings a verse about feeling discouraged and being lifted up again [^work1907][^dett1927]. No maker's name is known [^canterbury][^hymnary-text]. Its earliest known printing is in a songbook of 1907 from Nashville, made by two brothers from Fisk University, and hymnals of many churches print it today [^work1907][^hymnarchive][^hymnary-text].

## Where it comes from

**Newton's rhymes, 1779.** The refrain's rhymes are older than the spiritual. In Olney Hymns (1779), John Newton's "The good Physician", No. 62 in Book I, says that "There is but one Physician / Can cure a sin-sick soul" [^olney1779][^cdh-newton]. Later in Book I, No. 113, a hymn on the healing pool of Bethesda, has streams that flow "To heal a sin-sick soul" and "make the wounded whole" [^olney1779]. A hymnologist's survey names these two hymns as the roots of the spiritual's words [^hymnarchive].

**A chorus in the revival books.** By 1859 the lines had become a chorus. Hiram Mattison's Sacred Melodies for Social Worship (New York, 1859) prints Newton's "The good Physician" as No. 82, headed "Balm in Gilead", with a chorus: "There's a balm in Gilead To make the wounded whole; There's power enough in Jesus, To cure a sin-sick soul" [^mattison1859][^hymnarchive]. Joseph Hillman's The Revivalist (Troy, New York, 1868) prints the same hymn and chorus as No. 15 [^hillman1868][^hymnarchive]. The survey finds Hillman's tune much closer to the spiritual's [^hymnarchive].

**Peter and Paul.** In 1869 J. E. Gould's Songs of Gladness for the Sabbath School (Philadelphia) printed "Your Mission", a hymn signed "Rev. D. March" [^gould1869][^hymnarchive]. Its third verse begins, "If you cannot speak like angels, / If you cannot preach like Paul, / You can tell the love of Jesus, / You can say he died for all" [^gould1869]. The spiritual's third verse is very close to it [^gould1869][^work1907][^hymnarchive].

**From hymnbook to spiritual.** How these lines came together in Black churches is not recorded. The hymnologist's survey suggests they passed by ear among African American singers in the late 1800s, and the Canterbury Dictionary dates the spiritual to the 19th century [^hymnarchive][^canterbury]. No source we have read shows it sung in slavery, or names who first sang it.

**The Work brothers' book, 1907.** Its earliest known printing in the form sung today is in Folk Songs of the American Negro, Number One, edited by Frederick J. Work and published in Nashville by Work Bros. & Hart Co., with an introduction by his brother John W. Work dated at Fisk University, 15 May 1907 [^work1907][^cdh-fjwork][^folklorists]. The book's acknowledgments thank John, for years the leader of Fisk's Jubilee Singers, for collecting the songs [^work1907][^folklorists]. On page 31 is "Balm in Gilead", in E major and 2/4 time, set in four parts on two staves: the refrain with its repeat, then three verses for one voice while the lower staff rests, and back to the refrain [^work1907][^work1915].

**On record, 1909.** On 9 December 1909 a male quartet from Fisk, John Wesley Work among them, recorded it for Victor, released as Victor 16487 [^jukebox1909][^victor1912][^hymnarchive]. In his own book of 1915, Folk Song of the American Negro, John W. Work counts it among the songs "based upon direct scriptural passages", next to Jeremiah 8:22, and prints the 1907 setting again [^work1915].

**Dett's page, 1927.** R. Nathaniel Dett (1882 to 1943) was born in Drummondville, Ontario, now in Niagara Falls, and taught at Hampton Institute in Virginia from 1913, where he directed its choirs [^lva-dett][^ce-dett]. His Religious Folk-Songs of the Negro as Sung at Hampton Institute (1927) prints "There Is a Balm in Gilead" on page 88, among the "Hymns of Consolation", headed "Transcribed from the singing of the Hampton Institute students by R. Nathaniel Dett" [^dett1927]. It is in B major, marked "Andante molto espress" (at a walking pace, very expressively), with the verse marked "Solo" and verses 2 and 3 printed below [^dett1927]. Dett's introduction says the tunes and harmonies are as sung at Hampton, where the songs had been sung since 1868; where they differ from other books, "every locality is a law unto itself" [^dett1927].

## What the words mean

**The question.** Jeremiah grieves for his people: "For the hurt of the daughter of my people am I hurt". Then he asks, "Is there no balm in Gilead; is there no physician there? why then is not the health of the daughter of my people recovered?" (Jeremiah 8:21 to 22) [^kjv]. The refrain turns his question into an answer: yes, there is [^canterbury].

**The balm and Gilead.** Balm was a precious gum or resin, prized as medicine and traded by merchants [^easton][^hastings]. Gilead was the hill country east of the Jordan; in Genesis, traders carry balm from Gilead to Egypt [^kjv][^easton][^hastings]. Which plant gave the Bible's balm is not known for certain [^hastings].

**The refrain.** "To make the wounded whole" means to heal the hurt. A "sin-sick soul" is a soul made ill by sin. In Newton's hymn the healer is Jesus, the one "Physician" [^olney1779][^mattison1859].

**Verse 1.** The singer's work seems to come to nothing, "But then the Holy Spirit / Revives my soul again": hope comes back [^dett1927].

**Verse 2.** "Don't ever be discouraged, for Jesus is your friend." "If you lack for knowledge, He'll ne'er refuse to lend": if you do not know enough, he will never ("ne'er") hold back his help. Hymnary.org's index links the song to James 1:5, "If any of you lack wisdom, let him ask of God" [^dett1927][^hymnary-text][^kjv].

**Verse 3.** In the Book of Acts, Peter stands up and preaches to the crowd in Jerusalem [^kjv]. The verse says you need not be a great preacher: you can still tell of Jesus' love and say that he "died for all", the phrase Paul uses in his second letter to the Corinthians [^dett1927][^kjv][^hymnary-text].

### The full words

These are the refrain and three verses as Dett's 1927 page prints them, with its capitals [^dett1927]. The 1907 page has "Don't ever feel discouraged" in verse 2 and "And say" in verse 3's last line [^work1907]. Dett's page opens the quotation in that last line and never closes it; we close it.

**Refrain**
There is a Balm in Gilead,
To make the wounded whole,
There is a Balm in Gilead,
To heal the sin-sick soul.

**Verse 1**
Sometimes I feel discouraged,
And think my work's in vain,
But then the Holy Spirit
Revives my soul again.

**Verse 2**
Don't ever be discouraged
For Jesus is your friend,
And if you lack for knowledge,
He'll ne'er refuse to lend.

**Verse 3**
If you cannot preach like Peter,
If you cannot pray like Paul,
You can tell the love of Jesus,
You can say "He died for all."

## How it is sung

It is a church song of comfort and healing, for no fixed season. Dett filed it among his "Hymns of Consolation", and The United Methodist Hymnal lists it under topics including healing and assurance [^dett1927][^hymnary-umh]. Methodist, Episcopal, Lutheran, Presbyterian, Baptist and African American hymnals print it, its tune called BALM IN GILEAD, its meter irregular [^hymnary-text][^hymnary-umh].

A folk spiritual is sung by ear and changes from place to place; a concert spiritual is written out and arranged for trained singers. The 1907 and 1927 pages stand between the two: written in four parts for quartets and choirs, but taken from the way students sang. Dett hesitated to trust newer arrangements, even by Black students of his own day, and hoped his book would keep the melodies "in their natural and untarnished settings" [^dett1927].

Our arrangement, for 2 to 4 singers, keeps Dett's own four parts a whole tone lower, in A, at his 60 beats a minute. Everyone sings the refrain, twice through each time. Between refrains the soprano sings verse 1 alone, then verse 3; verse 2 is left out for length. Dett's held notes on "whole,", the first "soul." and the verse's "gain." are written out, a few breaths are added for beginners, and the bass sings Dett's one very low note, on "sick", an octave higher.

## Did you know?

- Mattison's page of 1859 tells singers to sing the chorus twice after each verse; Dett's page of 1927 also sends the refrain round twice, with a repeat sign [^mattison1859][^dett1927].
- John Wesley Work, who sang on the Fisk quartet's 1909 recording, was the brother of Frederick J. Work, whose book had printed the song two years before [^jukebox1909][^victor1912][^work1907][^cdh-fjwork].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/balm-in-gilead/soprano): you carry the tune, mostly on A and C-sharp, and you sing both verses alone. Your top E comes only there: on "discouraged,", "work's" and "Spirit" in the first verse, and on "Peter,", "pray" and "Jesus," in the second. In the refrain you drop to your low E on every "To" in "To heal the sin-sick soul".
- [Alto](/songs/balm-in-gilead/alto): you rest through both verses and sing Dett's alto in the refrain, mostly on C-sharp and E. Your top note, F-sharp, comes on every "wounded" and "whole," and on the first "soul." of each refrain.
- [Tenor](/songs/balm-in-gilead/tenor): you rest through both verses and sing the refrain on just three notes, mostly on A. You drop to E on every "in Gilead,", on "Balm" before each "To make the wounded whole" and on the last "soul." of each refrain, and to G-sharp on every "whole,", the "There is a" after it and "sick" in every "sin-sick".
- [Bass](/songs/balm-in-gilead/bass): you sing the roots of Dett's chords on just two notes, a low A about three quarters of the time and the E above it on every "whole,", the "There is a" after it and every "sin-sick". You rest through both verses.

## Questions

### What does "There is a balm in Gilead" mean?

It means there is healing for the hurt soul. It answers Jeremiah 8:22, where the grieving prophet asks, "Is there no balm in Gilead; is there no physician there?" [^kjv]. The spiritual sings back that there is a balm "To make the wounded whole" and "To heal the sin-sick soul" [^canterbury][^dett1927].

### What was the balm of Gilead?

A precious gum or resin, valued as medicine and carried by traders through Gilead, the hill country east of the Jordan River [^easton][^hastings]. Genesis tells of merchants coming "from Gilead with their camels bearing spicery and balm and myrrh" to sell in Egypt [^kjv]. Which tree gave it is not known for certain: one Bible dictionary of 1898 says the Hebrew word's meaning "must remain uncertain" [^hastings].

### Who wrote There Is a Balm in Gilead?

No one knows. It is a traditional African American spiritual, first known in print in Nashville in 1907 [^canterbury][^work1907][^hymnarchive]. Parts of its words are older: John Newton's hymns of 1779 rhyme "whole" with "sin-sick soul", revival books printed in New York in 1859 and 1868 sing "There's a balm in Gilead" as a chorus, and a hymn by Daniel March, printed in 1869, has "If you cannot preach like Paul" [^olney1779][^mattison1859][^hillman1868][^gould1869]. A hymnologist's survey traces its words to them [^hymnarchive].

### What does "balm in Gilead" mean in The Raven?

In Edgar Allan Poe's "The Raven", printed in February 1845 and signed "Quarles", the grieving narrator begs the bird to tell him whether there is "balm in Gilead": whether there is any cure for his sorrow. He borrows Jeremiah's question, and the raven answers, "Nevermore" [^eapoe][^poe1845][^americanreview1845][^kjv].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano. In our arrangement she carries the tune in every refrain over Dett's own harmony, as he printed it in 1927. She also sings both verses alone, "Sometimes I feel discouraged" and "If you cannot preach like Peter", while the others rest [^dett1927].
