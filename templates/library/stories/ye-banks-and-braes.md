Ye Banks and Braes o' Bonnie Doon, The Banks o' Doon in its earliest known printing, is a Scots song by Robert Burns about a young woman whose lover has left her. She walks by the river Doon in Ayrshire, near where Burns was born, and asks how its banks can bloom and its birds sing when she is so full of care [^smm1792][^rpo-flowery][^spl]. That printing is in the fourth volume of The Scots Musical Museum, published in Edinburgh in 1792, marked "Slow & tender" [^smm1792][^dick]. It was Burns's third try at the song [^dick][^chambers-wallace]. Burns told the odd story of its tune: an Edinburgh lawyer, told to keep to the black keys of a harpsichord, came up with the start of a "Scots air" [^stenhouse][^glen][^graham].

## Where it comes from

**Three versions.** Burns wrote the song three times. On 11 March 1791 he sent a first version, beginning "Sweet are the banks, the banks o' Doon", to his friend Alexander Cunningham, saying he had "this evening sketched out a song" [^dick][^chambers-wallace]. It was meant for a different tune, a strathspey [^dick]. Burns sent a second version, "Ye flowery banks o' bonie Doon", in an undated letter to John Ballantine in Ayr, Dick says; the editor R. H. Cromek printed it in his Reliques of Robert Burns in 1808 [^dick][^rpo-flowery]. G. F. Graham, following Allan Cunningham, says Cromek found it among Burns's papers [^graham]. The third, in the Museum, is the one still sung [^dick][^chambers-wallace]. Its second and fourth lines are longer than in the other two; James C. Dick, the Burns song scholar, wrote in 1903 that no one knew why Burns changed the measure [^dick].

**The page of 1792.** James Johnson's Scots Musical Museum gathered Scottish songs with their tunes, and Burns gathered and wrote songs for it [^dick][^spl]. In the fourth volume, whose preface is dated 13 August 1792, the song is No. 374 on page 387, headed "The Banks o' Doon" [^smm1792]. It is engraved on two staves, the tune with the first verse under it and an unsigned bass, in G and in 6/8. The second verse is printed as a poem under the music, with the letter "B" at the foot of the page [^smm1792]. The index explains: songs marked "B. R. X. &c." are new, by "Scots gentlemen" [^smm1792]. The index entry itself reads "Ye Banks and braes o' bonie Don", with "Don" for Doon, "Burns, the Music by Mr James Millar Writer in Edinr." [^smm1792]. A writer, in Scots, is a lawyer [^dsl-writer]. In Blackwood's reissue of 1839 the same page carries a new line under the title: "Written for this Work by Robert Burns." [^smm1839][^smm1792]

![[2]]

**The tune.** It first appeared in print as "The Caledonian Hunt's Delight", in Niel Gow's Second Collection of Strathspey Reels [^dick][^glen]. Dick and the collector John Glen date that book 1788; one library catalogue gives about 1789 [^dick][^glen][^newhailes]. Gow dedicated the book to "the Noblemen & Gentlemen of the Caledonian Hunt", to whom Burns had dedicated his Edinburgh edition of 1787 [^imslp-gow][^newhailes][^burns1787][^dumfries-museum]. In November 1794 Burns recommended the air to George Thomson, the Edinburgh editor of another song collection. "Do you know the history of the air? It is curious enough," he wrote. Years before, James Miller, "writer in your good town", told the organist Stephen Clarke he longed to compose a Scots air. Clarke, "partly by way of joke", told him to keep to the black keys of the harpsichord and keep some kind of rhythm. In a few days Miller had "the rudiments of an air", which Clarke, "with some touches and corrections, fashioned into the tune". Burns had the story, he said, from Clarke himself [^stenhouse][^glen][^graham][^dick]. William Stenhouse, who wrote notes on the Museum's songs, said he knew all the parties [^stenhouse].

![[3]]

**The manuscript.** Stenhouse had Burns's manuscript of the song in front of him: a slip of Excise paper with red ink lines on the back [^stenhouse]. Burns then worked for the Excise, the tax service [^spl][^dick].

**Myth and record.** Often told: the tune is really Irish, or Manx. What the record shows: Burns passed on both claims in the same letter. An Irish gentleman said he had heard it in Ireland among the old women, and a countess said a baronet's lady took it down from a wandering piper on the Isle of Man. "How difficult then to ascertain the truth," he wrote [^stenhouse][^dick]. The Glasgow publisher James Aird printed it as "Irish" in the fourth volume of his collection of airs [^dick][^glen]. Later the English historian William Chappell argued it was an English song, "Lost, lost, lost is my quiet", with a note or two changed [^chappell]. Glen and Dick answered that Gow had printed the tune in 1788, and that the English collection Chappell cited was later [^glen][^dick].

![[4]]

Often told, too: Burns wrote the song for Peggy Kennedy, a young woman who, in Allan Cunningham's words, "fell a victim to her love". Cunningham called this "an Ayrshire legend" [^chambers-wallace], and Robert Chambers, Dick says, dated the song to 1787 on that story [^dick]. What the record shows: Burns's own letter of 11 March 1791 says he sketched the first version that evening [^dick][^chambers-wallace]. Dick held that this "disposes of" Chambers's date. The 1896 revision of Chambers's own book allowed that Burns may still have had her story in mind [^dick][^chambers-wallace].

## What the words mean

The singer is a woman whose lover was false [^chambers-wallace].

1. "Banks and braes": the river's banks and steep slopes; "bonie" is pretty [^dick-gloss]. How can the banks of the Doon bloom "sae", so, fresh and fair, and how can the little birds sing, while she is so weary and "fu' o' care", full of care? [^chambers-wallace] The warbling bird that "wantons", flits and plays freely, through the flowering thorn bush will break her heart [^webster1828], because it "minds" her, brings to mind, joys that are gone for good [^dick-gloss].
2. "Oft hae I rov'd": often have I wandered by the Doon, to see the rose and the woodbine twine together. "Ilka", every, bird sang of its love, and so did she of hers [^chambers-wallace]. With a "lightsome", cheerful, heart she "pu'd", picked, a rose from its thorny bush [^chambers-wallace]. But her "fause luver", false lover, "staw", stole, the rose, and left her the thorn [^chambers-wallace][^dick-gloss].

Burns's first version says it plainly: the bird reminds her "o' the happy days When my fause Luve was true" [^chambers-wallace].

### The full words

These are Burns's two verses as James Johnson printed them in The Scots Musical Museum, vol. 4 (1792), No. 374, page 387: the first under the music, the second below it. The long s is printed here as s; the spelling is otherwise as printed, with "bonie" and "luve".

**Verse 1**
Ye Banks and braes o' bonie Doon,
How can ye bloom sae fresh and fair;
How can ye chant, ye little birds,
And I sae weary fu' o' care!
Thou'll break my heart thou warbling bird,
That wantons thro' the flowering thorn:
Thou minds me o' departed joys,
Departed never to return.

**Verse 2**
Oft hae I rov'd by bonie Doon,
To see the rose and woodbine twine;
And ilka bird sang o' its luve,
And fondly sae did I o' mine.
Wi' lightsome heart I pu'd a rose,
Fu' sweet upon its thorny tree;
And my fause luver staw my rose,
But, ah! he left the thorn wi' me.

## How it is sung

It is a slow Scottish song for one voice and keyboard. Johnson marked it "Slow & tender" [^smm1792]; the second volume of G. F. Graham's Songs of Scotland (Edinburgh, 1848; some copies dated 1849) marks it "Andante cantabile", with a piano part by J. T. Surenne, a pause on "thorn", and small changes to the words, among them "Ye'll break my heart, ye warbling birds", "Ye mind me", "love" and "But my fause lover stole my rose" [^graham]. Johnson's tune is full of Scotch snaps, a short note on the beat and a longer one after it; Graham's has fewer [^smm1792][^graham].

Our arrangement sings Burns's words as Johnson printed them in 1792, both verses, keeping "bonie", "luve" and "fause luver staw". The tune is Johnson's notes in Graham's smoother rhythm, keeping the snaps on "bloom", "weary" and "never" and leaving out Graham's grace note, his pause on "thorn" and his changed words. It is in G and in 6/8, as both books print it, for 2 to 4 singers. The soprano sings the tune; the alto, tenor and bass are new parts, one note a syllable, and breaths are written in before each line.

## Versions and other words

- **Burns's first two versions.** Cromek, it was said, preferred the version he printed, and Dick judged it the better poem, though he expected the Museum's to stay the one people sing [^graham][^dick].
- **The added verses.** Two stanzas beginning "O blaw ye flow'rs your bonnie bloom" were printed with it in a Glasgow Pocket Encyclopedia. They are not Burns's: John Hamilton, an Edinburgh music-seller, told Stenhouse he wrote them [^stenhouse][^dick].
- **"Lost, lost, lost is my quiet".** An English song printed by Dale to a tune close to this one, which Chappell thought older and Glen and Dick thought later [^chappell][^glen][^dick].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/ye-banks-and-braes/soprano): you sing Burns's tune, from D to the E an octave and a step above. The first line's tune comes back, after a different pickup, for the third and seventh lines, and the second line's for the fourth and the last, ending on G instead of A, so two lines teach you six of the eight. The high point, "Thou'll break my heart", jumps from B to D, then to your top E on "my". Keep the quick snaps on "bloom", "weary" and "never" light: a short G, then a longer E.
- [Alto](/songs/ye-banks-and-braes/alto): you sing close under the tune, from B up to G, one note a syllable. Listen for the half step from C down to B on "bloom sae", "wea-ry" and "ne-ver", and on "re-turn" at the end. In "Thou'll break my heart, thou warbling bird" you stay between E and G while the tune climbs; solo it in the player.
- [Tenor](/songs/ye-banks-and-braes/tenor): you fill in the chord, mostly on two notes. After "Ye banks and" on G, you sing "braes o' bonie Doon, How can ye" all on D, and "I sae weary fu' o'" all on G. "Thou'll break my heart" climbs G, A, C, D, and each verse ends low, stepping down from E to D on "return" and "wi' me".
- [Bass](/songs/ye-banks-and-braes/bass): you sing the bass line, from low G up an octave. You start on B for "Ye banks and", then lift to G on "bonie". "Thou'll break my heart" walks down E, D, C to the low G, and "That wantons thro' the flow-" sits low on A and G. Every verse ends on low G.

## Questions

### Who wrote Ye Banks and Braes o' Bonnie Doon?

Robert Burns wrote the words; the Museum of 1792 prints them as his, signed "B" [^smm1792][^dick]. The same book's index credits "the Music by Mr James Millar Writer in Edinr.", an Edinburgh lawyer [^smm1792][^dsl-writer]. In 1794 Burns wrote that Miller made "the rudiments of an air" on the black keys of a harpsichord, and the organist Stephen Clarke fashioned it into the tune [^stenhouse][^glen].

### What does Ye Banks and Braes mean in English?

It is a lament by a woman whose lover has deserted her. By the river Doon she asks how the banks ("braes" are slopes) can bloom and the birds sing while she is weary and full of care [^dick-gloss][^smm1792]. Once she picked a rose there with a light heart, but her false lover stole the rose and left her the thorn [^chambers-wallace].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, Johnson's notes of 1792 in the rhythm of G. F. Graham's book of 1848 (some copies dated 1849) [^smm1792][^graham]. With two singers, the soprano sings the tune and the alto sings close beneath it; the bass makes three, and the tenor, filling in the chord, makes four.
