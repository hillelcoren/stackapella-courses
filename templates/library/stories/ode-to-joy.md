Ode to Joy is the tune of the last movement of Beethoven's Ninth Symphony, first heard in Vienna on 7 May 1824. There soloists and a chorus sing Friedrich Schiller's German poem An die Freude, "To Joy", from 1785 [^thayer3][^elbphil][^score1826][^gedichte1803]. In English-speaking churches the same tune carries Joyful, Joyful, We Adore Thee, which the American minister and poet Henry van Dyke wrote for it by 1907 and called his Hymn of Joy [^h1911][^poems1911][^hymnarchive]. Since 1972 the tune, with no words, has also been the European anthem [^coe][^eu]. We sing the first verse of van Dyke's hymn for two to four singers.

## Where it comes from

**Schiller's poem.** Friedrich Schiller wrote An die Freude in 1785 as an occasional poem for friends [^elbphil][^gedichte1803]. He printed it in the second issue of his magazine Thalia, published by Göschen in Leipzig in 1786: nine stanzas, each followed by a chorus [^thalia]. Two first-stanza lines were sharper then: joy binds what "der Mode Schwerd", fashion's sword, had divided, and "Bettler werden Fürstenbrüder", beggars become princes' brothers [^thalia]. When he collected his poems in 1803 he left out the ninth stanza and made those lines "Was die Mode streng getheilt", what custom has sternly divided, and "Alle Menschen werden Brüder", all people become brothers [^gedichte1803][^thalia]. In October 1800 he had told his friend Körner it was "ein schlechtes Gedicht", a bad poem, which had become a kind of people's poem because it suited a faulty taste of its time [^schkoerner].

**Beethoven's long plan.** In 1793 Fischenich, a friend in Bonn, wrote to Schiller's family that a young musician of the town, sent by the Elector to study with Haydn, meant to compose Schiller's "Freude", stanza by stanza [^thayer1][^elbphil]. Some thirty years later it became the finale of a symphony finished early in 1824 [^elbphil][^hymnarchive] and first played on 7 May at the court theatre by the Kärntnertor in Vienna [^thayer3][^elbphil][^hymnarchive]. In the finale the bass soloist first sings Beethoven's own words, "O Freunde, nicht diese Töne!", "O friends, not these sounds!"; then come Schiller's first three stanzas and three of his choruses, in a new order, in the 1803 text [^wsbeethoven][^elbphil][^gedichte1803]. Schott of Mainz published the score in 1826 as a symphony "with closing chorus on Schiller's ode An die Freude" [^score1826][^hymnarchive].

**The tune becomes a hymn tune.** In 1846 Elam Ives printed the melody in New York in his Mozart Collection of Sacred Music as BONN, "From Beethoven's Choral Symphony, (No. 9.)", in four parts [^ives][^hymnarchive].

**Van Dyke's words.** Henry van Dyke was a Presbyterian minister, a Princeton professor of English literature and later the United States minister to the Netherlands [^tvd][^hymnarchive][^statehist]. A manuscript of the hymn survives in the papers of his friend Louis F. Benson, the hymnologist who edited the revised edition of the Presbyterian Church's The Hymnal, published in 1911; it carries a pencilled date in 1907 [^hymnarchive][^tvd][^h1911]. In a letter to Benson, van Dyke wrote that the hymn "was written with the music steadily in mind" [^hymnarchive].

The words reached print twice in 1911. Scribner's The Poems of Henry van Dyke calls them "Hymn of Joy", "To the Music of Beethoven's Ninth Symphony", four eight-line stanzas dated 1908 [^poems1911][^hymnarchive]. The Presbyterian Church's The Hymnal sets them as No. 115 to "The Hymn to Joy", "Arr. from Ludwig van Beethoven, 1824", signed "Rev. Henry van Dyke, 1907"; the scanned copy is Benson's own [^h1911][^hymnarchive]. So the two books disagree by a year. The Hymnal for American Youth of 1919, which took the words "From Poems of Henry van Dyke" by Scribner's permission, follows the book's 1908; the manuscript's pencilled date favours 1907 [^poems1911][^h1911][^hay1919][^hymnarchive]. The hymnal's text, not the book of poems', became the one most hymnals print [^hymnarchive].

**Myth and record.** Often told: van Dyke wrote it as a guest preacher at Williams College, Massachusetts, and handed it to the president, Harry A. Garfield, at breakfast. What the record shows: the story comes from van Dyke's son, years later, and Garfield was installed as president on 7 October 1908, after the manuscript's date, so it is doubtful [^hymnarchive][^garfield]. Often told too: at the first performance the deaf Beethoven did not hear the applause until the contralto Unger turned him to face it. What the record shows: the story comes in several forms, as both Grove and Thayer say. Unger herself told George Grove in London in 1869 that it was at the end of the symphony, with Beethoven still beating time, his back to the hall; the pianist Thalberg, who was there, told Thayer that it was after the Scherzo, the second movement, when Unger pulled Beethoven by the sleeve and pointed to the audience [^grove][^thayer3].

## What the words mean

Our words are not a translation of Schiller: van Dyke wrote a new Christian hymn of praise for Beethoven's tune [^poems1911][^hymnarchive]. It has four stanzas; we sing the first. Our fourth line is his book's "Praising Thee their sun above"; the 1911 hymnal has "Opening to the sun above", and the 1919 Hymnal for American Youth "Hail thee as the sun above" [^poems1911][^h1911][^hay1919].

**Verse 1.** We worship you with joy, God of glory and love. Hearts open to God as flowers open to the sun. Melt the clouds of wrongdoing and sorrow, chase off doubt, and fill us with daylight, giver of a gladness that never dies.

**Verse 2.** Everything God made surrounds God with joy. Earth and heaven reflect God's light, and stars and angels sing around God, the centre of praise that never stops. Field, forest, valley, mountain, meadow, sea, birdsong and running water all call us to rejoice, as all creation praises in Psalm 148 [^hymnarchive].

**Verse 3.** God gives and forgives, blesses and is blessed: a well-spring, or source, of the joy of living, and an ocean-deep rest. God is our Father and Christ our Brother, and all who live in love belong to God. Teach us to love each other and lift us to the joy of heaven.

**Verse 4.** Mortals, join the great chorus the morning stars began, an echo of Job 38:7 [^hymnarchive]. A father's love rules us and a brother's love binds people together. We march on singing, winning through struggle, and joyful music lifts us sunward, toward the sun.

Later lines vary too: the 1911 hymnal has "Flowery meadow" for the book's "Blooming meadow" and "leads us Sunward" for "lifts us sunward" [^poems1911][^h1911]. Many later hymnals reword the fourth verse's "Father-love" and "man to man", and some leave out or change the third verse [^hymnarchive].

### Schiller's first verse

This is the first stanza and chorus as Schiller's Gedichte printed them in 1803, the text Beethoven set, with our own plain English beneath each line; its dash after "Brüder" is shown as a comma [^gedichte1803][^wsbeethoven]. Elysium is the paradise of Greek myth.

**Verse 1**
> Freude, schöner Götterfunken,
Joy, beautiful spark of the gods,
> Tochter aus Elisium,
daughter from Elysium,
> Wir betreten feuertrunken
we enter, drunk with fire,
> Himmlische, dein Heiligthum.
heavenly one, your holy place.
> Deine Zauber binden wieder,
Your magic binds together again
> Was die Mode streng getheilt,
what custom has sternly divided;
> Alle Menschen werden Brüder,
all people become brothers
> Wo dein sanfter Flügel weilt.
where your gentle wing rests.

**Chorus**
> Seid umschlungen Millionen!
Be embraced, you millions!
> Diesen Kuß der ganzen Welt!
This kiss for the whole world!
> Brüder, überm Sternenzelt
Brothers, above the tent of stars
> Muß ein lieber Vater wohnen.
a loving Father must dwell.

### The full words

These are van Dyke's four stanzas as The Poems of Henry van Dyke printed them in 1911, pages 332 and 333, with spelling as printed. The book prints a dash after "Christ our Brother,", left out here, and dates the poem 1908 [^poems1911].

**Verse 1**
Joyful, joyful, we adore Thee,
God of glory, Lord of love;
Hearts unfold like flowers before Thee,
Praising Thee their sun above.
Melt the clouds of sin and sadness;
Drive the dark of doubt away;
Giver of immortal gladness,
Fill us with the light of day!

**Verse 2**
All Thy works with joy surround Thee,
Earth and heaven reflect Thy rays,
Stars and angels sing around Thee,
Centre of unbroken praise:
Field and forest, vale and mountain,
Blooming meadow, flashing sea,
Chanting bird and flowing fountain,
Call us to rejoice in Thee.

**Verse 3**
Thou art giving and forgiving,
Ever blessing, ever blest,
Well-spring of the joy of living,
Ocean-depth of happy rest!
Thou our Father, Christ our Brother,
All who live in love are Thine:
Teach us how to love each other,
Lift us to the Joy Divine.

**Verse 4**
Mortals join the mighty chorus,
Which the morning stars began;
Father-love is reigning o'er us,
Brother-love binds man to man.
Ever singing march we onward,
Victors in the midst of strife;
Joyful music lifts us sunward
In the triumph song of life.

## How it is sung

In the symphony the poem is sung in German, by four soloists and a four-part chorus with full orchestra [^score1826][^wsbeethoven]. As a hymn it is sung in English in Protestant churches as praise: the 1911 hymnal files it under "His Fatherhood and Love", the 1919 one under "God in His World" [^h1911][^hay1919]. The tune is HYMN TO JOY, in the meter 8.7.8.7 D: eight lines that alternate eight and seven syllables [^hay1919][^h1911][^hymnary]. The hymnals date the tune differently: 1824, the year of the first performance, in 1911, and 1826, the year the score was published, in 1919 [^h1911][^hay1919][^score1826].

Our melody and words come from H. Augustine Smith's Hymnal for American Youth of 1919, No. 47, in G major and 4/4, checked against Beethoven's score of 1826 [^hay1919][^score1826]. We sing the first verse, with its fourth line as his 1911 book prints it, "Praising Thee their sun above" [^poems1911]. Breaths are written in: several phrases end a beat early, some by shortening the last note and four ("a-dore Thee", "be-fore Thee", "sad-ness", "glad-ness") by singing their last two notes twice as fast. The soprano, alto and tenor sing the words; the bass sings "oo".

## Did you know?

- Ives's BONN of 1846 set an older hymn, "Head of the church triumphant", whose second line is "We joyfully adore thee", some sixty years before van Dyke [^ives][^hymnarchive].
- Beethoven's own manuscript score of the Ninth is in the Berlin State Library, and UNESCO added it to its Memory of the World register in 2001 [^spk][^elbphil].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/ode-to-joy/soprano): you sing the tune, from the D above middle C up to the D an octave higher. It moves by step almost everywhere: "Joy-ful, joy-ful" climbs B, B, C, D and walks back down. The one big leap comes in the sixth line, where "a-way" drops from A to your low D; land it lightly, because the tenor meets you on that same D.
- [Alto](/songs/ode-to-joy/alto): you sing the words close under the tune, from the B below middle C up to B. The lines that start "Joy-ful", "Hearts" and "Giv-er" begin a third under the tune and settle on F-sharp. The lines that start "God of glo-ry", "Prais-ing Thee" and "fill us" begin low on B and climb B, C, E to F-sharp. On "a-way" in the sixth line your F-sharp sits above the soprano's low D for a moment.
- [Tenor](/songs/ode-to-joy/tenor): you sing the middle of the chord, from the D below middle C up to the D just above it, with many repeated notes. You open on that top D. "Prais-ing Thee their" is four Gs, then you drop to the low D for "sun a-bove", and the last line ends the same way, on a long low D for "day!". In the sixth line you climb G, B, C, D on "of doubt a-way" to meet the soprano.
- [Bass](/songs/ode-to-joy/bass): you sing "oo", one note every two beats, from low G up to the G an octave above, mostly G and D. In the first, third and seventh lines you climb G, D and up to the high G. Under "Melt the clouds of sin" you sing D three times, six beats in all, and your only B comes in the sixth line.

## Questions

### Who wrote Ode to Joy, Beethoven or Schiller?

Both: Friedrich Schiller wrote the poem, An die Freude, in 1785, and Beethoven set it in the finale of his Ninth Symphony, first played in 1824 [^gedichte1803][^thalia][^thayer3][^elbphil]. The English hymn Joyful, Joyful, We Adore Thee is Henry van Dyke's, written for Beethoven's tune by 1907 [^h1911][^hymnarchive].

### Was Beethoven deaf when he wrote it?

Yes. Beethoven was deaf by the time the Ninth Symphony was first played in Vienna in May 1824 [^thayer3][^elbphil][^grove]. The best-known story, which Grove and Thayer each say is told in several forms, has the contralto Unger turning him to face the applause he could not hear [^grove][^thayer3].

### Is Ode to Joy the European anthem?

Yes. The Council of Europe chose Beethoven's Ode to Joy theme as its anthem in 1972 [^coearch][^coe][^eu]. The European Community took the same music as its own: in 1985 by the European Union's account, and in 1986 by the Council of Europe's [^eu][^coearch]. The anthem has no words, only music, and it is not meant to replace national anthems [^eu].

### Is Ode to Joy a Christian song?

Schiller's poem is not a hymn: it praises joy and friendship, though its choruses speak of a loving Father above the stars [^gedichte1803]. The English words most often sung to the tune are a Christian hymn: Joyful, Joyful, We Adore Thee, by the Presbyterian minister Henry van Dyke, in hymnals since 1911 [^h1911][^hymnarchive][^tvd].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings Beethoven's tune, which moves almost entirely by step, from the D above middle C up to the D an octave higher [^hay1919]. With two singers the alto sings the words close beneath it; the bass, on "oo", makes three, and the tenor, filling the middle of the chord, makes four.
