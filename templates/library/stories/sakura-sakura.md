Sakura Sakura (さくら さくら), often just Sakura, is the Japanese song of cherry blossom: "sakura" means cherry blossoms, and the song calls everyone out to see the trees in flower [^sokyokushu][^tsuge]. It was printed as a first piece for learners of the koto, the long Japanese zither, and koto teachers still use it [^sokyokushu][^tsuge][^shimada]. Its earliest known printing, words and tune, is a Tokyo koto book of October 1888, made by the Ministry of Education's music office [^sokyokushu][^ndl-record]. We sing it as Rudolf Dittrich, a teacher at Tokyo's music school, printed it in Leipzig in 1894 [^dittrich]. Koto tradition says the tune is older, from the Edo period, but no earlier copy has been found [^crd][^shimada].

## Where it comes from

**The music office.** In October 1879 Japan's Ministry of Education set up the Ongaku Torishirabe Gakari, the Music Investigation Committee, to study and teach music. In October 1887 it became the Tokyo Music School, today the Faculty of Music of Tokyo University of the Arts [^geidai-archives][^geidai-musicology]. Its head, Isawa Shūji (1851 to 1917), had studied teaching in the United States and became the school's first principal [^ndl-isawa][^geidai-isawa].

**The book.** On 28 October 1888 the Ministry published *Sōkyokushū*, with the English title *Collection of Japanese Koto Music* [^sokyokushu][^ndl-record]. Its English preface, dated 1 September 1888, says it gathered pieces of "Japanese popular Koto music" suited to "serve as first steps to learning the Koto" [^sokyokushu-preface]. Where old words and tunes were "liable to offend the public feelings", "pure and elegant ones" had been put in their place [^sokyokushu]. Isawa directed the work; two of the office's staff, named as Y. Satomi and I. Kabe, chose the words, and six musicians "whose speciality is the Japanese popular music", S. Yamase, M. Yamato, S. Yamato, S. Yamataki, K. Araki and T. Okuyama, chose the music [^sokyokushu][^sokyokushu-preface].

![[2]]

**The 1888 page.** Sakura is No. 2 in the book. The music opens with a chart of hira-jōshi, the koto's basic tuning, its 13 strings numbered from one to thirteen [^sokyokushu][^kb-hirajoshi]. Sakura is set on two staves: the voice, with the words in katakana and roman letters under each note, and the koto, with small numbers beside its notes [^sokyokushu]. The words are spelled the old way, "yayohi", "nihohi" and "idzuru" in the kana, though the roman letters give them as sung, "yayoi", "nioi" and "izuru" [^sokyokushu]. On a separate page the words are written out in brush script, with the song's old name in small letters beside the title: "formerly Saita sakura", the cherry has bloomed [^sokyokushu][^crd].

**The older piece.** Where the tune came from is disputed. A koto player of the Yamada school told the music historian Tanabe Hisao that "Saita sakura" was a beginners' piece of the Edo period, and that the music office kept its tune and changed only its words; a Japanese library's research answer says the same [^shimada][^crd]. But no Edo-period copy of the tune or of these words has been found, and a 2002 study concluded that the 1888 Sakura was most likely new. It notes that many popular songs of the day began with the words "saita sakura", so the old name may mark a popular song reformed rather than an old tune kept [^shimada]. A London book on Japanese music of 1893 does show a koto piece of that name in use: a girl's first koto lessons, before her first diploma, included "Saita Sakurai", which the book glossed as "the Song of the Blooming of the Cherry-trees" [^piggott]. Its tune opens like Sakura's, then goes its own way as a longer piece for the instrument [^piggott]. Why the 1888 book changed the words is not known [^crd].

![[3]]

**The page we read.** Rudolf Dittrich (1861 to 1919), a graduate of the Vienna Conservatory, taught at the Tokyo Music School from 1888 to 1894 [^mdw][^dittrich]. His *Nippon Gakufu* (日本樂譜, "Japanese scores") holds six Japanese popular songs "collected and arranged for the pianoforte", published by Breitkopf & Härtel in Leipzig, its first page marked "Copyright 1894". Its colour cover, printed in Tokyo, shows a numbered scene for each song; scene 3 is people under cherry trees in flower [^dittrich][^imslp-dittrich]. Sakura is No. 3, on pages 4 and 5. At the top Dittrich prints the words as a poem in roman letters, with German and English translations and a footnote on "Yayoi". Below them comes a piano piece marked "Semplice", whose top line is the tune; a footnote at its first bar reads "Mit Kotobegleitung", with koto accompaniment [^dittrich].

**After 1888.** The 2002 study found Sakura little sung outside the Yamada school's koto lessons until 1941, when the Ministry's school songbook printed it with new words and made it a fixture of school music [^shimada][^utanohon].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Sakura is an ancient koto piece of the Edo period, and songbooks label it "Nihon koyō", an old Japanese song [^shimada][^crd]. What the record shows: its earliest known printing, tune and words, is the Ministry's koto book of 1888, where its words replaced older ones; no Edo-period copy has been found [^sokyokushu][^shimada].

## What the words mean

The words are seven short lines with no known author. Their earliest known printing is the 1888 book, and Dittrich printed the same words in 1894 [^sokyokushu][^crd][^dittrich].

**"Sakura sakura".** Cherry blossoms, cherry blossoms: the call that names the whole song.

**"Yayoi no sora wa".** "Yayoi" is the old name of the third month of the lunar calendar [^kb-yayoi]; Dittrich's footnote calls it "a poetical expression for the third month of the ancient Japanese calendar" [^dittrich]. By today's calendar it falls from about late March to late April [^kb-yayoi]. "No sora wa": the sky. So: the spring sky, in cherry time.

**"Miwatasu kagiri".** As far as the eye can see.

**"Kasumi ka kumo ka".** Is it mist, or is it cloud? "Kasumi" is the soft haze of spring, a word that marks spring in Japanese poetry [^kb-kasumi].

**"Nioi zo izuru".** In old Japanese "nioi" meant a colour that glows and shines as well as a scent [^kb-niou][^kb-nioi]. "Zo" stresses it, and "izuru" means comes out or rises. So a glow and a scent rise up from the trees. Translators have read it as scent: Dittrich's English of 1894 has "No! for the fragrance of the blossoms diffuses itself" [^dittrich], and a modern translation does the same [^tsuge].

**"Izaya izaya, mi ni yukan".** "Izaya" is an old word for calling people to act: come now [^kb-izaya]. "Mi ni yukan": let's go and see them [^dittrich][^tsuge].

### The full words

These are the words as Rudolf Dittrich prints them above his music, in roman letters, with his capitals and punctuation; his asterisk after "Yayoi", pointing to his footnote, is left out [^dittrich]. He prints no Japanese script, so the line above each of his is the 1888 *Sōkyokushū*'s katakana, the same words with its old spellings [^sokyokushu]. In today's spelling they read: さくら さくら / やよいの空は / 見わたす限り / かすみか雲か / 匂いぞ出ずる / いざや いざや / 見にゆかん. The words are public domain: traditional, with no known author, their earliest known printing Tokyo, 1888; Dittrich printed them in Leipzig in 1894 and died in 1919 [^sokyokushu][^dittrich].

## How it is sung

Our arrangement, new for Stackapella, reads the tune and words from Dittrich's 1894 page [^dittrich]. His tune is the top line of his piano piece, written with one flat, in the in scale on A; we sing it a whole tone lower, in a beginner's range. We change two things: we rest at the start of the last line, where his piano plays a note, and we shorten the long notes that end lines, to breathe. He prints the words above the music, not under the notes, so we fit the syllables to the notes as the 1888 *Sōkyokushū* does, rest included [^sokyokushu]. It is slow, in 4/4, sung in Japanese with the words shown in romaji, for 2 to 4 singers. The soprano sings the tune. The others hold single notes on "oo", like a koto's open strings, with no Western chords. We leave out his piano's opening and closing bars. For two singers it is soprano and alto; add the bass for three and the tenor for four.

## Versions and other words

**The 1941 words.** In 1941 the Ministry of Education's school songbook *Uta no hon*, volume 2, printed the tune as "Sakura sakura" with new words and changed its last line [^utanohon][^shimada]. They begin "Sakura sakura, noyama mo sato mo": cherry blossoms, cherry blossoms, over hills and fields and villages, as far as the eye can see. "Asahi ni niou": glowing in the morning sun. Cherry blossoms, cherry blossoms, "hana zakari", in full bloom [^utanohon]. The teacher's book said the old words were too hard for children of that age, so new words were written in the same spirit [^crd][^shimada]. The 1941 words carry no author's name, only the Ministry's, and the National Diet Library marks the book public domain [^ndl-1941][^utanohon]. Japanese school books have printed the 1941 words ever since, labelled "old Japanese song"; some books print the 1888 words as a second verse [^shimada][^crd]. We sing the 1888 words, as Dittrich printed them.

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/sakura-sakura/soprano): the tune, in its five-note scale. Lines two and four ("yayoi no sora wa", "kasumi ka kumo ka") are the same music and climb to the top E-flat; lines three and five ("miwatasu kagiri", "nioi zo izuru") are the same music too, and fall to the low D. "Izaya izaya" repeats the opening's "sakura sakura". Watch the quick two-note turns on one syllable: "ra" of "sora", "gi", "mo", "zu" and "yu".
- [Alto](/songs/sakura-sakura/alto): one soft C on "oo", breathing apart from the tenor and bass so the sound never stops, until all three breathe together before "mi ni yukan".
- [Tenor](/songs/sakura-sakura/tenor): one G on "oo", a fourth below the alto's C, breathing at your own moments until the breath all three share before the last line.
- [Bass](/songs/sakura-sakura/bass): one low C on "oo", an octave under the alto, the floor of the koto-like sound. The last line is two long notes, held to the end.

## Questions

### What does Sakura Sakura mean in English?

"Sakura sakura" means "cherry blossoms, cherry blossoms". The 1888 words go on: the spring sky in cherry time, as far as the eye can see; is it mist, or is it cloud? A glow and a scent rise up; come now, come now, let's go and see them [^sokyokushu][^kb-yayoi][^dittrich][^tsuge].

### Who wrote Sakura Sakura?

No one knows. Its earliest known printing is the Ministry of Education's koto book of 1888, which notes beside the title that it was formerly "Saita sakura" [^sokyokushu]. Koto tradition calls that an Edo-period beginners' piece, but a 2002 study found no copy older than 1888 and thinks the tune was new then [^crd][^shimada]. The book names the two music-office staff who chose its words, but not who wrote Sakura's [^sokyokushu]. Rudolf Dittrich, whose 1894 printing we sing, arranged it for piano; he did not write it [^dittrich].

### Why does Sakura Sakura sound Japanese?

Its tune uses only five notes, the in scale of Japanese koto and shamisen music, mi, fa, la, si and do, with two half steps [^kb-miyakobushi][^kb-in]. It was printed for the koto in hira-jōshi, its basic tuning [^sokyokushu][^kb-hirajoshi], and it ends on a note that sounds open rather than finished.
