Sakura Sakura (さくら さくら), often just Sakura, is the Japanese song of cherry blossom: "sakura" means cherry blossoms, and the song calls everyone out to see the trees in flower [^sokyokushu][^tsuge]. It was printed as a first piece for learners of the koto, the long Japanese zither, and has been used to teach koto beginners ever since [^sokyokushu][^tsuge][^shimada]. The words we sing come from a Tokyo koto book of October 1888, made by the Ministry of Education's music office [^sokyokushu][^ndl-record]. Koto tradition says the tune is older, from the Edo period, but the sources disagree: no copy from before 1888 has been found [^crd][^shimada]. In 1941 the Ministry gave it a second set of words for schoolchildren [^utanohon][^crd].

## Where it comes from

**The music office.** In October 1879 Japan's Ministry of Education set up the Ongaku Torishirabe Gakari, the Music Investigation Committee, to study and teach music. In October 1887 it became the Tokyo Music School, today the Faculty of Music of Tokyo University of the Arts [^geidai-archives][^geidai-musicology]. Its head, Isawa Shūji (1851 to 1917), had studied teaching in the United States and became the school's first principal [^ndl-isawa][^geidai-isawa].

**The book.** On 28 October 1888 the Ministry published *Sōkyokushū*, with the English title *Collection of Japanese Koto Music*, priced at 75 sen [^sokyokushu][^ndl-record]. Its English preface, dated 1 September 1888, says it gathered pieces of "Japanese popular Koto music" suited to "serve as first steps to learning the Koto". Where old words and tunes were "liable to offend the public feelings", "pure and elegant ones" had been put in their place: some songs were rewritten whole, some only in part, and some left alone [^sokyokushu]. Isawa directed the work; two of the office's staff, named as Y. Satomi and I. Kabe, chose the words, and six musicians of Japanese popular music chose the music [^sokyokushu].

**What the page shows.** Sakura is No. 2 in the book, after a song called Hime-matsu. The music opens with a chart of hira-jōshi, the koto's basic tuning, its 13 strings numbered from one to thirteen [^sokyokushu][^kb-hirajoshi]. Sakura is set on two staves: the voice, with the words in katakana and roman letters under each note, and the koto, with small numbers beside its notes. The voice has fourteen bars; then the koto plays four more bars alone [^sokyokushu]. The words are spelled the old way, "yayohi", "nihohi" and "idzuru" in the kana, though the roman letters give them as sung, "yayoi", "nioi" and "izuru" [^sokyokushu]. On a separate page the words are written out in brush script, with the song's old name in small letters beside the title: "formerly Saita sakura", the cherry has bloomed [^sokyokushu][^crd].

**The older piece.** Where the tune came from is disputed. A koto player of the Yamada school told the music historian Tanabe Hisao that "Saita sakura" was a beginners' piece of the Edo period, and that the music office kept its tune and changed only its words; a Japanese library's research answer says the same [^shimada][^crd]. But no Edo-period copy of the tune or of these words has been found, and a 2002 study concluded that the 1888 Sakura was most likely new. It notes that many popular songs of the day began with the words "saita sakura", so the old name may mark a popular song reformed rather than an old tune kept [^shimada]. A London book on Japanese music of 1893 does show a koto piece of that name in use: a girl's first koto lessons, before her first diploma, included "Saita Sakurai", which the book glossed as "the Song of the Blooming of the Cherry-trees" [^piggott]. Its tune, printed on the staff, opens like Sakura, two notes alike and then a longer note a step higher, twice over, and then goes its own way as a longer piece for the instrument [^piggott]. Why the 1888 book changed the words is not known [^crd].

**After 1888.** The 2002 study found Sakura little sung outside the Yamada school's koto lessons until 1941, when the Ministry's school songbook printed it with new words and made it a fixture of school music [^shimada][^utanohon].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Sakura is an ancient folk song, a koto piece of the Edo period, and songbooks label it "Nihon koyō", an old Japanese song [^shimada][^crd]. What the record shows: its earliest known printing, tune and words, is the Ministry's koto book of 1888, where its words replaced older ones; no copy from the Edo period has been found, and the study that traced its history questions the label "old song" [^sokyokushu][^shimada].

## What the words mean

The words are the 1888 book's, seven short lines with no known author [^sokyokushu][^crd].

**"Sakura sakura".** Cherry blossoms, cherry blossoms: the call that names the whole song.

**"Yayoi no sora wa".** "Yayoi" is the old name of the third month of the lunar calendar [^kb-yayoi]. By today's calendar it falls from about late March to late April, and one Japanese encyclopedia notes it was also called the flower-viewing month and the cherry month [^kb-yayoi]. "No sora wa": the sky. So: the spring sky, in cherry time.

**"Miwatasu kagiri".** As far as the eye can see.

**"Kasumi ka kumo ka".** Is it mist, or is it cloud? "Kasumi" is the soft haze of spring, a word that marks spring in Japanese poetry [^kb-kasumi].

**"Nioi zo izuru".** In old Japanese "nioi" meant a colour that glows and shines as well as a scent [^kb-niou][^kb-nioi]. "Zo" stresses it, and "izuru" means comes out or rises. So a glow and a scent rise up from the trees. Translators have read it as scent: Rudolf Dittrich's piano edition of 1894, as Daisyfield transcribes it, gave the line as the fragrance of the blossoms spreading through the air [^daisy-sakura], and a modern translation does the same [^tsuge].

**"Izaya izaya, mi ni yukan".** "Izaya" is an old word for calling people to act: come now [^kb-izaya]. "Mi ni yukan": let's go and see them [^daisy-sakura][^tsuge].

## How it is sung

Sakura belongs to spring and the cherry blossoms, and its words are an invitation to go out and see them [^sokyokushu][^kb-yayoi].

Our arrangement, new for Stackapella, is read from the 1888 *Sōkyokushū*. The book writes the tune in the in scale on E; we move it a minor third up, so it sits in a beginner's range. It is slow, in 4/4, sung in Japanese with the words shown in romaji, for 2 to 4 singers. The soprano sings the tune. The others hold single notes on "oo", like a koto's open strings, with no Western chords: the alto a C, the tenor the G below it, the bass the C an octave under the alto. We leave out the koto's closing bars. For two singers it is soprano and alto; add the bass for three and the tenor for four.

## Versions and other words

**The 1941 words.** In 1941 the Ministry of Education's school songbook *Uta no hon*, volume 2, printed the tune as "Sakura sakura" with new words, and only those words, and changed its last line [^utanohon][^shimada]. They begin "Sakura sakura, noyama mo sato mo": cherry blossoms, cherry blossoms, over hills and fields and villages, as far as the eye can see. "Asahi ni niou": glowing in the morning sun. Cherry blossoms, cherry blossoms, "hana zakari", in full bloom [^utanohon]. The teacher's book said the old words were too hard for children of that age, so new words were written in the same spirit [^crd][^shimada]. The 1941 words carry no author's name, only the Ministry's, and the National Diet Library marks the book public domain [^ndl-1941][^utanohon]. Japanese school books have printed the 1941 words ever since, labelled "old Japanese song", which is why they are often taken for the present words; some books print the 1888 words as a second verse [^shimada][^crd]. We sing the 1888 words.

**Rudolf Dittrich's piano version.** Rudolf Dittrich, who taught at the Tokyo Music School from 1888 to 1894 [^mdw][^daisy-dittrich], arranged six Japanese songs for piano as *Nippon Gakufu*, published by Breitkopf & Härtel in Leipzig in 1894, with Sakura among them and the words in Japanese, German and English [^imslp-dittrich][^daisy-dittrich][^daisy-sakura].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/sakura-sakura/soprano): the tune, in its five-note scale. Lines two and four ("yayoi no sora wa", "kasumi ka kumo ka") are the same music and climb to the top E-flat; lines three and five ("miwatasu kagiri", "nioi zo izuru") are the same music too, and fall to the low D. "Izaya izaya" repeats the opening's "sakura sakura". Watch the quick two-note turns on one syllable: "ra" of "sora", "gi", "mo", "zu" and "yu".
- [Alto](/songs/sakura-sakura/alto): one soft C on "oo", breathing apart from the tenor and bass so the sound never stops, until all three breathe together before "mi ni yukan".
- [Tenor](/songs/sakura-sakura/tenor): one G on "oo", a fourth below the alto's C, breathing at your own moments until the breath all three share before the last line.
- [Bass](/songs/sakura-sakura/bass): one low C on "oo", an octave under the alto, the floor of the koto-like sound. The last line is two long notes, held to the end.

## Questions

### What does Sakura Sakura mean in English?

"Sakura sakura" means "cherry blossoms, cherry blossoms". The 1888 words go on: the spring sky in cherry time, as far as the eye can see; is it mist, or is it cloud? A glow and a scent rise up; come now, come now, let's go and see them [^sokyokushu][^kb-yayoi][^daisy-sakura][^tsuge]. "Yayoi" is the old third month, about late March to late April [^kb-yayoi].

### Who wrote Sakura Sakura?

No one knows. Its earliest known printing is the Ministry of Education's koto book of 1888, which notes beside the title that it was formerly "Saita sakura" [^sokyokushu]. Koto tradition calls that an Edo-period beginners' piece, but a 2002 study found no copy older than 1888 and thinks the tune was new then [^crd][^shimada]. The book names the two music-office staff who chose its words, but not who wrote Sakura's [^sokyokushu]. The second set of words, "Noyama mo sato mo", came from the Ministry in 1941 [^utanohon][^crd].

### Why does Sakura Sakura sound Japanese?

Its tune uses only five notes, the in scale of Japanese koto and shamisen music, mi, fa, la, si and do, with two half steps [^kb-miyakobushi][^kb-in]. It was printed for the koto in hira-jōshi, its basic tuning [^sokyokushu][^kb-hirajoshi], and it ends on a note that sounds open rather than finished.

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings it from the first note to the last. The alto, tenor and bass each hold one note on "oo" under it, a C, a G and a low C, so two singers give the tune over the alto's soft C.
