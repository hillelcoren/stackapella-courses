Frère Jacques, sung in English as Are You Sleeping? or Are You Sleeping, Brother John?, is a round: one short tune that singers begin one after another, so that it sounds against itself [^grove-round][^rodeheaver1928]. In the French words a friar called Brother Jacques is asked whether he is asleep and told to ring the bell for matins, the prayers of the night [^widor1883][^academie-matines]. Its earliest known printing, in Paris in 1811, gives the tune with no words at all [^cle1811][^ballad]. By 1817 a book on music called it the canon everyone knows [^stendhal1817], and in 1916 an American music educator printed the English words for a national week of community singing [^normal1916]. Gustav Mahler turned it into a minor-key funeral march in his First Symphony [^sfs][^bso].

## Where it comes from

**Before print.** No one knows who made the tune. The sheet-music historian James Fuld reported a manuscript copy of the tune from about 1780, under another title; we have not seen it [^ballad]. In 2014 a French scholar of Jean-Philippe Rameau (1683 to 1764), writing his biography, argued that the canon is his [^aleteia][^slippedisc][^loc-rameau][^imslp-rameaudates]. According to press reports of her 2014 biography, a manuscript of 86 canons in the Bibliothèque nationale de France, gathered by the collector Jacques Joseph Marie Decroix, names Rameau as the canon's composer, and manuscript pages bound into Louis-Joseph Francoeur's Diapason général, a treatise of 1772, mention four canons by Rameau, this one among them [^aleteia][^slippedisc][^imslp-rameau]. IMSLP dates those pages only to about 1772, the treatise's year, and files the canon as attributed to Rameau, not certainly his [^imslp-rameau]. The attribution is an argument, not a settled fact.

**The earliest known printing.** In 1811 Pierre Capelle, who signed the title page only "C***, du Caveau moderne", published La Clé du Caveau in Paris: a book of hundreds of numbered song tunes, so that song lovers far from Paris and its theatres could find the tune a song was meant to be sung to [^cle1811][^ballad]. His preface explains the timbre: a tune named by the first line of the song it came from [^cle1811]. Number 726, on page 309, is our tune: one line of notes in G, marked 2 for two beats to the bar, with no words, starting halfway through a bar [^cle1811]. The table of tunes names it "Air des Matines du frère Jacques, ou frère Jacques, dormez-vous?", the air of Brother Jacques's matins, or "Brother Jacques, are you sleeping?" [^cle1811]. Since a timbre is the first line of a song, the French words were already known in 1811, though the page prints none [^cle1811].

![[1]]

**"Everyone knows it."** In 1817 a book of lives of Haydn, Mozart and Metastasio, first issued in 1814 under the pen name Bombet and later printed under Stendhal's name, explained a fugue in a footnote: everyone knows the canon of "Frère Jacques, dormez-vous? Sonnez les matines", and that is a kind of fugue [^stendhal1817][^stendhal-levy]. François-Joseph Fétis, director of the Brussels Conservatoire, said the same in his guide to music, printed at Brussels in 1839 and at Paris in 1847: everyone knows the canon that begins "Frère Jacques, dormez-vous?" [^fetis1839][^fetis1847].

**Words and tune together.** James Fuld, as the Ballad Index reports him, dated the earliest printing of the words and music together to 1860; we have not seen that printing [^ballad]. The earliest page with the French words and the tune together that we have read is in Vieilles chansons pour les petits enfants, old songs for little children, published in Paris in 1883 with piano parts by Charles-Marie Widor [^widor1883][^met1883]. On pages 18 and 19 it is a canon for three voices in G, each coming in two bars after the last, above a plain piano bass, framed by a drawing of five bells in a belfry, with friars hauling on the ropes [^widor1883].

![[2]]

**The English words.** "Jacques" is the French form of James, not John [^yonge]. Who wrote "Are you sleeping, Brother John?" is not known. The earliest printing of the English words we have found is in a teachers' magazine, Normal Instructor and Primary Plans, in February 1916. Peter W. Dykema, head of community music at the University of Wisconsin, listed "Are You Sleeping" among five rounds for a National Week of Song around Washington's birthday, and printed its words [^normal1916]. Rounds, he wrote, "can hardly be surpassed for developing interest and enthusiasm" [^normal1916]. By 1928 it was in Homer Rodeheaver's Sociability Songs, the book our tune is read from [^rodeheaver1928].

![[3]]

**Myth and record.** Often told: the song mocks Frère Jacques, a 17th-century surgeon who cut patients for bladder stones dressed in a monk's habit [^mentalfloss][^ganem1999]. What the record shows: he was real, and a London book of 1726 credits "Frere Jacques in France" with first trying the operation it describes [^douglas1726]. But two urologists who reviewed his career in 1999 found no direct link between him and the song [^ganem1999].

## What the words mean

**Brother Jacques.** "Frère" means brother. In French it was also the usual name for any religious who was not a priest, as in "Frère Antoine" [^academie-frere]. The English word friar comes from it [^webster-friar]. So Frère Jacques is a friar or lay brother called Jacques.

**"Dormez-vous?"** Are you sleeping? The singers are waking him.

**"Sonnez les matines."** Ring the bell for matins. Matins was the first part of the daily round of prayer in a church or monastery, psalms and readings said at night [^academie-matines][^catholic-matins]. The Académie française's dictionary of 1835 gives "Sonner les matines" as its own example [^academie-matines]. The brother's job is to ring it, and he has overslept.

**"Din, din, don."** The bell itself [^widor1883].

**What English changed.** In the English words, "Morning bells are ringing": the bells are already ringing, and the sleeper is late [^normal1916][^rodeheaver1928]. "Jacques" became John, though the name means James [^yonge]. Our 1928 page prints "ding, ding, dong"; we sing "ding, dang, dong" [^rodeheaver1928].

### The French words

These are the words as printed on pages 18 and 19 of Vieilles chansons pour les petits enfants (Paris, 1883), with our own plain English under each line [^widor1883].

> Frère Jacques, Frère Jacques,
Brother Jacques, Brother Jacques,
> Dormez-vous? Dormez-vous?
are you sleeping? Are you sleeping?
> Sonnez les matines, Sonnez les matines,
Ring the bell for matins, ring the bell for matins,
> Din, din, don! Din, din, don!
ding, ding, dong! Ding, ding, dong!

### The full words

These are the words as printed on page 121 of Rodeheaver's Sociability Songs (Chicago, 1928), the page our arrangement is read from. They are traditional, no author is named, and the book was printed before 1931 by an editor who died in 1955, so they are in the public domain.

Are you sleeping, are you sleeping?
Brother John, Brother John.
Morning bells are ringing, Morning bells are ringing,
Ding, ding, dong, Ding, ding, dong.

## How it is sung

**As a round.** In a round, each singer begins the same melody at a fixed distance after the one before [^grove-round]. Widor's 1883 page brings in three voices, two bars apart [^widor1883]; the 1928 page numbers four entries [^rodeheaver1928]. In 1916 it was one of five rounds suggested for schools and community sings in a National Week of Song [^normal1916], and later Scout and 4-H songbooks kept it [^ballad][^scouts1931].

**What our arrangement sings.** Our tune follows the 1928 page note for note in the same key, E-flat, written in 4/4 for the page's 2/4, with the same entry points [^rodeheaver1928]. The soprano starts alone; the alto comes in two bars later at the same pitch; then the tenor and then the bass, each two bars after the last and an octave lower. Everyone sings the tune twice through, our choice, and the parts stop one by one, so the bass finishes alone. We shorten "John" and the first three "dong"s to a single beat with a breath after, and sing "ding, dang, dong" for the page's "ding, ding, dong".

## Versions and other words

- **Mahler's funeral march.** The third movement of Gustav Mahler's First Symphony, first played in Budapest on 20 November 1889, takes the round and sets it in a gloomy minor key as a funeral march "in the manner of Callot" [^sfs][^bso]. Mahler keeps it a round: the tune passes from a solo double bass to bassoon to bass tuba [^bso][^gustavmahler].
- **New words for the same round.** Under the round, our 1928 page offers four sets of words "to the above round": Cheer Up, Black-eyed Susan!, Rheumatism and Perfect Posture [^rodeheaver1928].

## Did you know?

- Our 1928 page prints Three Blind Mice and Are You Sleeping one above the other, both in E-flat, with a note that the two rounds may be sung together [^rodeheaver1928].
- Fétis put Frère Jacques among the canons once sung at table, most of which, he said, had comic or bawdy words [^fetis1839][^fetis1847].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/are-you-sleeping/soprano): you start the round alone and carry the tune twice through, so the others hear it first. Your top note is the C on the "-ing" of "Morning", and your lowest the B-flat on every "dang". "Morning bells are" is the quick part, two notes to a beat.
- [Alto](/songs/are-you-sleeping/alto): the same tune at the same pitch, two bars after the soprano: nothing new to learn. The hard part is holding your place: as you start "Are you sleeping", the soprano is already on "Brother John".
- [Tenor](/songs/are-you-sleeping/tenor): the same tune an octave lower, two bars after the alto, from the low B-flat on "dang" up to middle C on the "-ing" of "Morning".
- [Bass](/songs/are-you-sleeping/bass): the tenor's low tune, coming in last, two bars after it; from your entry all four lines sound at once. You finish alone on the bells' line, "ding, dang, dong" twice.

## Questions

### Who wrote Frère Jacques?

No one knows for certain. James Fuld reported a manuscript copy from about 1780, and the earliest known printing, in Paris in 1811, names no composer [^ballad][^cle1811]. In 2014 a Rameau scholar argued that Jean-Philippe Rameau wrote it, pointing to a manuscript collection of canons that names him [^aleteia][^slippedisc]. The attribution is not settled; IMSLP files it only as attributed to him [^imslp-rameau]. The English words are anonymous, in print by 1916 [^normal1916].

### What does Frère Jacques mean in English?

"Frère Jacques" means Brother Jacques, a friar. The words ask him, "Are you sleeping?", and tell him, "Ring the bell for matins", the night prayers he should be waking the house for [^academie-frere][^academie-matines][^widor1883]. The last line is the bell: "din, din, don". The English version calls him Brother John and has the morning bells already ringing [^normal1916].

### Does Frère Jacques have a dark meaning?

Not in the record. A story links it to Frère Jacques, a 17th-century stone surgeon who dressed as a monk [^mentalfloss][^ganem1999]. He was real, but urologists who studied his career in 1999 found no direct link to the song [^ganem1999][^douglas1726]. The words themselves, in print by 1817, are about a friar who oversleeps and a bell [^stendhal1817][^widor1883].

### Is Frère Jacques a canon?

Yes. A round is a canon at the unison: every singer sings the same tune, starting at set distances [^grove-round]. Writers called Frère Jacques a canon in 1817 and in 1839 [^stendhal1817][^fetis1839], and Widor's 1883 page sets it for three voices entering two bars apart [^widor1883].

### Which part sings the tune?

All of them. In a round everyone sings the same tune, starting at different times [^grove-round]. In our arrangement the soprano starts, the alto comes in two bars later at the same pitch, and the tenor and bass follow, each two bars apart and an octave lower. Each part goes through the tune twice, and the bass ends alone.
