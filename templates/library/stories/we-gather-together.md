We Gather Together, sung to the tune KREMSER and once known in America as the Prayer of Thanksgiving, is the hymn many American churches sing on Thanksgiving Day [^hymnary-text][^komter1942]. It began as a Dutch song, "Wilt heden nu treden" ("Come, step forward today"), whose earliest known printing is in Adrianus Valerius's Nederlandtsche Gedenck-clanck, printed at Haarlem in 1626 [^valerius1626][^dbnl1942]. There it gives thanks to God after the Dutch victories over Spain in 1597 [^valerius1626][^komter1942]. It reached English by way of Eduard Kremser's Vienna setting of 1877 and Theodore Baker's words of 1894 [^komter1942][^loc1894].

## Where it comes from

**The book.** Adrianus Valerius (in Dutch, Adriaen Valerius) lived at Veere, in Zeeland [^valerius1626][^meertens1942]. His Nederlandtsche Gedenck-clanck, a "memorial sound" of the Netherlands, tells the Dutch revolt against Spain up to 1625, with songs set among the prose [^valerius1626][^dbnl1942]. He died in 1625, and his heirs had it printed at Haarlem in 1626 [^valerius1626][^meertens1942]. Its title page calls the songs "meest alle nieu", mostly all new, each with its notes and tablature for lute and cittern [^valerius1626]. Scholars think that, apart from a few older songs such as the Wilhelmus, the songs are his own [^tenhaeff1942][^vanduyse].

![[1]]

**The page.** In the Ghent University copy of 1626, the song stands on the page numbered 170, straight after Valerius's account of 1597 [^valerius1626]. Two pages earlier, a note in the margin marks the year and "the battle of Turnhout, and great victory of Prince Maurits": on 24 January his cavalry routed a Spanish force, and its commander, Count Varax, was killed [^valerius1626][^rct][^dbnl1942]. Valerius writes that days of prayer and thanksgiving were held, giving God alone the honour, and medals struck reading "Venit, Vidit, Deus vicit": he came, he saw, God conquered [^valerius1626][^komter1942]. A short poem and quotations, the last from Psalm 33, lead into the song [^valerius1626][^kjv-ps33].

The song has no title, only "Stem: Hey wilder dan wild", the tune to sing it to [^valerius1626]. The melody stands in diamond-headed notes with the first verse under it, opening on a large decorated letter; the other three verses follow, two side by side and the last centred under them, and lute tablature runs along the foot [^valerius1626].

![[2]]

**The tune.** Valerius's own table of tunes lists "Hey! wilder dan wilt" among the Dutch ones [^dbnl-tafel]. It was older than his book: Hymnary.org's notes and the 1942 edition both trace it to a Dutch song of the sixteenth century [^hymnary-kremser][^komter1942], one in which a young man, betrayed by his sweetheart, is to be put to death [^komter1942][^vanduyse1903].

**Vienna, 1877.** In 1871 A. D. Loman published a set of the long-forgotten songs with piano, and the Dutch began to sing them again [^komter1942][^bohme]. Six years later Eduard Kremser, choirmaster of the Wiener Männergesang-Verein, the Vienna men's choral society, arranged six of them for soloists, men's choir and orchestra, in German words by Josef Weyl [^hanslick1878][^oeml][^bohme]. The sixth, the Dankgebet, was this song, beginning "Wir treten zum Beten" ("We step forward to pray") [^komter1942][^imslp-kremser]. The choir sang the set in Vienna in 1877 [^komter1942][^bohme]. Reviewing it on 3 January 1878, the critic Eduard Hanslick admired how the Dankgebet grows, from a first verse in unison over soft organ to a third in four parts with full organ and orchestra [^hanslick1878].

**The English words.** Theodore Baker, born in New York in 1851, studied music at Leipzig [^bakerdict][^kirkpatrick] and became literary editor and translator for the publisher G. Schirmer; he died in Dresden in 1934 [^bakerdict][^hymnary-text]. In 1894 Schirmer published Kremser's six songs in English as Six Ancient Folk-Songs of the Netherlands (A.D. 1626) [^loc1894][^komter1942]; when Baker renewed its copyright in 1922, the entry read "by E. Kremser, tr. by T. Baker" [^ccr1922].

**In America.** In 1902 the organist of the Brick Presbyterian Church in New York asked Julia Cady Cory, whose family attended the church, for new words to the tune for Thanksgiving Day; a Lutheran hymnal handbook, quoted by Hymnary.org, says he found the old words militaristic and unchristian [^hymnary-cory][^blb-cory]. The Apollo Club sang it as "A Prayer of Thanksgiving" at New York's Hudson-Fulton Celebration in 1909, and it opened Harvard's Memorial Day commemoration of its First World War dead in 1920 [^hudsonfulton1910][^harvard1920]. In 1935 The Methodist Hymnal printed it as No. 20 [^mh1935][^ncca1935].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Dutch Protestants, under Spanish rule, wrote it to celebrate the victory at Turnhout in 1597, as a song of freedom, and "the wicked oppressing" are their persecutors [^kirkpatrick][^hymnary-text]. What the record shows: the earliest known printing is Valerius's book of 1626 [^valerius1626][^komter1942]. From its text, the 1942 edition's historian, N. B. Tenhaeff, dated the writing of the whole book to between 1622 and early 1625, mostly 1623 and 1624, though he found the songs themselves very hard to date; F. C. Wieder held that Valerius made them one after another shortly before his death, as a poet looking back, not a witness [^tenhaeff1942][^vanduyse]. The same edition's music historian, though, calls it the song Valerius wrote after Maurits's victories of 1597, without saying when [^komter1942]. So the song may remember 1597 from about twenty-five years on; when it was written is not known. The lines about the wicked oppressing belong to the English, not to his four Dutch verses [^valerius1626][^harvard1920]. The reading is old: in 1878 Hanslick heard the songs as coming from Holland's oppression by Spain under Alba, a generation before 1597 [^hanslick1878].

### Timeline

- 1597: Dutch victories at Turnhout and through the autumn, with days of thanksgiving [^valerius1626][^rct]
- 1622 to 1625: Valerius writes the book, by the 1942 edition's dating; he dies at Veere in January 1625 [^tenhaeff1942][^meertens1942]
- 1626: Earliest known printing, in the Nederlandtsche Gedenck-clanck, Haarlem [^valerius1626][^dbnl1942]
- 1877: Kremser's German Dankgebet is sung in Vienna [^komter1942][^bohme]

## What the words mean

The words we sing are Theodore Baker's English of 1894, three verses made from Kremser's German setting, not a line-by-line translation of Valerius's Dutch, which has four verses [^loc1894][^hymnary-kremser][^valerius1626]. His words were printed two ways. Harvard's program of 1920 and The Abingdon Hymnal of 1928 have "cease them from distressing", "the glory be thine" and "Leader in battle"; The Beacon Hymnal of 1924, which we sing from, and The American Student Hymnal, which prints it by Schirmer's permission, have "now cease from distressing", "all glory be thine" and "Leader triumphant" [^harvard1920][^abingdon1928][^beacon1924][^ash1928]. We have not seen Schirmer's printing of 1894, so which wording came first is not known.

1. We come together to ask God's blessing. He "chastens", that is, he corrects and disciplines, as the Bible says of a father with his children, and he "hastens" to make his will known [^harvard1920][^kjv-heb12]. The wicked who oppress us stop troubling us. Praise his name: he does not forget his own.
2. God is beside us as our guide, joining with us, setting up ("ordaining") and keeping ("maintaining") his kingdom. So from the start we were winning the fight; God was at our side, and the glory is his [^harvard1920].
3. We praise God as "Leader triumphant" and pray that he will still be our defender. Let his people ("congregation") escape their great troubles ("tribulation"). His name be praised; Lord, make us free [^beacon1924][^ash1928].

Valerius's Dutch, given in full below, thanks God for striking down "our enemy"; its third verse pictures the devil as a roaring lion, as in the First Epistle of Peter, and its fourth echoes "watch and pray" from Matthew [^valerius1626][^kjv-1pet5][^kjv-mt26]. The 1942 editors gloss "Maeckt u" as "take care", "driesschen" as "bluster, rage" and "preeu" as "a bite" [^dbnl1942].

### Valerius's Dutch

Valerius's four verses as printed on p. 170 of the 1626 book [^valerius1626], in his spelling, with our own English under each line. The printer's double commas, which mark the rhymes inside a line, are made single here, and "V" at the start of the last verse's third line is the old capital U.

**Verse 1**
> Wilt heden Nu treden Voor God den Heere;
Come today, step forward now before God the Lord;
> Hem boven Al loven, Van herten seer,
praise him above all, with all your heart,
> End' maken groot syns lieven naemens eere,
and make great the honour of his dear name,
> Die daer nu onsen vyand slaet ter neer.
who now strikes our enemy down.

**Verse 2**
> Ter eeren, ons Heeren, Wilt al u dagen,
To honour our Lord, all your days
> Dit wonder, bysonder, gedencken toch.
be sure to remember this wonder above all.
> Maeckt u o mensch! voor God steets wel te dragen,
Take care, O man, always to behave well before God;
> Doet yder recht, en wacht u voor bedrog.
do right by everyone, and guard against deceit.

**Verse 3**
> D'arg-losen, Den boosen, om yet te vinden,
The Evil One, out to catch the unsuspecting,
> Loopt driesschen, En briesschen gelyck een Leeu,
runs raging and roaring like a lion,
> Soeckende wie hy wreedelyck verslinden,
looking for someone he might cruelly devour
> Of geven mocht een doodelycke preeu.
or give a deadly bite.

**Verse 4**
> Bid, waket, End' maket, Dat g'in bekoring,
Pray, watch, and see that you do not fall
> End' 'tquade, Met schade, Toch niet en valt.
into temptation and into evil, to your harm.
> V vroomheyt brengt de vyand tot verstoring,
Your goodness brings the enemy to ruin,
> Alwaer syn ryck noch eens soo sterck bewalt.
were his kingdom walled twice as strong.

### The full words

Theodore Baker's three verses, as The Beacon Hymnal printed them in 1924, with its spelling [^beacon1924]. Its dash after "name," is printed here as a comma. We sing verses 1 and 2.

**Verse 1**
We gather together to ask the Lord's blessing,
He chastens and hastens his will to make known;
The wicked oppressing now cease from distressing,
Sing praises to his name, he forgets not his own.

**Verse 2**
Beside us to guide us, our God with us joining,
Ordaining, maintaining his kingdom divine,
So from the beginning the fight we were winning;
Thou, Lord, wast at our side, all glory be thine!

**Verse 3**
We all do extol thee, thou Leader triumphant,
And pray that thou still our Defender wilt be.
Let thy congregation escape tribulation:
Thy name be ever prais'd! O Lord, make us free!

## How it is sung

In the United States it is sung on and around Thanksgiving Day [^hymnary-text][^komter1942][^ncca1935]. Its tune is KREMSER, named after the Viennese arranger, though the melody is the old Dutch tune in Valerius's book [^hymnary-kremser][^komter1942]. Its meter is 12.11.12.11: the first line of each verse has twelve syllables, the second eleven [^hymnary-kremser][^harvard1920].

The Beacon Hymnal of 1924 prints it as No. 17, under "Opening of Service", headed "Old Netherlands Melody, 1625", the words "Anon.", and marked "Sing in unison" [^beacon1924]. The same four parts appear in The Abingdon Hymnal of 1928 as "Arr. by E. Kremser", with a note that the first two verses be sung in unison, men and women taking turns if they like, and the last "in full harmony" [^abingdon1928].

![[4]]

Our arrangement sings verses 1 and 2 of Baker's English, in D major and 3/4 with a one-beat pickup, on the four parts The Beacon Hymnal prints, adapted for 2 to 4 singers, in harmony throughout. The soprano sings the old Dutch tune in Kremser's form; the harmony parts sing one note to a syllable, and breaths are written in [^beacon1924].

## Versions and other words

Kremser's German by Josef Weyl, "Wir treten zum Beten vor Gott, den Gerechten", is known in Germany and Austria as the Niederländisches Dankgebet [^komter1942][^kropfl]. It was handed out widely in the war that began in 1914 and sung in thanks after the armistice, the 1942 editors write [^komter1942], and in Germany it was sung at state occasions; the Nazi regime used it too, at its rally in Vienna on 9 April 1938, the eve of the vote on the annexation of Austria, where it was sung after Hitler's speech [^kropfl][^otz1938]. Cory's hymn of 1902, "We praise thee, O God, our Redeemer, Creator", was printed in Hymns of the Living Church in 1910 [^hymnary-cory][^blb-cory].

## Did you know?

- The same book of 1626 holds the Wilhelmus, and the tune sung today comes from it [^litgesch][^neerlandistiek]. In 1932 the Wilhelmus became the official Dutch national anthem [^litgesch][^neerlandistiek].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/we-gather-together/soprano): you carry the old Dutch tune, its lilt in the long-short pairs of "gath-er" and "chas-tens". Line 2 climbs to a quick top E at the end of "hastens" ("maintaining" in verse 2), and the tune keeps two notes on one word in "cease" (A, B) and "name" (A, G). Each verse ends on your low D: "his own", and the held "thine!".
- [Alto](/songs/we-gather-together/alto): you start a third under the tune, F-sharp and G under "We gather". In line 3 you hold F-sharp through "-pressing now cease" while the tune moves above you, and line 4 starts on your low A for "Sing". You end above the tune, on F-sharp over its low D, for "own" and "thine!".
- [Tenor](/songs/we-gather-together/tenor): you sing most of the first two lines on one note, A, with a G-sharp on "hastens" and "make" to tune carefully. Your top C-sharp comes on "The" before "wicked" and on "from dis-"; line 4 drops to your low D on the first syllable of "praises" and holds E for "name".
- [Bass](/songs/we-gather-together/bass): you start on D for "We gather togeth-", then move E, F-sharp, E and up to A on "Lord's". Line 2 ends high, on A for "known", and line 3 walks back down from it a step at a time, A, G, F-sharp, E, under "The wicked oppressing now cease". Your lowest note, G, comes on "to" in "Sing praises to his name".

## Questions

### Who wrote We Gather Together?

Several hands made it. The Dutch song is in Adrianus Valerius's Nederlandtsche Gedenck-clanck of 1626, and scholars think he wrote it himself, though some hymnals list the Dutch words as anonymous [^valerius1626][^tenhaeff1942][^hymnary-text]. Eduard Kremser of Vienna arranged the tune in 1877, hence its name, KREMSER [^komter1942][^hymnary-kremser]. Theodore Baker wrote the English words, published in New York in 1894 [^loc1894][^ccr1922].

### Why is We Gather Together sung at Thanksgiving?

Because it was a song of thanks from the start. Valerius's book places it after the Dutch days of thanksgiving for the victories of 1597, Kremser called it a Dankgebet, a prayer of thanks, and American choirs sang it as A Prayer of Thanksgiving [^valerius1626][^hanslick1878][^hudsonfulton1910]. In 1935 a Methodist review called it a hymn for the Thanksgiving season [^ncca1935].

### What does "He chastens and hastens His will to make known" mean?

It means that God corrects his people and is quick to show them what he wants. To "chasten" is to discipline, the word the King James Bible uses for a father correcting the child he loves [^kjv-heb12]. "Hastens" means he hurries to make his will known. The line comes from Baker's English of 1894, not from the Dutch [^harvard1920][^valerius1626].
