Eliyahu HaNavi, "Elijah the Prophet", is a short Hebrew refrain. It is sung on Saturday night, once the Sabbath is over and Havdalah has been made, and at the Passover seder, when the door is opened for Elijah.[^ejhav][^jehab][^milken][^merwin] It calls the prophet by three names: Elijah the prophet, Elijah the Tishbite, Elijah of Gilead.[^idel1929][^sefard] The prophet Malachi promised that Hashem would send Elijah before the great day to come, and the Talmud teaches that Elijah will not come on a Friday, nor on the Sabbath itself. Saturday night is the first moment he might.[^malachi][^eruvin][^mb295] The scholar A. Z. Idelsohn called this refrain the oldest part of the Elijah songs that, he wrote, Jewish communities already sang at the close of the Sabbath in the eleventh century.[^idel1929][^milken]

## Where it comes from

Elijah was a prophet of the northern kingdom of Israel in the ninth century BCE, in the days of King Ahab.[^jeelijah][^milken] The Book of Kings brings him in suddenly, without even his father's name, as "Elijah the Tishbite, who was of the settlers of Gilead", announcing a drought.[^kings][^jeelijah] He fought the worship of Baal, and, as Kings tells it, he did not die: a chariot of fire and horses of fire parted him from his disciple Elisha, and he went up to heaven in a whirlwind.[^kings][^jeelijah][^milken] The prophet Malachi then promised that Hashem would send Elijah before the great and terrible day, to turn the hearts of parents to their children and of children to their parents.[^malachi][^jeelijah]

From that promise grew the Elijah of Jewish tradition: the herald of the Messiah, the unseen guest at a circumcision and at the seder, and a protector who might turn up anywhere.[^jeelijah][^milken] The Talmud, in a discussion of a vow tied to the Messiah's coming, says that Elijah will not arrive on a Friday, because people would leave their Sabbath preparations to go out and greet him. It takes for granted that he cannot come on the Sabbath either.[^eruvin][^jehab] Books of Jewish custom, Sefer ha-Manhig and Abudarham, record the legend that follows: Elijah is expected on Saturday night, at the beginning of a new week.[^jehab][^abrahams] The law code called the Tur gives the reason for the custom: now that the Sabbath is past and he can come, we pray that he will, and bring us the good news.[^mb295][^sa295]

Poets wrote many songs for that moment. Idelsohn, in his history of Jewish music of 1929, wrote that already in the eleventh century songs of Elijah were sung by all Jewish communities at the close of the Sabbath, and that the oldest part of them is this refrain: Elijah the prophet, the Tishbite, the Gileadite, "may he speedily come to us with the Messiah, the son of David."[^idel1929] His footnote points to Sefer ha-Manhig, a book of customs written around 1200.[^idel1929][^jeabn] The Milken Archive's notes are more cautious: by the eleventh century, they say, such songs were the custom in many if not most communities.[^milken] The best known of the songs built around the refrain, as those notes describe it, is a long alphabetical poem by an unknown author. Most of its stanzas begin "Ish", "a man", and name something Elijah did.[^milken][^sefard]

The tune has no known composer. Idelsohn printed it in the same book, in Table XXXII, "Tunes of the Eastern Ashkenazic folk-song", the songs of the Ashkenazi Jews of Eastern Europe. He gave two tunes for these words, "both very popular", and ours is the first.[^idel1929] He wrote that its melodic line, like those of several tunes beside it, resembles Polish, Ukrainian, Spanish, Arabic and old French tunes.[^idel1929] The Milken Archive's notes say its origin remains undetermined, and hear Polish dance rhythms in it: a polonaise pattern and a mazurka's stress on the second beat.[^milken] Idelsohn, who lived from 1882 to 1938, was then professor of Jewish music at Hebrew Union College in Cincinnati.[^ejidel][^loc][^idel1929]

**Myth and record.** Often told: Elijah visits every seder and sips from his cup, while the children watch the wine for a ripple.[^milken][^chabad] What the record shows: there is no Talmudic or midrashic source for a yearly visit, and the Talmud says nothing of a cup for Elijah. The earliest known mention of one is from the fifteenth century.[^zivotofsky][^brodt] Its usual explanation is a question about a fifth cup of wine, which tradition says Elijah will one day settle.[^uh1923][^zivotofsky] The Rema explains the open door differently: it shows trust that the seder is "a night of watching", as Exodus calls it, and for that faith, he writes, the Messiah will come.[^sa480][^zivotofsky]

### Timeline

- 9th century BCE: Elijah prophesies in the northern kingdom of Israel under King Ahab. [^jeelijah][^milken]
- 11th century: by Idelsohn's account, Jewish communities sing songs of Elijah at the close of the Sabbath. [^idel1929][^milken]
- 15th century: the earliest known mention of a cup poured for Elijah at the seder. [^zivotofsky][^brodt]
- 1929: Idelsohn prints this tune in Jewish Music in its Historical Development. [^idel1929]

## What the words mean

We sing only the refrain's first line, the three names, as Idelsohn printed it. His print spells the words the Ashkenazi way, "Eliyohu hanovi" and "hagilodi"; we sing them in modern Israeli pronunciation, syllable for syllable as his notes carry them.[^idel1929]

- **Eliyahu hanavi**: Elijah the prophet.
- **Eliyahu hatishbi**: Elijah the Tishbite. Kings calls him this, probably after a place called Tishbe, whose site is uncertain.[^jeelijah]
- **Eliyahu, Eliyahu, Eliyahu hagiladi**: Elijah, Elijah, Elijah of Gilead, the land east of the Jordan where he lived among its settlers.[^kings][^jeelijah]

In the prayer books the refrain goes on: "bimhera yavo eleinu im mashiach ben David", may he come to us soon with the Messiah, the son of David.[^sefard][^edot] Idelsohn's print has only part of that line, "bimheroh yovo elenu", may he soon come to us, set as a free recitative after the measured first section, and nothing of the longer, measured second half many sing today. We leave the recitative out.[^idel1929] The Milken Archive's notes suggest that carrying the tune on into that second line may be a later addition.[^milken]

### The full words

Here are the refrain and the long poem's ten verses as Siddur Sefard on Sefaria prints them for the meal after the Sabbath, from the public-domain Torat Emet text; the meanings are ours.[^sefard] The refrain follows every verse but the last, and has "Eliyahu" once before "hagiladi", where Idelsohn's tune repeats it three times.[^idel1929][^sefard] The transliteration gives Hashem's names as sung outside prayer: Kel, Adoshem and Elokim.

**Refrain**
> אֵלִיָּהוּ הַנָּבִיא.
*Eliyahu hanavi,*
Elijah the prophet,
> אֵלִיָּהוּ הַתִּשְׁבִּי.
*Eliyahu hatishbi,*
Elijah the Tishbite,
> אֵלִיָּהוּ הַגִּלְעָדִי.
*Eliyahu hagiladi,*
Elijah of Gilead,
> בִּמְהֵרָה יָבֹא אֵלֵינוּ עִם מָשִׁיחַ בֶּן דָּוִד:
*bimhera yavo eleinu im mashiach ben David.*
may he come to us soon with the Messiah, the son of David.

**Verse 1**
> אִישׁ אֲשֶׁר קִנֵּא לְשֵׁם הַקֵּל.
*Ish asher kinei leshem haKel,*
A man who was zealous for the name of Hashem,
> אִישׁ בֻּשַּׂר שָׁלוֹם עַל יַד יְקוּתִיאֵל.
*ish busar shalom al yad Yekutiel,*
a man promised peace by the hand of Yekutiel,
> אִישׁ גָּשׁ וַיְכַפֵּר עַל בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל:
*ish gash vaychaper al b'nei Yisrael.*
a man who stepped forward and atoned for the children of Israel.

**Verse 2**
> אִישׁ דּוֹרוֹת שְׁנֵים עָשָׂר רָאוּ עֵינָיו.
*Ish dorot sh'neim asar ra'u einav,*
A man whose eyes saw twelve generations,
> אִישׁ הַנִּקְרָא בַּעַל שֵׂעָר בְּסִמָּנָיו.
*ish hanikra ba'al se'ar besimanav,*
a man known by his signs as a hairy man,
> אִישׁ וְאֵזוֹר עוֹר אָזוּר בְּמָתְנָיו:
*ish ve'ezor or azur bemotnav.*
a man with a leather belt bound around his waist.

**Verse 3**
> אִישׁ זָעַף עַל עוֹבְדֵי חַמָּנִים.
*Ish za'af al ovdei chamanim,*
A man who raged at the worshippers of sun images,
> אִישׁ חָשׁ וְנִשְׁבַּע מִהְיוֹת גִּשְׁמֵי מְעוֹנִים.
*ish chash venishba mihyot gishmei me'onim,*
a man who hurried to swear that no rain would fall from the skies,
> אִישׁ טַל וּמָטָר עָצַר שָׁלשׁ שָׁנִים:
*ish tal umatar atzar shalosh shanim.*
a man who held back the dew and rain for three years.

**Verse 4**
> אִישׁ יָצָא לִמְצֹא לְנַפְשׁוֹ נַחַת.
*Ish yatza limtzo lenafsho nachat,*
A man who went out to find rest for his soul,
> אִישׁ כִּלְכְּלוּהוּ הָעוֹרְבִים וְלֹא מֵת לַשַּׁחַת.
*ish kilk'luhu ha'orvim velo met lashachat,*
a man whom the ravens fed, who did not die and go down to the pit,
> אִישׁ לְמַעֲנוֹ נִתְבָּרְכוּ כַּד וְצַפַּחַת:
*ish lema'ano nitbarchu kad vetzapachat.*
a man for whose sake a jar and a flask were blessed.

**Verse 5**
> אִישׁ מוּסָרָיו הִקְשִׁיבוּ כְמֵהִים.
*Ish musarav hikshivu chemehim,*
A man whose teaching was heard by those who longed for it,
> אִישׁ נֶעֱנֶה בָאֵשׁ מִשְּׁמֵי גְבוֹהִים.
*ish ne'ena va'esh mishmei g'vohim,*
a man answered with fire from the high heavens,
> אִישׁ סָחוּ אַחֲרָיו יְיָ הוּא הָאֱלֹקִים:
*ish sachu acharav Adoshem hu haElokim.*
a man after whom they said, "Hashem, He is the Almighty."

**Verse 6**
> אִישׁ עָתִיד לְהִשְׁתַּלֵּחַ מִשְּׁמֵי עֲרָבוֹת.
*Ish atid lehishtale'ach mishmei aravot,*
A man who will one day be sent from the highest heavens,
> אִישׁ פָּקִיד עַל כָּל בְּשׁוֹרוֹת טוֹבוֹת.
*ish pakid al kol b'sorot tovot,*
a man put in charge of all good news,
> אִישׁ צִיר נֶאֱמָן לְהָשִׁיב לֵב בָּנִים עַל אָבוֹת:
*ish tzir ne'eman lehashiv lev banim al avot.*
a faithful messenger, to turn the hearts of children to their parents.

**Verse 7**
> אִישׁ קָרָא קַנֹּא קִנֵּאתִי לַייָ בְּתִפְאָרָה.
*Ish kara kano kineti laAdoshem betif'ara,*
A man who cried out, "I have been very zealous for Hashem", in glory,
> אִישׁ רָכַב עַל סוּסֵי אֵשׁ בִּסְעָרָה.
*ish rachav al susei esh bis'ara,*
a man who rode on horses of fire in a storm,
> אִישׁ שֶׁלּא טָעַם טַעַם מִיתָה וּקְבוּרָה:
*ish shelo ta'am ta'am mita ukvura.*
a man who never tasted death or burial.

**Verse 8**
> אִישׁ תִּשְׁבִּי עַל שְׁמוֹ נִקְרָא.
*Ish Tishbi al sh'mo nikra,*
A man called by the name of the Tishbite:
> תַּצְלִיחֵנוּ עַל יָדוֹ בַּתּוֹרָה.
*tatzlicheinu al yado baTorah,*
through him, let us succeed in the Torah,
> תַּשְׁמִיעֵנוּ מִפִּיו בְּשׁוֹרָה טוֹבָה בִּמְהֵרָה.
*tashmi'enu mipiv b'sora tova bimhera,*
let us hear good news from his mouth soon,
> בִּמְהֵרָה תּוֹצִיאֵנוּ מֵאֲפֵלָה לְאוֹרָה:
*bimhera totzi'enu me'afela le'ora.*
soon bring us out of darkness into light.

**Verse 9**
> אַשְׁרֵי מִי שֶׁרָאָה פָנָיו בַּחֲלוֹם.
*Ashrei mi shera'a fanav bachalom,*
Happy is one who has seen his face in a dream,
> אַשְׁרֵי מִי שֶׁנָּתַן לוֹ שָׁלוֹם וְהֶחֱזִיר לוֹ שָׁלוֹם.
*ashrei mi shenatan lo shalom vehechezir lo shalom,*
happy is one who greeted him with peace and whom he greeted with peace in return.
> יְיָ יְבָרֵךְ אֶת עַמּוֹ בַשָּׁלוֹם:
*Adoshem yevarech et amo vashalom.*
May Hashem bless His people with peace.

**Verse 10**
> כַּכָּתוּב הִנֵּה אָנֹכִי שׁלֵחַ לָכֶם אֵת אֵלִיָּה הַנָּבִיא.
*Kakatuv: hine anochi sholeach lachem et Eliya hanavi*
As it is written: "Here, I am sending you Elijah the prophet
> לִפְנֵי בּא יוֹם יְיָ הַגָּדוֹל וְהַנּוֹרָא.
*lifnei bo yom Adoshem hagadol vehanora,*
before the coming of the great and awesome day of Hashem,
> וְהֵשִׁיב לֵב אָבוֹת עַל בָּנִים וְלֵב בָּנִים עַל אֲבוֹתָם:
*veheshiv lev avot al banim velev banim al avotam.*
and he will turn the hearts of parents to their children and the hearts of children to their parents."

## How it is sung

**At Havdalah.** On Saturday night, when the Sabbath is over, Havdalah ("separation") is said over wine, spices and a flame.[^jehab][^abrahams] After it, it is customary to sing hymns, and Eliyahu HaNavi is among the best known, beside Ha-Mavdil.[^ejhav][^jehab] The Rema records the custom of calling on Elijah on Saturday night, praying that he come and announce the redemption.[^sa295][^mb295] In the Edot HaMizrach prayer book, of the Sephardi and Mizrahi rite, some say the poem before Havdalah.[^edot][^jehab] Most people now sing only the refrain, the Milken Archive's notes observe.[^milken]

**At the seder.** After the grace after the meal and the third cup of wine, with Elijah's cup on the table, the door is opened.[^zivotofsky][^milken][^uh1923] Many say the verses beginning "Shefokh chamatkha", "Pour out Your wrath", while it stands open.[^zivotofsky][^sa480] Eliyahu HaNavi is usually sung then, with the door open.[^milken][^merwin]

**Our arrangement.** We read the tune and words from page 401 of Idelsohn's book, a melody alone, Moderato, in E minor, and sing it a minor third higher, in G minor, in 3/4.[^idel1929] We sing the first, measured section, up to the print's Fine, three times through, the third time softly. The four parts are our own. Breaths are written in for beginners at the end of "navi", "tishbi" and "giladi".

## Versions and other words

The long poem's stanzas run through the Hebrew alphabet in their second words and tell Elijah's story, from his zeal for Hashem to his ride on horses of fire without tasting death.[^sefard][^milken] Some versions add a stanza asking him to bring good news "on Saturday nights".[^edot][^milken]

In the Edot HaMizrach prayer book each stanza ends "Eliyahu hanavi zachur latov", Elijah the prophet, remembered for good.[^edot] Idelsohn's second popular tune, printed beside ours and marked "recit.", sets only the first two words, "Eliyohu hanovi", again and again.[^idel1929] The Milken Archive's notes trace Idelsohn's tune to a variant in a German synagogue hymnbook from Braunschweig, which they say has been cited as its earliest known notation. Idelsohn himself names no source and files the tune among Eastern Ashkenazi folk songs. That variant, the notes add, has fewer dotted rhythms than the tune now sung almost everywhere in American Ashkenazi circles.[^milken][^idel1929]

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/eliyahu-hanavi/soprano): the folk tune, most often on G, then B-flat and D. Its long notes on "navi,", "tishbi," and "giladi." fall on the second beat of the bar. Listen for the last "Eliyahu" before "hagiladi.", where the syllable "ya" leaps from G up to the top E-flat, the tune's only visit there each time through.
- [Alto](/songs/eliyahu-hanavi/alto): mostly a third or a sixth under the tune, most often on E-flat, then D. It dips to its low A on "hanavi," and climbs to its top G on "hatishbi,", the first "Eliyahu," and the "Eliyahu" before "hagiladi.". Its two F-sharps, on the second "Eliyahu," and the "ha-" of "hagiladi.", are worth tuning high.
- [Tenor](/songs/eliyahu-hanavi/tenor): the middle of the chord, most often on B-flat, then G. It rises to its top D on the first "Eliyahu," each time through, and ends on its low D, an octave under that, only on the very last "hagiladi.", under the final chord.
- [Bass](/songs/eliyahu-hanavi/bass): the bass line, most often on G, then B-flat. It opens on its low G, and on the "Eliyahu" before "hatishbi," it jumps an octave to its top G; solo it in the player to hear the leap.

## Questions

### Who composed the Eliyahu HaNavi song?

No one knows. The words are a traditional Hebrew refrain whose author is unknown, and the tune is a folk tune. A. Z. Idelsohn printed it in 1929 among the tunes of the Eastern Ashkenazi folk song, and the Milken Archive's notes say its origin is still undetermined, though its dance rhythms sound Polish.[^idel1929][^milken]

### Why is Eliyahu HaNavi sung at Havdalah?

Because Saturday night is when Elijah may come. The Talmud teaches that he will not arrive on a Friday, when people are busy preparing for the Sabbath, nor on the Sabbath itself. So once the Sabbath is over, the custom is to call on him and pray that he come soon with news of the redemption. The Rema, in his gloss to the Shulchan Aruch, records the custom.[^eruvin][^sa295][^mb295][^jehab]

### Why is Eliyahu HaNavi sung at Passover?

Because Elijah is the expected guest of the seder night. After the grace after the meal, with a cup ready for him, the door is opened, and in many homes the refrain is sung while it stands open. Elijah is the herald of the Messiah, so the open door looks forward to the final redemption as the seder looks back to the Exodus.[^milken][^merwin][^zivotofsky][^uh1923]

### What does "Eliyahu hanavi zachur latov" mean?

"Elijah the prophet, remembered for good." Zachur latov is an honour added to Elijah's name: the grace after meals asks Hashem to send "Elijah the prophet, remembered for good", with good tidings, and the Edot HaMizrach prayer book ends each stanza of the Saturday-night poem with the same words. Because in Jewish tradition Elijah records people's deeds, as the Jewish Encyclopedia notes, some read it as a pun: mazkir latov, "who records for good".[^birkat][^sinclair][^edot][^jeelijah]

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune throughout, as Idelsohn printed it, a minor third higher. The alto sings with it, mostly a third or a sixth below. With three singers the bass joins, and with four the tenor fills the middle of each chord.[^idel1929]
