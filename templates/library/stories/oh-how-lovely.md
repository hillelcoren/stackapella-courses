Oh, How Lovely Is the Evening, also printed "O How Lovely Is the Evening" and "Lovely Evening" [^dann1916], is a short round about evening bells: three lines sung by voices that start one after another, the last of them just "Ding, dong" on one low note [^emc1896]. It is the English form of a German round, "O wie wohl ist mir am Abend", which means "Oh, how good I feel in the evening" [^fliedner1842][^liederprojekt]. The earliest printing we found is in a German songbook for small children of 1842, where the children mimed pulling the bell ropes at its "bim, bam, bom!" [^fliedner1842]. An English version was printed in Canada in 1869, and the words we sing were taught to a New York hall of working people in 1892 [^sefton1869][^harpers1892].

## Where it comes from

**A bell song for small children.** The earliest printing we found is No. 54 in a songbook for infant schools and the lower classes of elementary schools, edited by Theodor Fliedner, pastor at Kaiserswerth on the Rhine; its preface is dated 18 June 1842 [^fliedner1842]. On page 29 it is one line of tune, in F and in 3/8, with no composer's name and no marks for where voices come in. Bracketed words turn it into a midday song: "Mittag", midday, for "Abend", and "zum Tisch", to the table, for "zur Ruh", to rest. The next song, No. 55, is "Bruder Jacob", the German "Frère Jacques", with a school bell of its own, and a game in the book, "Das Läuten", the ringing, has the children mime pulling the bells at "Bim! Bam! Bom!" in either song [^fliedner1842].

**Whose round?** The 1842 book names no maker [^fliedner1842]. Forty years later, Liederlust und Psalter, a German-language songbook printed in Cincinnati in 1882, put it fourth of "Vier Canons", four canons, signed simply "Schulz." [^liebhart1882]. In 1889 the German music writer Wilhelm Tappert printed its opening as an unsigned "Canon", and right after it a canon by "K. Schulz" that begins with nearly the same rising notes: "Ich bin fröhlich, willst du mit mir fröhlich sein?", I am happy, will you be happy with me? [^tappert1889] Today the Lieder-Projekt of Carus and SWR2 calls "O wie wohl ist mir am Abend" a folk song after that canon, "Ich bin fröhlich", by Carl Friedrich Schulz (1784 to 1850) [^liederprojekt]. Karl or Carl Schulz was a teacher who ended his career in Fürstenwalde: a music dictionary of 1878 says he taught at the teachers' seminary at Neuzelle and later headed the Fürstenwalde Gymnasium, while a songbook of 1882 says he died there as the former deputy head of its town school [^mendel1878][^erk1882]. He wrote a singing method for village schools, in print by 1816 [^mendel1878][^musikverket]. Whether he made the round, or the round was made from his canon, the printings we read do not say.

![[1]]

**Myth and record.** Sometimes told: the round is by Johann Abraham Peter Schulz (1747 to 1800), a better-known composer of the generation before. The large German encyclopedia Der Große Brockhaus, in its volume of 1933, called it a four-part canon by J. A. Schulz, dated 1782 [^brockhaus1933][^erk1882][^mendel1878]. A German folk-song website today credits "Chr. Schulz" instead, with the year 1820 [^volkslieder]. What the record shows: the earliest printing we found, from 1842, names no one; a Schulz is named in the printings we read only from 1882; and every printing we read that marks the voices sets it for three, not four, Brockhaus's own music example included [^fliedner1842][^liebhart1882][^sefton1869][^emc1896][^brockhaus1933].

**Into English.** An English novelist knew it, in German, by 1851. In Elizabeth Gaskell's story "Mr. Harrison's Confessions", serialised in The Ladies' Companion in 1851, the young surgeon who tells the story is at a picnic at an old hall with a boat on its moat when, as the sun gets low, three sisters on the lawn strike up "the little German canon", and the story quotes its first line [^duffy][^chadwick1910][^gaskell1855][^godey1852]. The earliest English version we found is in Three-Part Songs for the pupils of Canada's public schools, by Henry Francis Sefton, music teacher at the Normal and Model Schools of Ontario, and printed in Toronto in 1869 [^sefton1869]. On page 41, "Oh, How Lovely! (Round for Three Voices.)" is in 6/8 and in G, its three lines numbered for the voices: "Oh how lovely is the evening, is the evening, When to rest the bells are ringing, bells are ringing!", and for the third line, six times on one note, "Bome!" [^sefton1869].

![[2]]

**A round for a whole hall.** On Sunday 23 October 1892, at Cooper Union in New York, Frank Damrosch gave the first lesson of his People's Singing Classes, meant, as Harper's Weekly put it, for "the working-people, who could not afford to have music made for them"; 2,500 came [^nyt1892][^dispatch1892][^harpers1892]. The magazine reports that after explaining notes on a blackboard he taught them a short tune, printed there as "how lonely is the evening, When the bells are sweetly ringing", a misprint for "lovely". Then he split the hall into three groups, they sang it as a round, and it "went without a hitch" [^harpers1892].

![[3]]

**The book we sing from.** The Educational Music Course was a series of school music readers by Luther Whiting Mason, James M. McLaughlin and George A. Veazie, school music supervisors in and around Boston, and W. W. Gilchrist, with poems by Nathan Haskell Dole; Ginn & Company copyrighted it in 1896 [^emc1896]. Page 22 of its First Reader prints "Round." in three lines marked I, II and III, in F and in 3/4: "O how lovely is the evening", "When the bells are sweetly ringing", and "Ding, dong" six times on one F [^emc1896]. No author is named for it; the publishers say that the book's poems, except those selected, were composed, translated or adapted by Dole, and these words were already being sung in New York four years before [^emc1896][^harpers1892].

## What the words mean

1. **"O how lovely is the evening, is the evening."** The singer is glad the day is over. The German is warmer and more personal: "O wie wohl ist mir am Abend" is "Oh, how good I feel in the evening", "wohl" being well, at ease [^fliedner1842].
2. **"When the bells are sweetly ringing, sweetly ringing."** In the German they ring "zur Ruh", to rest; the English of 1869 kept that, "When to rest the bells are ringing" [^fliedner1842][^sefton1869].
3. **"Ding, dong, ding, dong, ding, dong!"** The bell itself. Each book has its own sound for it: "bim, bam, bom!" in 1842, "Bome!" in 1869, "bum, bum, bum, bum" in 1882, "Ding, dong" in 1896 [^fliedner1842][^sefton1869][^liebhart1882][^emc1896].

### The German round

The evening words of No. 54 as printed at Kaiserswerth in 1842, with our own plain English under each line [^fliedner1842].

> O wie wohl ist mir am Abend, mir am Abend,
Oh, how good I feel in the evening, in the evening,
> Wenn zur Ruh die Glocke läutet, Glocke läutet:
when the bell rings for rest, the bell rings:
> bim, bam, bom! bim, bam, bom!
bim, bam, bom! bim, bam, bom!

### The full words

These are the words as printed on page 22 of The Educational Music Course, First Reader (Boston, copyright 1896). No author is named, and the book was printed before 1931 by makers who all died by 1935, so the words are in the public domain.

O how lovely is the evening, is the evening,
When the bells are sweetly ringing, sweetly ringing:
Ding, dong, ding, dong, ding, dong!

## How it is sung

**A round in three.** Every voice sings the same tune, starting one line apart. The 1896 page stacks its three lines and marks them I, II and III; Sefton's page of 1869 numbers them 1, 2, 3 at the left and 2, 3, 1 at the right, showing where each singer goes next [^emc1896][^sefton1869]. Stacked, the lines also show the harmony: the second line runs a third above the first, and the third holds the keynote, so when all three sound, they make a chord [^emc1896].

**What our arrangement sings.** Our round is read from the 1896 page, in 3/4, a whole tone lower, in E-flat instead of F, for 2 to 4 singers. The soprano starts alone and sings the tune three times through. The alto, tenor and bass come in six bars apart, the tenor and bass an octave lower, and the bass finishes alone on the last "Ding, dong".

## Did you know?

- In Thomas Mann's novel Doktor Faustus (1947; in English 1949), the boy Adrian Leverkühn, who grows up to be a composer, learns rounds from Hanne, the stable girl at his family's farm, and "O wie wohl ist mir am Abend" is the first round named [^dnb1947][^mann1949].
- In Wandernde Melodien, "wandering melodies" (1889), Wilhelm Tappert put the round's opening in a family of tunes that includes a 14th-century Prague processional, "Ubi est spes mea?", and Haydn's Austrian anthem of 1797 [^tappert1889]. A Berlin scholar, Oskar Fleischer, traced the same group to an old psalm tone in 1901 to 1902 [^fleischer1902].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/oh-how-lovely/soprano): you start the round alone and sing it three times through, so everyone hears it first. You go up to the top C on the "sweet-" of each "sweetly", and down to the low E-flat on "O", at the end of "lovely" and of each "evening", and for the whole "Ding, dong" line. Keep those six long notes steady, ringing like a bell under the others.
- [Alto](/songs/oh-how-lovely/alto): the same tune at the same pitch, six bars after the soprano, coming in just as she starts "When the bells are sweetly ringing". Your "O how lovely" sits a third below her line, so hold your own tune and listen for the chord.
- [Tenor](/songs/oh-how-lovely/tenor): the same tune an octave lower, six bars after the alto, coming in as the soprano reaches "Ding, dong". From your entry all three lines sound at once: count the "Ding, dong" notes, three beats each.
- [Bass](/songs/oh-how-lovely/bass): the tenor's low tune, coming in last, six bars after it: the soprano's line an octave below her. You finish alone on the last "Ding, dong", so the round ends on the bell.

## Questions

### What are the German lyrics of Oh, How Lovely Is the Evening?

The German round begins "O wie wohl ist mir am Abend", oh, how good I feel in the evening. As printed in 1842 it goes on "Wenn zur Ruh die Glocke läutet", when the bell rings for rest, and ends with the bell itself, "bim, bam, bom!" [^fliedner1842]. A German book printed in Cincinnati in 1882 has the bells in the plural, "die Glocken läuten", and rings them "bum, bum, bum, bum" [^liebhart1882].

### Who wrote Oh, How Lovely Is the Evening?

No one knows for certain. The earliest printing we found, from 1842, names no composer, and our English words of 1896 name no author [^fliedner1842][^emc1896]. From 1882 some books credit "Schulz", but the sources do not agree on which Schulz, or on whether he made it [^liebhart1882][^liederprojekt][^brockhaus1933].

### Which part sings the tune?

Everyone does. In a round there is no separate harmony part: the three lines of the one tune fit together as harmony when they sound at once [^emc1896]. In our arrangement the soprano and alto sing it at the same pitch, and the tenor and bass sing it an octave lower.
