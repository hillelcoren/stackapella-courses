Hey, Ho, Nobody Home, also known as Hey Ho, Nobody at Home or Heigh Ho, Nobody at Home, is a short English round: one five-bar tune that singers begin a bar apart, so that it fits against itself [^pammelia1609][^naylor1905]. Its earliest known printing is No. 85 in Pammelia, a London book of rounds and catches of 1609, credited to Thomas Ravenscroft, where it is set for five voices and ends "fill the pot Eadie" [^pammelia1609][^grove-pammelia]. The book names no composers, and who made the words or the tune is not known [^grove-pammelia][^lindsey1929]. A London comedy printed in 1613 has a penniless old man sing it instead of eating supper [^kbp1613][^lindsey1929]. The ending many know today, "yet will I be merry", is in print by 1905 [^naylor1905].

## Where it comes from

**The book of 1609.** Pammelia's title page promises "Mixed Varietie of Pleasant Roundelayes, and delightfull Catches, of 3. 4. 5. 6. 7. 8. 9. 10. Parts in one", printed in London by William Barley in 1609 [^pammelia1609]. No compiler is printed on it (a later hand has written Ravenscroft's name at the top of this copy), but music historians have long given it to Thomas Ravenscroft and count it the earliest printed collection of rounds and catches in England; the Dictionary of National Biography, more cautiously, says it "is said to be" [^grove-pammelia][^grove-round][^dnb-ravenscroft]. The dictionaries disagree on Ravenscroft's birth, about 1582 or about 1592, and say he died about 1635, or by some accounts 1630 [^grove-ravenscroft][^dnb-ravenscroft]. The same year he put out a second book, Deuteromelia, with a version of Three Blind Mice [^deuteromelia1609][^grove-ravenscroft][^dnb-ravenscroft].

![[1]]

Its preface, "To the well disposed to reade, and to the merry disposed to sing", says music had kept "this more familiar mirth and iocund melodie" to itself, "neuer (as yet) publikely communicating" it [^pammelia1609]. It also admits that some of its songs are old: "what seemes old, is at least renewed, Art hauing reformed what pleasing tunes iniurious time and ignorance had deformed" [^pammelia1609]. A study of 1929 agrees that most of the book's pieces were already old in 1609, and that Ravenscroft collected rather than composed most of them [^lindsey1929].

**The page.** The round is No. 85, on page 41, under the running head "Canons in the vnison. 5 Vo.": a canon at the unison for five voices [^pammelia1609][^grove-round]. Two staves of diamond-shaped notes, some hollow and some filled, carry the tune. A large woodcut capital H begins the words, so the words under the two staves read "Ey ho no body at home, meate nor drinke nor money haue I none, fill the pot Eadie. Hey vt supra." [^pammelia1609]. A small sign over the third note marks where the next voice comes in, a bar after the first [^pammelia1609][^naylor1905]. It sits among the book's sixteen songs of five voices, a few numbers after Latin canons such as Verbum Domini manet (No. 82) and just before a round that begins on the sol-fa syllables, Vt, re, mi, fa, sol, la; the book runs to 100 pieces [^pammelia1609][^grove-pammelia].

![[2]]

**On the stage.** The Knight of the Burning Pestle, a London comedy, was printed in 1613 with no author's name; a printing of 1635 names Francis Beaumont and John Fletcher [^kbp1613][^kbp1635]. An editor of 1898 gives Beaumont the chief share, and the 1929 study calls it Beaumont's play [^moorman1898][^lindsey1929]. Its publisher, Walter Burre, wrote that the public had rejected it, missing "the priuy marke of Ironie about it" [^kbp1613]. The same editor dated its writing to 1610 or 1611, from Burre's remark that he had kept it "these two yeares"; the 1929 study put it in 1607 or 1608 [^kbp1613][^moorman1898][^lindsey1929]. In the fourth act Old Merrythought, who has spent everything, says he does not know how to come by "meate to supper, yet am I merry still" [^kbp1613]. When a boy brings word that there is no bread, he answers "Hang bread and supper" and calls for a catch: "Ho, ho, no body at home, meate, nor drinke, nor money ha wee none, fill the pot Eedy, neuer more need I" [^kbp1613]. The 1898 editor and the 1929 study both name Pammelia as the source of its music [^moorman1898][^lindsey1929].

![[3]]

**Myth and record.** Often told: Hey, Ho, Nobody Home is an English folk song of the 16th century [^musicforall]. What the record shows: its earliest known printing is from 1609 [^pammelia1609]. The earliest 16th-century date we found is in E. W. Naylor's An Elizabethan Virginal Book of 1905, which heads the round "Date probably early 16th century" and gives no evidence for it [^naylor1905]. The tune may be older than the book, as its preface allows, but we found no earlier copy [^pammelia1609].

### Timeline

- 1609: Pammelia is printed in London by William Barley, with Hey ho no body at home as No. 85 [^pammelia1609][^grove-pammelia]
- 1613: The Knight of the Burning Pestle is printed, with Merrythought's catch "Ho, ho, no body at home" [^kbp1613][^lindsey1929]
- 1618: A second edition of Pammelia keeps it as No. 85 [^pammelia1618][^grove-pammelia]
- 1905: E. W. Naylor prints the tune with the ending "Yet will I be merry!" [^naylor1905]
- 1929: A study of the play's music links its catch to the Pammelia tune [^lindsey1929]

## What the words mean

1. **"Hey ho, nobody at home."** "Heigh-ho" is a cry of weariness, or of joy or surprise [^webster-heighho]. The house is empty. In the play the singer's wife and sons have gone he knows not where [^kbp1613].
2. **"Meat nor drink nor money have I none."** "Meat" here means food of any kind [^webster-meat]. The singer has no food, no drink and no money.
3. **"Fill the pot, Eadie."** A pot was an earthen or pewter cup for drink, and a pot of ale was what it held [^webster-pot]. Eadie is someone asked to fill it; the play spells the name "Eedy", and no source we read says who Eadie is [^pammelia1609][^kbp1613].
4. **"Hey vt supra."** The page ends with these words: "supra" is Latin for "above", so each singer goes back to "Hey" and begins again [^webster-supra][^pammelia1609].

So the song is a cheerful complaint. In the play it is sung by a man who would rather keep his mirth than eat, and a 1929 study heard in the tune a "merry disorder" [^kbp1613][^lindsey1929]. The later ending, "Yet will I be merry", says outright what the old words leave to the singer [^naylor1905].

### The full words

These are the words of No. 85 as printed on page 41 of Pammelia (1609), with the book's spelling; the first letter is the woodcut capital H, and the line breaks are ours. On the 1609 copy we read, an ink blot covers the "nor" before "money"; the 1618 edition prints it clearly. The book is anonymous and was printed in 1609, so the words are in the public domain.

Hey ho no body at home,
meate nor drinke nor money haue I none,
fill the pot Eadie.

## How it is sung

**A round, or catch.** A round is a canon at the unison: the singers begin the same tune one after another, and each goes back from the end to the beginning [^grove-round]. Early writers on music used "round" and "catch" for the same thing, and plays of the time are full of "alehouse catches" [^grove-round]. A catch is "a humorous canon or round" [^webster-catch]. Pammelia sets this one for five voices, and Naylor explains the plan: five bars and five voices, each starting a bar after the last [^pammelia1609][^naylor1905]. Writers have heard it differently: Naylor read it as Aeolian, an old minor mode, resting on two alternating chords, while the 1929 study called it major, and heard it sung in the play by three voices, Merrythought and two boys [^naylor1905][^lindsey1929]. As our arranger reads the page, the tune is minor [^pammelia1609].

**What our arrangement sings.** Our round is read from a facsimile of the 1609 page (a scan of the 1971 Da Capo Press reprint, also on IMSLP), written in F minor, a minor third above the print, in 4/4 [^pbm-pammelia][^imslp]. The words are the 1609 ones in today's spelling. It is for 2 to 4 singers: the soprano starts alone, the alto comes in a bar later at the same pitch, then the tenor and the bass, each a bar after the last, an octave lower. Each part sings the tune three times, our choice. The parts finish one by one, and the bass ends alone.

## Versions and other words

- **The play's words (1613).** "Ho, ho" for "Hey ho", "money ha wee none" for "money haue I none", "Eedy" for "Eadie", and a last line, "neuer more need I" [^kbp1613].
- **The second edition (1618).** Printed by Thomas Snodham for Mathew Lownes and John Browne, it lists the round again as "Hey hoe no body", No. 85 [^pammelia1618].
- **"Yet will I be merry" (by 1905).** Naylor prints the same tune, in A, as "Heigh-ho, nobody at home! Meat nor drink nor money have I none: Yet will I be merry!" He gives no source for the new last line [^naylor1905].

## Did you know?

- Merrythought's first song in the same scene, "Who can sing a merrier noate, Then he that cannot change a groat?", is close to a line in Pammelia's last round, No. 100: "who can sing so merry a note, as he that cannot change a groat" [^kbp1613][^pammelia1609][^moorman1898].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/hey-ho-nobody-home/soprano): you start alone and carry the tune three times, so everyone hears it first. "Hey" and "ho" are long notes; the "home" of "nobody at home" falls to middle C, your lowest note; and the last line, "fill the pot, Eadie", sits on your top note, C. Hold the last "Eadie".
- [Alto](/songs/hey-ho-nobody-home/alto): the same tune at the same pitch, a bar after the soprano: nothing new to learn. The hard part is keeping your place: as you sing "Hey", the soprano is already on "nobody at home".
- [Tenor](/songs/hey-ho-nobody-home/tenor): the same tune an octave lower, from the C below middle C up to middle C, a bar after the alto. As you start, the soprano is on "meat nor drink nor" and the alto on "nobody at home".
- [Bass](/songs/hey-ho-nobody-home/bass): the tenor's low tune, coming in last, a bar after it; from your entry, four of the tune's five bars sound at once. You finish alone, holding the last "Eadie" after the tenor stops.

## Questions

### What does Hey, Ho, Nobody Home mean?

It is a cheerful complaint: the singer's house is empty, and there is no food, no drink and no money, so the singer calls for the pot to be filled [^pammelia1609][^webster-meat][^webster-pot]. "Hey ho" is a cry of weariness [^webster-heighho]. In a London comedy printed in 1613 it is sung by a man who has spent everything and would rather keep his mirth than eat [^kbp1613]. The later ending, "Yet will I be merry", says the same thing plainly [^naylor1905].

### Is Hey, Ho, Nobody Home from the 16th century?

No one has shown that it is. Its earliest known printing is from 1609, in Pammelia [^pammelia1609][^grove-pammelia]. In 1905 E. W. Naylor dated it "probably early 16th century", giving no evidence, and a music school's teaching page still gives a 16th-century date [^naylor1905][^musicforall]. The 1609 preface admits some of its tunes were old, but we found no earlier copy [^pammelia1609].

### Is Hey, Ho, Nobody Home a Christmas song?

Not by its words or its early history. Its 1609 words name no season, and in 1613 it is sung by a hungry, penniless man calling for drink [^pammelia1609][^kbp1613]. A New York music school's page says it was once linked with Christmas carol singing, but it gives no source, and we found none [^musicforall].

### How do you sing Hey, Ho, Nobody Home as a round?

Everyone sings the same five-bar tune, each voice starting a bar after the one before, and at the end each goes back to the start [^naylor1905][^pammelia1609][^grove-round]. The 1609 page is set for five voices [^pammelia1609]. In our arrangement, for 2 to 4 singers, the soprano starts, then the alto, tenor and bass come in a bar apart, the tenor and bass an octave lower; each part sings it three times, and the bass finishes alone.
