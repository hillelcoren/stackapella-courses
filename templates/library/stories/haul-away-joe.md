Haul Away, Joe, also printed as "Haul Away, Jo" and "Way, Haul Away", is a sea shanty: a work song sailors sang to pull together on a rope [^whall][^fssj]. The shantyman sings a line about his girls or his voyage, and the crew answers "Way, haul away, haul away, Joe", all hauling at once on the last word [^sharpscan][^smith]. It served for a few short, heavy pulls, on the tacks and sheets that hold down the corners of the sails, or to sweat a rope up tight [^sharp][^bullen][^whall][^smyth]. The tune we sing is the one John Short, an old sailor of Watchet in Somerset, sang to Cecil Sharp in 1914 [^sharpscan][^sharppeople].

## Where it comes from

**The earliest printing we found.** In July 1882 Harper's New Monthly Magazine in New York printed "Sailor Songs", by William L. Alden, with music [^alden][^fssj]. After "the Bowline" he printed "another pulling song of almost equal popularity in old days": the solo "Way, haul away, haul away, my Josie", and the chorus "Way, haul away, haul away, Joe". Both, he wrote, were "undoubtedly very old" [^alden]. In 1914 a note in the Folk-Song Society's Journal, signed A. G. G., reprinted his tune, called it dorian, and judged it "one of our oldest chanty-tunes" [^fssj].

![[1]]

**Two old sailors in England.** On 24 October 1908 Cecil Sharp noted the shanty from Charles Robbins, aged 65, at the Marylebone Workhouse in London; it was printed in January 1914 [^fssj][^sharp]. In April 1914 he began visiting John Short at Watchet, a Somerset harbour town [^sharppeople][^halsway]. Short, born in 1839, had spent more than fifty years in sailing ships, mostly as a chanteyman, the solo singer who led the shanties, and was now the town crier [^sharp][^sharppeople][^halsway]. Of the 60 shanties in Sharp's *English Folk-Chanteys*, published that year, 46 are his; Haul Away, Joe is No. XXVII, on page 32 [^sharp][^halsway][^sharpscan].

![[2]]

**The page we read.** Sharp printed the tune with a piano part, in 4/4, with three flats and written-in A-naturals that make it dorian, a minor-sounding mode with a raised sixth. The chorus comes first, then the shantyman's two lines and the crew's answer, with an arrowhead over each "Joe" to mark the pull. Ten more verses follow, and a footnote explains that "Aver" means Havre, the French port [^sharpscan]. In his note Sharp wrote that Short called it a "tacks and sheets" chantey [^sharp].

![[3]]

**Major or minor.** The books disagree. Sharp notes major versions in Tozer, Bullen and Whall, and Robbins sang it in the mixolydian mode [^sharp][^fssj]. Richard Terry, who printed a minor version in 1921, wrote that the major one was more common in the last days of sail, but the minor one was what he heard on the Tyne, the only one his oldest sailor relatives sang, so he took it to be the older [^terry].

**Where shanties came from.** Black sailors and dockworkers shaped the shanty: a Library of Congress folklorist writes that the most valued singer on board was often a Black sailor [^winick]. Alden traced many sailor songs to Black workers stowing cotton in Southern ports, and Short told Sharp that one of his shanties was sung while screwing cotton into the hold at New Orleans [^alden][^sharp]. This tune's roots were argued over in 1914. Frank Bullen wrote that most shanty tunes came from Black singers of the Caribbean and the American South, and his music editor, W. F. Arnold, held that most shanties were Black in origin. Bullen first heard his "Mudder Dinah" from Black workers heaving at a winch as they unloaded cargo in the Demerara River, off Georgetown [^bullen]. Sharp could not agree with Arnold, though he granted that Black singers had left their mark on a number of shanty tunes. He accepted that the words of "Mudder Dinah" were Black, but heard its tune as a close variant of Haul Away, Joe, a tune he found free of Black influence [^sharp]. Alden, in 1882, had called this shanty and the Bowline purely nautical in origin [^alden]. Steam ended the shanty's age: by the end of the 1870s, Terry wrote, it had driven the sailing ship from the seas [^alden][^terry].

## What the words mean

Each verse is two lines from the shantyman, and the crew answers once. The words were not fixed: shantymen made up their lines as they went, and Terry called this shanty's verses endless [^sharp][^terry].

**The chorus.** To haul, says *The Sailor's Word-Book* of 1867, is to pull on a single rope by hand [^smyth]. Short sings "my Rosie" where Alden's sailors sang "my Josie" [^sharpscan][^alden]. None of the printings says who Joe was.

**Verses 1 and 2.** A boast about girls in port: the girls of Havre and Sally round the corner are no match for the girls in Booble Alley [^sharpscan]. Sharp does not say where Booble Alley was, and we found no early source that does.

**Verses 3 to 5.** A run of sweethearts. Sharp's verse 3 uses a racist slur for a Black girl, and we do not print it. In verse 4 the girl is "all a posy", as pretty as a bunch of flowers [^sharpscan].

**Verses 6 to 11.** A voyage to the East Indies and home. To square the yards is to set them across the ship to run before the wind [^smyth]. Then the song turns to the job in hand, "up aloft this yard must go", and ends with "another pull and then belay": make the rope fast round a pin or cleat [^smyth]. The song stops when the work does.

### The full words

These are the words of No. XXVII in Cecil Sharp's *English Folk-Chanteys* (1914), page 32, as John Short sang them, with Sharp's spelling and numbering, and the crew's answer written out after each verse [^sharpscan]. Sharp's verse 3 uses a racist slur, and we leave it out. We keep Sharp's numbers, so the verses run 1, 2, then 4 to 11. Sharp wrote that he only cut a few lines and softened two or three expressions [^sharp]. The words are traditional, printed in 1914, and Sharp died in 1924, so they are in the public domain [^sharppeople][^hymnary].

**Chorus**
Haul away, haul away, haul away, my Rosie,
Way, haul away, haul away, Joe.

**Verse 1**
O you talk about your Aver girls,
And round the corner Sally;
Way, haul away, haul away, Joe.

**Verse 2**
But they cannot come to tea
With the girls in Booble Alley.
Way, haul away, haul away, Joe.

**Verse 4**
O! once I had a nice young girl,
And she was all a posy.
Way, haul away, haul away, Joe.

**Verse 5**
And now I've got an English girl,
I treat her like a lady.
Way, haul away, haul away, Joe.

**Verse 6**
We sailed away for the East Indies,
With spirits light and gay.
Way, haul away, haul away, Joe.

**Verse 7**
We discharged our cargo there, my boys.
And we took it light and easy.
Way, haul away, haul away, Joe.

**Verse 8**
We loaded for our homeward bound,
With the winds so free and easy.
Way, haul away, haul away, Joe.

**Verse 9**
We squared our yards and away we ran,
With the music playing freely.
Way, haul away, haul away, Joe.

**Verse 10**
Now, up aloft this yard must go,
We'll pull her free and easy.
Way, haul away, haul away, Joe.

**Verse 11**
Another pull and then belay,
We'll make it all so easy.
Way, haul away, haul away, Joe.

## How it is sung

It is a pulling shanty with a single pull where most have a double one, and a longer refrain between verses; Sharp's arrowheads put the pull on "Joe", and Smith wrote in 1888 that all hands pull there [^sharp][^sharpscan][^smith]. The books differ a little on the job.

- **Tacks and sheets.** Short called it a "tacks and sheets" chantey, and Bullen put it with the fore sheet chanties, sung while hauling that great rope "with one collective drag" [^sharp][^bullen]. A sheet holds down a lower corner of a sail, and a tack the windward corner of the big lower sails [^smyth].
- **Sweating up.** Whall gives it as "a last short pull for sweating up", the final hard pulls that bring a rope home, and Terry prints it among his "fore-sheet or sweating-up" shanties [^whall][^terry]. A Library of Congress folklorist calls it one of the best-known short-drag shanties [^winick].

At sea, Terry adds, the last note was not held but cut short, more like a grunt as the men pulled [^terry]. Sharp asked for chanteys to be sung slowly and mostly unaccompanied [^sharp].

Our arrangement is read from Sharp's page, a semitone up in C-sharp dorian, on the harmony of his piano part, so the harmony is Sharp's, not the ships' [^sharpscan]. It is the chorus, verses 1, 2 and 4, and the chorus again, for 2 to 4 singers: the soprano is the shantyman and sings each verse alone, and everyone sings the choruses and every answer, landing together on "Joe", where Sharp marks the pull.

## Versions and other words

- **Other verses.** Whall and Terry add a joke about King Louis of France losing his head in the Revolution, which the Traditional Ballad Index (Roud 809) thinks a later addition; Terry has a verse about Geordie Charlton's pig; and Smith, Whall and Terry run through an Irish girl, a Spanish girl, a Dover lass [^whall][^terry][^balladindex][^smith].
- **A racist verse.** Smith, Whall, Sharp, Terry and Robbins all have a verse about a Black girl that uses a racist slur, its words different in each book, and Terry wrote that it and the King Louis verse never seemed to be left out [^smith][^whall][^sharpscan][^fssj][^terry]. We do not print it.
- **Other words for the chorus.** Robbins sang the answer as "O away, audle away, O audle away Joe" [^fssj]. Bullen and Arnold's fore sheet version gives the shantyman a line about a girl called Kitty, and the crew still lands on "Joe" [^bullen].

## Did you know?

- John Short in Watchet and Charles Robbins in London sang nearly the same voyage verses: Robbins's 4 to 9 are Short's 6 to 11, though Robbins sang "our minds so free and easy" where Short sang "the winds" [^fssj][^sharpscan].
- In *Moby-Dick* (1851), as the Pequod weighs anchor, the men at the windlass roar "some sort of a chorus about the girls in Booble Alley". Melville does not name the song, and it was windlass work, not a short haul [^melville][^melvilletext].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/haul-away-joe/soprano): you are the shantyman. Start each verse on the top E ("O", "But", "O!") and sing both lines alone, then lead the crew's answer. Every "Joe" drops to the low C-sharp, an octave under the "Way" that begins the answer. The A-sharp, on "my" before "Rosie" and in each verse's lines ("Aver", "corner", "Booble"), is the dorian note.
- [Alto](/songs/haul-away-joe/alto): you rest through the shantyman's lines and come in on every "Way, haul away, haul away, Joe", mostly on E, with D-sharp and B, and you open each chorus on G-sharp on "Haul".
- [Tenor](/songs/haul-away-joe/tenor): you sit in the middle of the chord, mostly on G-sharp, with B and D-sharp. In each verse's answer you drop to D-sharp for "-way, haul away" and come back to G-sharp on "Joe"; in the choruses you hold up on B instead.
- [Bass](/songs/haul-away-joe/bass): you sing the roots, mostly C-sharp, with B and E, down to a low G-sharp on the chorus's last "away" and up to G-sharp on "Rosie". Land every "Joe" on C-sharp with everyone else: that is the pull.

## Questions

### What does Haul Away, Joe mean?

It is a work song for pulling a rope: "haul away" means pull, and the crew pulled on "Joe" [^smyth][^sharpscan][^smith]. In John Short's words the shantyman boasts of girls in port, then tells a voyage to the East Indies and home, ending with "another pull and then belay", when the rope is made fast [^sharpscan][^smyth]. None of the old printings says who Joe was.

### Who wrote Haul Away, Joe?

No one knows: it is traditional, and no early source names a maker. The earliest printing we found is in Harper's New Monthly Magazine in July 1882, where William L. Alden gave its solo line and chorus with a tune and called it very old [^alden]. The verses and dorian tune we sing are John Short's, as Cecil Sharp printed them in 1914 [^sharpscan].

### Where does Haul Away, Joe come from?

It comes from the sailing ships, sung by British and American crews, and no record says where or when it began. The earliest printing we found is William L. Alden's, in Harper's New Monthly Magazine, New York, July 1882; he called it "undoubtedly very old" and purely nautical [^alden]. Cecil Sharp noted it from two old English sailors, Charles Robbins in London in 1908 and John Short of Watchet, whose version he printed in 1914 [^fssj][^sharpscan]. In 1914 Bullen and Arnold traced most shanty tunes to Black singers; Sharp heard this one as free of that influence [^bullen][^sharp].

### What genre is Haul Away, Joe?

It is a sea shanty, and of a particular kind: a pulling shanty for a few short, heavy pulls, one to each verse, rather than for steady work at the capstan or the pumps [^sharp][^alden]. John Short called it a "tacks and sheets" chantey, Bullen's book puts it on the fore sheet, and Whall gives it for sweating up [^sharp][^bullen][^whall].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune. In our arrangement the soprano is the shantyman, singing each verse alone and leading both choruses; the alto, tenor and bass join every "Way, haul away, haul away, Joe" on the harmony of Sharp's 1914 piano part [^sharpscan].
