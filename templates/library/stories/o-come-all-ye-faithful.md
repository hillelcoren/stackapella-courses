O Come, All Ye Faithful is the English form of Adeste Fideles, a Latin Christmas hymn of the eighteenth century. Its earliest known copies, words and tune together, are in the hand of John Francis Wade, an English Catholic who earned his living copying church music [^hymnarchive][^zon]. Whether he wrote it or only copied it is not known [^hymnarchive][^zon][^america]. By the 1790s it was sung at the Portuguese embassy chapel in London, and in 1841 an Anglican priest, Frederick Oakeley, put it into English for his London congregation [^julian][^hymnarchive]. By 1907 Julian's Dictionary of Hymnology found Oakeley's English in "a great number of collections" [^julian].

## Where it comes from

**The copyist.** Wade's obituary, printed in the Catholic Laity's Directory, called him "Mr. John Francis Wade, a Layman, aged 75"; he died in 1786 [^zon][^hymnarchive][^canterbury]. He made his living copying plainchant, the unaccompanied chant of the Latin liturgy, for Catholic chapels, among them the London embassy chapels [^hymnarchive][^zon][^canterbury]. Many accounts place him, and his death, at the English College in Douai, in France [^canterbury][^phh]. A 1996 study of his manuscripts finds that they point to England instead, perhaps London itself [^zon][^hymnarchive].

**The copies.** Adeste Fideles appears, in four Latin verses with its tune, in several of Wade's books [^hymnarchive][^phh]. Probably the oldest, a copy described by Dom John Stéphan of Buckfast Abbey in a 1947 study and now lost, dates from the mid-1740s: about 1743 or 1744 in one account of Stéphan's dating, around 1745 in the 1996 study [^hcnotes][^zon]. The Irish musicologist W. H. Grattan Flood dated a copy at Clongowes Wood College in Ireland to 1746; James Britten, writing in 1924, said the manuscript itself showed 1749. It is now lost [^hymnarchive][^britten][^america]. Two surviving copies are dated 1750, one of them at the University of Glasgow, and one at Stonyhurst College is dated 1751 [^hymnarchive][^britten][^zon].

**The tune.** In Wade's earliest copies the tune moves in threes. The even, four-beat tune sung today is first found in print in 1782, in An Essay on the Church Plain Chant, published in London by J. P. Coghlan [^zon][^phh][^hymnarchive]. Who prepared the Essay is disputed: Stéphan and others name Samuel Webbe, an organist of the London embassy chapels, while the editors of The New Oxford Book of Carols (1992) call it anonymous [^hcnotes][^phh][^hymnarchive]. The Latin words were printed earlier, in The Evening Office of the Church of 1760, a prayer book in Latin and English [^hymnarchive][^hymnary-latin].

![[1]]

**The Portuguese Hymn.** Webbe was also organist of the Portuguese embassy chapel, where the hymn was sung by the 1790s, so in England the tune was long known as the Portuguese Hymn [^cath-webbe][^julian][^hymnarchive][^america]. Vincent Novello, a later organist there, credited the tune to John Reading, a seventeenth-century organist of Winchester Cathedral; scholars set that aside, since the tune is not in Reading's known music and no copy is older than the 1740s [^julian][^hymnarchive][^britten]. The 1996 study has also suggested Thomas Arne, whom it places at the Sardinian embassy chapel, granting that the idea may seem implausible [^zon][^hymnarchive].

**The English.** Frederick Oakeley was the Anglican minister of Margaret Chapel in Margaret Street, London [^hymnary-oakeley][^canterbury-o]. He translated Wade's four verses for his congregation there in 1841, beginning "Ye faithful, approach ye" [^julian][^hymnarchive][^canterbury-o]. Julian says Oakeley never published it and that it first appeared in print in Francis H. Murray's A Hymnal for Use in the English Church of 1852; Hymnology Archive finds it in a Leeds collection, Sacred Hymns and Anthems, as early as 1846 [^julian][^hymnarchive]. Murray's book gave it the first line everyone knows, "O come, all ye faithful" [^julian][^hymnarchive][^praise].

**Myth and record.** Often told: the carol is a coded Jacobite song, a birth ode to Bonnie Prince Charlie, the exiled Stuart heir, in which "Bethlehem" means England, the faithful are the Jacobites, and "regem angelorum", king of angels, hides "regem Anglorum", king of the English. What the record shows: this is a scholar's theory, first set out in the 1996 study [^zon][^durham]. Its evidence is where the hymn sits. In some books it stands beside a prayer for the king, naming the exiled James Stuart in one and "Carolus" in another, and the study finds Jacobite emblems in Wade's printed books [^zon][^hymnarchive][^durham]. The study itself notes that "Carolus" could be Charles Edward Stuart or Charles Emmanuel, King of Sardinia, and a printed Evening Office leaves the king's name blank [^zon][^hymnarchive]. The study concluded that Jacobitism alone does not explain the hymn; quoted by Durham University in 2022, its author put the case more firmly [^zon][^durham].

### Timeline

- 1740s: Wade's earliest known copies of the hymn, words and tune together, are made. [^zon][^hymnarchive][^hcnotes]
- 1750: Wade signs and dates a copy now at the University of Glasgow, among the earliest dated copies that survive. [^hymnarchive][^britten][^zon]
- 1760: The Latin words are printed in The Evening Office of the Church, in four verses. [^hymnarchive][^hymnary-latin]
- 1782: The tune is printed in London, in An Essay on the Church Plain Chant, in even four-beat time. [^zon][^phh][^hymnarchive]
- 1841: Frederick Oakeley makes his English version for Margaret Chapel, London. [^julian][^hymnarchive][^canterbury-o]
- 1852: Murray's hymnal prints it with the opening line "O come, all ye faithful". [^julian][^hymnarchive]

## What the words mean

Wade's Latin has four verses. Later writers added four more, so the full Latin has eight; English hymnals sing Wade's four, which are the first, second, seventh and eighth of the eight [^julian][^hymnary-latin]. Oakeley translated those four [^canterbury-o][^hymnarchive]. We sing the first verse and the refrain in the form most hymnals print: Murray's opening line of 1852, with the rest close to Oakeley [^hymnarchive][^praise].

**Verse 1, the one we sing.** The Latin opens "Adeste fideles", be here, or draw near, you faithful [^hymnarchive][^julian]. "Ye" is the old plural "you". "Joyful and triumphant" means glad and exulting. "O come ye to Bethlehem" puts the singers beside the shepherds of Luke's Gospel, who said to one another, "Let us now go even unto Bethlehem" [^kjv-luke]. "Behold" means look at; the Latin here, "natum videte regem angelorum", means "see him born, the king of angels". The refrain, "O come, let us adore him", is the Latin "venite adoremus": come, let us worship him. It ends on "Christ the Lord", the angel's own words to the shepherds in Luke 2:11 [^kjv-luke].

**Verse 2 (not sung here).** "God of God, Light of Light" borrows the Nicene Creed almost word for word: the child is God from God, light from light, "begotten, not created", that is, born of the Father and not made [^hymnarchive][^creed]. "Abhors not the Virgin's womb" means he did not shrink from being born of Mary.

**Verses 3 and 4 (not sung here).** In "Sing, choirs of angels" the angels sing "Glory to God in the highest", as in Luke 2:14 [^kjv-luke][^phh]. "Yea, Lord, we greet thee" greets the child born "this happy morning", the Word of the Father "now in flesh appearing": John's Gospel says "the Word was made flesh" [^kjv-john][^phh]. Some hymnals mark this verse "On Christmas Day" [^neh].

### The Latin

The four Latin verses English hymnals sing are given here as Julian's Dictionary of Hymnology prints them in 1907, from an undated prayer book printed at Mechlin that has all eight; they are its first, second, seventh and eighth [^julian]. Printings vary in small readings: Julian thought "Io", a shout of joy, in place of "hymnos" in the seventh verse probably the original [^julian]. Below each Latin line is our own plain English.

### The full words

These are Frederick Oakeley's words of 1841, with the changes Murray's hymnal made in 1852, as Hymns of Worship and Service (New York, 1909) prints them at No. 89, the page our arrangement is read from; it spells "Oh" where we sing "O", and sings the refrain after each verse [^hws1909][^julian]. That book prints three verses and leaves out Oakeley's second, "God of God", so that verse is not from the 1909 book: it is given here as The English Hymnal printed it in 1906, at No. 28 [^eh1906]. Fuller hymnals add W. T. Brooke's English of the four later Latin verses, from 1884 [^hymnary-latin][^hymnarchive].

## How it is sung

Adeste Fideles began in Catholic Latin worship. The 1760 Evening Office sets it for Benediction at Christmas, and Julian says it was sung so in England and France by the late eighteenth century [^hymnarchive][^julian]. Oakeley's English brought it into Anglican churches, and Catholic collections took up the English too [^julian]. Its tune is called ADESTE FIDELES, and its meter is irregular, with a refrain, and the English does not rhyme [^hymnary-text][^phh]. One hymnal handbook suggests the verses in unison and the refrain in parts [^phh].

Our arrangement sings the first verse and the refrain, in G and in 4/4, for 2 to 4 singers, with everyone singing the words. Two singers take soprano and alto; a third adds bass. The refrain says "O come, let us adore him" three times, each "come" higher than the last, on G, then B, then C, before "Christ the Lord" brings it home to G.

## Versions and other words

- **The added Latin verses:** three by the French priest Étienne Borderies, printed in 1822, about the shepherds, the Father's glory and the poor child in the manger, which French use sings in place of three of Wade's; and an anonymous one about the wise men [^hymnarchive][^hcnotes][^julian].
- **All eight in English:** W. T. Brooke translated the four added verses for The Altar Hymnal of 1884, which printed them with Oakeley's for a full English text [^hymnary-latin][^hymnarchive].
- **The English Hymnal (1906):** four verses at No. 28, and a complete version at No. 614 [^eh1906].

## Did you know?

- The priest who brought this Catholic hymn into Anglican churches became a Catholic himself. Frederick Oakeley was received into the Roman Catholic Church in 1845 and became a canon in Westminster [^hymnary-oakeley][^america][^phh].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/o-come-all-ye-faithful/soprano): the tune, from low D to high D. "Bethlehem" sinks to the low D and holds it, then "Come and behold him" opens on the high D: take a full breath between. Each "come" in the refrain lands higher, on G, B, then C, so save some voice.
- [Alto](/songs/o-come-all-ye-faithful/alto): mostly a third under the tune; "joyful and triumphant" is the clearest stretch. On "Bethlehem" it touches C-sharp, its one note outside the key, then holds F-sharp while the tune drops to its low D: for those beats the alto is the top voice.
- [Tenor](/songs/o-come-all-ye-faithful/tenor): mostly G, A and D. "Joyful and triumphant" is almost all one D, and "Bethlehem" ends on an A held for nearly five beats. In the second "O come, let us adore him" it stays mostly on G under the tune's B and C: keep it steady. It ends on its lowest note, a long D under "Lord".
- [Bass](/songs/o-come-all-ye-faithful/bass): exactly one octave, low G to high G. "Come and behold him" starts on the high G and walks down to C. In the refrain it leaps from the low G on "him" up to the high G on the next "O come", and later drops back. It ends on the low G.

## Questions

### What does "Adeste Fideles" mean?

"Adeste fideles" is Latin for "be here, you faithful", or "draw near, you faithful" [^hymnarchive][^julian]. The whole first line, "Adeste, fideles, laeti triumphantes", means "be here, you faithful, glad and exulting" [^julian]. English hymnals sing "O come, all ye faithful, joyful and triumphant": Frederick Oakeley's English of 1841, with the first line Murray's hymnal gave it in 1852[^julian][^hymnarchive].

### What Bible verse is O Come, All Ye Faithful based on?

It draws on the Christmas story in Luke 2. The call to Bethlehem echoes the shepherds' "Let us now go even unto Bethlehem" (Luke 2:15), and "Christ the Lord" is the angel's phrase in Luke 2:11 [^kjv-luke]. The third verse has the angels' song of Luke 2:14, and the fourth echoes John 1:14 [^kjv-john][^phh]. The second verse quotes the Nicene Creed, not the Bible [^hymnarchive][^creed].

### What are the original lyrics of O Come, All Ye Faithful?

The original is Latin, Adeste Fideles, in four verses known first from John Francis Wade's copies of the 1740s on, and printed in 1760 [^hymnarchive][^zon][^hymnary-latin]. The English most people sing is Frederick Oakeley's of 1841, which began "Ye faithful, approach ye"; "O come, all ye faithful" replaced that line in 1852 [^julian][^hymnarchive].

### Is there a Catholic version of O Come, All Ye Faithful?

The hymn is Catholic to begin with: a Latin hymn copied by a Catholic layman and sung at Benediction at Christmas [^hymnarchive][^julian]. The English came from Oakeley, then an Anglican priest, and by 1907 Julian found it in several Roman Catholic collections [^julian]. Catholic and Protestant hymnals both print Oakeley's English; fuller ones add later verses about the shepherds and the Magi [^julian][^eh1906][^hymnarchive].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune in our arrangement. With two singers you have the tune and the alto, mostly a third below it; with three, add the bass. The tune runs from low D to high D, an octave.
