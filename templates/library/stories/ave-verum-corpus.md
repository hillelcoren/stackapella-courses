Ave verum corpus, "Hail, true body", is a short Latin prayer to the body of Christ in the bread of the Mass, made in the Middle Ages to be said or sung when the priest lifts up the host [^ah54][^mone][^nce]. Many composers have set it, and the most famous setting is Mozart's motet, K. 618, which he dated at the spa town of Baden near Vienna on 17 June 1791, the last year of his life [^ah54][^nma][^autograph][^koechel1862]. It runs only 46 bars, all of it marked to be sung softly, sotto voce, "under the voice" [^nma]. We sing Mozart's own four voice parts, in Latin, for four singers.

## Where it comes from

**The prayer.** No one knows who wrote the words. The oldest dated copy is in a Franciscan book of prayers of 1293, now in the Laurentian Library in Florence, where it is headed as another prayer "to the host" [^ah54][^bandini][^biblissima]. It belongs to the elevation, the moment after the consecration when the priest raises the host for the people to see, a custom that began in the 12th century [^cdh][^nce]. The hymnologist Mone, reading its manuscripts in 1853, found that it was sung in Italy, France and Germany after the Preface or during the consecration [^mone]. Most early copies are in prayer books for private devotion, some with promises of indulgences for saying it at the elevation; others are in the choir's graduals and missals [^ah54]. Hymnologists date it to the 13th or early 14th century [^cdh][^nce], and the editors of the Analecta Hymnica, the great collection of medieval hymns, placed its birth, most likely, in northern Italy [^ah54].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Pope Innocent VI wrote it. What the record shows: a 14th-century manuscript from the abbey of Reichenau says only that "Pope Innocent composed the following salutation" ("Salutationem sequentem composuit Innocentius papa"), and adds that the prayer carries three years of indulgence from a Pope Leo [^mone][^julian][^ah54]. Some reference works name Innocent VI, one of them saying the Reichenau manuscript does, but Mone's and the Analecta Hymnica's readings of it give no number [^cdh][^nce][^mone][^ah54]. Innocent VI reigned from 1352 to 1362, after the prayer was already being copied, so he cannot have written it [^cdh][^nce]. The Analecta Hymnica's editors pointed instead to a story that Innocent IV, a pope from Genoa who died in 1254, wrote it, since, they wrote, the oldest copy was made by a Franciscan in Genoa about forty years after his death; they report the story without proving it [^ah54][^ce].

**Mozart in Baden.** In the summer of 1791 Mozart's wife Constanze was taking the waters at Baden [^jahn][^mozstoll]. There Mozart's friend and admirer Anton Stoll was the schoolteacher and choirmaster, "Regens Chori", and the two traded favours: late in May Mozart wrote asking Stoll to find Constanze ground-floor rooms near the baths, and to send him, with all its parts, one of his Masses that Stoll had for his choir, promising to return it soon [^mozstoll][^jahn]. The motet was probably written for Stoll, who kept its manuscript [^koechel1862][^jahn][^sothebys][^mozstoll].

**The manuscript.** Mozart's score is two leaves, four written pages, now in the Austrian National Library in Vienna [^koechel1862][^omi][^nma]. Its first page carries his heading in Italian, "di Wolfgango Amadeo Mozart mp" (manu propria, in his own hand), "Baaden li 17 di giunnio 1791", as Köchel transcribed it in 1862, over the title "Ave verum Corpus" and the tempo Adagio [^autograph][^koechel1862]. The staves run two violins, viola, then "Canto", "Alto", "Tenore" and "Basso", and last organ and bass with figures for the organist, and each voice has "sotto voce" written at its entry [^autograph]. In his own catalogue of his works, Mozart entered it a day later, "Baaden, 18ten Juny", as No. 139, for soprano, alto, tenor and bass, two violins, viola, organ and bass [^cat1805][^nma][^koechel1862]. In 1860 the manuscript belonged to Joseph Schellhammer, a retired school director in Graz, whose name, "Ex rebus Joseph Schellhammer", is written at the foot of the first page [^koechel1862][^autograph].

![[1]]

**Into print.** The earliest known printing is Johann André's in Offenbach am Main, undated but placed in 1808: a score with a keyboard part beneath it, plate 2478, price 30 kreuzer, its title "Komponirt in Baaden den 18ten Junius 1791" following the catalogue's date [^andre1808][^sothebys]. A notice inside, in German and French, says the piece can be used at the consecration in the Mass, that Mozart composed it for that purpose, and that by his own thematic catalogue it is one of his last works [^andre1808]. Ludwig von Köchel gave it the number 618 in his catalogue of Mozart's works in 1862, and Breitkopf & Härtel printed it in Mozart's collected works in 1880, the edition we read [^koechel1862][^mw1880].

## What the words mean

The prayer greets the host as the true body of Jesus, the same body that was born of Mary and died on the cross, and asks that, in the testing of death, it be a foretaste for us. "Ave" is a greeting, "hail", and "immolatum" means offered up as a sacrifice [^ls]. "Verum" means true. "Cujus latus perforatum" recalls the soldier's spear that opened Jesus's side, from which blood and water flowed, in John's Gospel [^vulgate]. "Praegustatum" is something tasted beforehand, a foretaste [^ls]. "Examen" can mean the tongue of a balance, and so a weighing or testing; "in mortis examine" is in the trial, or the testing, of death [^ls].

Mozart's words differ a little from the oldest copies. He sings "Ave" twice, says "de Maria Virgine" for "ex Maria virgine", and "unda fluxit et sanguine", water flowed and blood, where the oldest copy has "vero fluxit sanguine", flowed with true blood; Mone found "unda fluxit et" in a prayer book from Languedoc and a breviary from Langres [^nma][^ah54][^mone]. William Byrd's setting of 1605 also has "unda" [^byrd1605]. The oldest copy, a private prayer book, also says "esto mihi", be for me, where Mozart has "esto nobis", be for us, as the choir books do, and "mortis in examine" for his "in mortis examine". Mozart ends there, leaving out the prayer's last lines [^nma][^ah54].

### The lines Mozart left out

The prayer closes with short calls to Jesus as Mary's son, echoing the end of the Salve Regina, a prayer to Mary, with the roles turned around [^nce][^mone]. This is the ending as the Analecta Hymnica prints it; some copies add "Jesu" and a plea, "miserere mei", have mercy on me [^ah54].

> O dulcis, o pie,
O sweet one, O loving one,
> O fili Mariae.
O son of Mary.

### The full words

These are the words as Mozart set them and as we sing them from the 1880 edition, the same as the Neue Mozart-Ausgabe prints them, with his repeat of the last line. The words are medieval and Mozart died in 1791, so all of it is in the public domain. The English is our own.

> Ave, ave,
Hail, hail,
> verum corpus,
true body,
> natum de Maria virgine,
born of the Virgin Mary,
> vere passum immolatum
that truly suffered and was sacrificed
> in cruce pro homine.
on the cross for humankind.

> Cujus latus perforatum
Whose pierced side
> unda fluxit et sanguine,
ran with water and blood,
> esto nobis praegustatum
be for us a foretaste
> in mortis examine,
in the test of death,
> in mortis examine.
in the test of death.

## How it is sung

The prayer was made for the elevation of the host [^ah54][^mone], a later Catholic prayer book gives it as a private prayer "sub elevatione" [^julian], and André offered Mozart's motet for the same moment [^andre1808]. A modern Catholic reference work calls the prayer no longer in liturgical use [^nce].

Mozart's motet is in D major, Adagio, alla breve, two beats to the bar [^nma][^autograph]. The strings begin alone, play a short interlude before "Cujus latus" and end alone [^nma]. Our arrangement is read from the 1880 edition, in D, with Mozart's four voice parts and the Latin words, written here in quarter-note beats with breaths for beginners [^mw1880]. The strings' introduction and ending are left out and their interlude becomes a bar's rest. It is for four singers only, because Mozart's chords need all four voices.

## Versions and other words

William Byrd, the English royal organist, printed his Ave verum corpus in his Gradualia, published in London by Thomas East in 1605, nearly two centuries before Mozart [^byrd1605]. Edward Elgar, then organist of St George's Catholic church in Worcester, wrote a Pie Jesu in 1887 for the funeral of William Allen, the solicitor he had worked for at fifteen; in 1902 he reworked it as an Ave verum corpus, and Novello published it as his Op. 2 No. 1, in its series Cantiones Sacrae, No. 36, dedicated "In Memoriam W. A." [^kennedy][^imslp-elgar]. Many other composers set the words too [^nce]. In English, Julian's Dictionary of Hymnology lists several translations sung in churches, among them one beginning "Hail, true Body, born of Mary" [^julian].

## Did you know?

- William Byrd's setting of 1605 goes on past where Mozart stops, through "O dulcis, o pie" to "O Jesu fili Mariae, miserere mei", the plea some old copies add [^byrd1605][^ah54].
- André's first edition prints the choir's parts in the old soprano, alto and tenor clefs, and adds a keyboard part, labelled "Cembalo", under the score [^andre1808].
- Copies of the prayer vary: the Analecta Hymnica lists manuscripts that add lines of their own, one of them a Franciscan missal with lines on Christ offered on the altar under the veil of bread and tasted in the consecrated cup [^ah54].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/ave-verum-corpus/soprano): you sing Mozart's smooth, slow tune, mostly between E and B. The first "A-ve" leaps from A up to D and falls to F-sharp. On "in cru-ce" you hold a high D for five beats. Near the end you sing "in" alone, then hold a D while the others enter under you and climb to your one top E, on the last "mor-tis".
- [Alto](/songs/ave-verum-corpus/alto): you sing the Latin close under the tune, mostly on E and D, with F-sharp and C-sharp. Through "Cu-jus la-tus per-fo-ra-" you stay on one E for thirteen beats while the tune moves above you, and your lowest note, a B, comes on "no-bis".
- [Tenor](/songs/ave-verum-corpus/tenor): you sing in the middle of the chords, mostly on A and C-sharp. From "ve-rum cor-pus" to "vir-gi-ne" you stay on a single A, so tune it well. You reach E on "cru-ce" and "flu-xit", and on "san-gui-ne" you drop to your low E.
- [Bass](/songs/ave-verum-corpus/bass): you sing Mozart's bass line, mostly D and A. Under "un-da flu-xit et san-gui-ne" you start on F, walk down E, D, C-sharp to A, then sing B-flat, A, your low G-sharp and A. At "Es-to no-bis" you and the tenor wait a bar and enter after the soprano and alto. Your highest note, B, comes on "pas-sum" and the first "in mor-tis".

## Questions

### What does Ave verum corpus mean in English?

"Ave verum corpus" means "Hail, true body": it greets the host at Mass as the real body of Jesus [^ah54][^ls]. Word by word, Mozart's text says: hail, true body, born of the Virgin Mary, that truly suffered and was sacrificed on the cross for humankind, whose pierced side ran with water and blood; be for us a foretaste in the test of death [^nma][^vulgate]. The full Latin with our English is above.

### Who wrote Ave verum corpus?

The words are anonymous: a medieval Latin prayer whose oldest dated copy is from 1293, later credited to a "Pope Innocent" without proof; Innocent VI, the one usually named, reigned after it was already being copied [^ah54][^mone][^cdh][^nce]. The most famous music for it is Mozart's, K. 618, dated at Baden on 17 June 1791, probably for his friend Anton Stoll, the town's choirmaster [^autograph][^nma][^koechel1862][^mozstoll]. William Byrd's setting was printed in London in 1605, and Edward Elgar's, Op. 2 No. 1, in 1902 [^byrd1605][^imslp-elgar][^kennedy]. Ours is Mozart's.

### When is Ave verum corpus sung?

It was made for the elevation, when the priest lifts the host at Mass, and Mozart's first publisher offered the motet for that moment [^ah54][^andre1808]. Mozart dated his score six days before the feast of Corpus Christi, which in 1791 fell on Thursday 23 June, but names no occasion [^eastertable][^feasts][^autograph][^nma]. The Catholic Church's chant book, the Liber Usualis, printed it in 1961 among the chants at Benediction of the Blessed Sacrament [^liber1961]. A modern Catholic reference calls the prayer no longer in liturgical use, but Elgar's setting, by one account, has kept its place in the choral repertory [^nce][^kennedy].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings Mozart's tune, mostly between E and B, rising to a top E only once, on the last "mortis". The alto moves close beneath it, the tenor holds the middle of the chords, and the bass sings Mozart's bass line, so all four are needed for his harmony [^mw1880][^nma].
