Steal Away, also sung as Steal Away to Jesus, is an African American spiritual, one of the religious songs made by enslaved people. Its refrain urges the singer to slip away quietly to Jesus, because "I hain't got long to stay here" [^seward1872]. Its earliest known printing is on page 28 of Jubilee Songs (1872), the book of Fisk University's touring student singers, whose introduction says none of its songs had been published before, as far as the singers knew [^seward1872]. A tradition from the Choctaw Nation, in what is now Oklahoma, credits it to Wallace Willis [^flickinger1914][^okhist]. The Fisk singers opened their first London concert with it in May 1873, and sang it for Queen Victoria the next day [^pike1875][^marsh1875][^natarchives].

## Where it comes from

**The singers and their book.** On 6 October 1871 George L. White, the treasurer of Fisk University in Nashville, set out with a small company of students to raise money for the school, deep in debt [^marsh1875][^fisk]. In 1872 Biglow & Main of New York published 24 of their songs as Jubilee Songs: White took the words down "from their lips", and Theodore F. Seward wrote down the music [^seward1872]. The book's introduction says the singers learned most of the songs as children and received the others "directly from those who were accustomed to sing them"; seven of the nine, it says, had been enslaved [^seward1872]. A footnote admits that two of the songs, not Steal Away, had in fact been printed before [^seward1872]. Steal Away is not in the earlier Slave Songs of the United States (1867), made by three white collectors [^slavesongs1867].

**What the 1872 page shows.** "Steal Away." heads page 28, in F major and 2/4 time, for four voices on two staves [^seward1872]. It opens softly, with a pause over each "away". "Steal away home" is marked loud, with a quick triplet, and "I haint got long to stay here" falls back to soft. The page before holds "Many thousand Gone", whose first line is "No more auction block for me" [^seward1872].

**Wallace and Minerva Willis.** The 1872 book names no maker for any song [^seward1872]. The fullest early account we have read is R. E. Flickinger's history of the Choctaw freedmen (1914). Before the Civil War, it says, "Uncle Wallace and Aunt Minerva" worked at Spencer Academy, a Choctaw school run by the Reverend Alexander Reid [^flickinger1914][^okhist]. On summer evenings they sang at their cabin door songs learned "in Mississippi in their early youth". In 1871, it goes on, Reid heard the Fisk singers in Newark, New Jersey, wrote down about half a dozen of the Willis songs and rehearsed them with the singers; "Steal away to Jesus" was one [^flickinger1914][^okhist]. The Oklahoma Historical Society's encyclopedia says instead that Wallace made the songs himself, in the fields, and reports family research finding that Minerva was his daughter, not his wife [^okhist]. Frances Banks, a Choctaw freedwoman interviewed at 82, said her grandfather, Uncle Wallace, "made up" Swing Low and Steal Away, and "a Mr. Reid" helped him keep them [^banks]. So the accounts agree on Reid, but not on whether Wallace made the song or learned it.

**In Britain.** The singers sailed for Britain in 1873 [^natarchives]. Their first London concert, at Willis's Rooms on 6 May, began with Steal Away. G. D. Pike quotes a London music paper: "It was sung slowly", soft chords, then the words in unison, then "the touching, throbbing cadence" of "I hain't got long to stay here" [^pike1875]. The next day, at Argyll Lodge in Kensington, the Duke of Argyll asked them to sing it for Queen Victoria; the Lord's Prayer and Go Down, Moses followed [^pike1875][^marsh1875]. Later that year, Pike writes, the evangelist Dwight L. Moody heard it at a revival meeting in Newcastle, and told Pike "they stole his heart" [^pike1875].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Nat Turner, who led a revolt of enslaved people in Southampton County, Virginia, in August 1831, used Steal Away to call secret meetings; or the song signalled an escape on the Underground Railroad [^yale1992][^carnegiefolk][^bordewich]. Also told: Queen Victoria asked for it [^okhist][^natarchives]. What the record shows: Turner's confession, taken down by Thomas R. Gray and printed in 1831, mentions no song [^turner1831][^encva]. The early accounts present a religious song, and the Willis tradition ties it to Wallace Willis and the Choctaw Nation, far from Turner's Virginia [^seward1872][^flickinger1914][^okhist]. A historian of the Underground Railroad names Steal Away among songs said to have signalled escape and finds no evidence that any song was used that way [^bordewich]. Pike, who was in the room, wrote that the Duke asked for it, not the Queen [^pike1875]. A second meaning in spirituals is documented, though: Frederick Douglass wrote in 1855 that when he and his friends sang of Canaan, "the north was our Canaan" [^douglass1855]. A singer could hear freedom in this song too; that it was a code has no evidence behind it.

## What the words mean

- **"Steal away".** To steal away is to slip off quietly, as Laban says Jacob did in the King James Bible: "Wherefore didst thou flee away secretly, and steal away from me?" [^kjv]. Enslaved people used the phrase for slipping off to worship in secret: Elvira Boles, interviewed at 94 in El Paso, Texas, remembered, "We had to steal away at night to have church on de ditch bank" [^boles].
- **"I hain't got long to stay here".** "Hain't" is an old spoken form of "haven't"; the Johnsons' 1925 book prints "I ain't got long to stay here" [^seward1872][^johnson1925]. The singer's time in this world is short.
- **Verses 1 and 3.** The Lord calls by thunder and lightning, and a trumpet sounds in the soul. At Mount Sinai in Exodus there are "thunders and lightnings" and "the voice of the trumpet exceeding loud", and the people tremble; Hymnary.org lists that chapter among the song's scriptures [^kjv][^hymnary].
- **Verses 2 and 4.** Trees bend in the storm, "poor sinners stand trembling", and "tombstones are bursting": the dead rise, as in Paul's words, "the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised" [^kjv][^hymnary].

Du Bois called the message of such songs "naturally veiled and half articulate" [^dubois1903].

### The full words

These are the words as Jubilee Songs (1872) prints them on page 28, with its spelling; its dashes are given as commas [^seward1872]. The refrain is sung after each verse. Under the music the refrain spells "haint" without an apostrophe; the verses spell it "hain't". We sing the refrain, verse 1 and the refrain again, with "hain't" throughout.

**Refrain**
Steal away, steal away, steal away to Jesus!
Steal away, steal away home,
I haint got long to stay here.

**Verse 1**
My Lord calls me, He calls me by the thunder;
The trumpet sounds it in my soul,
I hain't got long to stay here.

**Verse 2**
Green trees are bending, poor sinners stand trembling;
The trumpet sounds it in my soul,
I hain't got long to stay here.

**Verse 3**
My Lord calls me, He calls me by the lightning;
The trumpet sounds it in my soul:
I hain't got long to stay here.

**Verse 4**
Tombstones are bursting, poor sinners stand trembling;
The trumpet sounds it in my soul:
I hain't got long to stay here.

## How it is sung

A folk spiritual was sung by enslaved people among themselves; a concert spiritual is arranged in set harmony for the stage, and the Fisk singers were the first to make choral spirituals widely known [^carnegie]. James Weldon Johnson, in 1925, used Steal Away to show a developed form: the congregation begins with the chorus "in part harmony", the verse is sung by "the leader alone or the congregation in unison", and all answer in harmony on "I ain't got long to stay here" [^johnson1925]. It is in many church hymnals now, the African Methodist Episcopal Church Hymnal (2011) among them [^hymnary].

Our arrangement takes its melody and words from the 1872 page, in its key of F, slow and free; the book's 2/4 is written in 4/4 with the values doubled, and the harmony is our own [^seward1872]. We sing the refrain, verse 1 and the refrain again. In the verse the soprano sings the words while the others hum on "oo", and everyone joins on "I hain't got long to stay here", the shape Johnson described. Two singers are soprano and alto; three add the bass, four the tenor.

## Versions and other words

- **Pike, 1873, and Marsh, 1875.** The tour books reprint the 1872 music; their last verse has "Poor sinners are trembling" for "stand trembling" [^pike1873][^marsh1875].
- **The Johnsons, 1925.** An arrangement by J. Rosamond Johnson, dedicated to Roland Hayes, with the verse line "The trumpet sounds within-a my soul" [^johnson1925].
- **Tippett, 1944.** The English composer Michael Tippett set it in his oratorio A Child of Our Time, first performed at the Adelphi Theatre in London [^ort][^tippettfdn][^operatoday].

## Did you know?

- James Weldon Johnson opened his preface to the 1925 book with his own poem O Black and Unknown Bards, which asks whose heart "poured out such melody / As 'Steal away to Jesus'?" [^johnson1925].
- Tippett found the answer for his oratorio when he heard Steal Away on the radio: spirituals, he saw at once, had the universal reach he wanted [^ort][^wigglesworth].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/steal-away/soprano): you carry the tune, on five notes, F, G, A, C and D. Your top D comes on "to" in "steal away to Jesus" and starts the quick falling D, C, A before "home". In the verse you sing the words alone over the hums.
- [Alto](/songs/steal-away/alto): you sing the refrain's words mostly a sixth below the tune. In the verse you hum, low at first, then a high A held through "He calls me by the thunder". Your very last "here" rises to that high A.
- [Tenor](/songs/steal-away/tenor): you stay on F for much of the refrain, rising to B-flat on "to Je-" and "stay". In the verse you hum two long notes, F and then C, each about six beats. Your last "here" lifts to C, your top note.
- [Bass](/songs/steal-away/bass): you hold the low F for much of the refrain. In the verse you hum slow notes up to your top A, then hold F for six beats. "Here" at the end of the first refrain and the verse lands an octave above your low F; the final "here" goes back down to it.

## Questions

### What does "Steal Away" mean?

To steal away is to slip off quietly. The song asks the singer to slip away "to Jesus" and "home", because "I hain't got long to stay here": life is short, and home is with Jesus [^seward1872][^kjv]. The verses add thunder, a trumpet and bursting tombstones: the call of judgment day [^seward1872][^hymnary].

### Was Steal Away a code song for escape, or Nat Turner's signal?

There is no evidence that it was. Turner's 1831 confession mentions no song, and a historian of the Underground Railroad finds no evidence that Steal Away or any other song signalled escape [^turner1831][^bordewich]. The earliest account of its origin ties it to Wallace Willis in the Choctaw Nation, far from Turner's Virginia [^flickinger1914][^okhist].

### Who wrote Steal Away?

No one knows for sure; its earliest printing, Jubilee Songs (1872), names no maker [^seward1872]. A tradition credits Wallace Willis, enslaved in the Choctaw Nation, whose songs Alexander Reid taught the Fisk singers in 1871 [^flickinger1914][^okhist]. Whether he made it or learned it in his youth, the accounts differ [^flickinger1914][^banks].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano, all the way through, on five notes: F, G, A, C and D, with no B-flat or E. Seward's 1872 preface found that more than half the Fisk melodies leave out the fourth and seventh notes this way, and the Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology calls this one pentatonic [^seward1872][^canterbury].
