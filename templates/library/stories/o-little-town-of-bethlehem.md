O Little Town of Bethlehem is a Christmas carol by Phillips Brooks, an Episcopal priest, written in 1868 for the Sunday school of his church in Philadelphia [^benson1903][^allen1900][^julian]. He had spent Christmas Eve of 1865 in Bethlehem, at the Church of the Nativity [^lot1893][^canterbury]. His organist, Lewis H. Redner, wrote its tune for the children's Christmas service, and another editor named it ST. LOUIS in 1874 [^benson1903][^churchporch1874]. British and Irish books such as the Church of Scotland's Church Hymnary set it instead to FOREST GREEN, an English folk tune that Ralph Vaughan Williams set to the words in The English Hymnal of 1906 [^eh1906][^ch4][^iph2004].

## Where it comes from

**Bethlehem, 1865.** In August 1865 Phillips Brooks, the rector of the Church of the Holy Trinity in Philadelphia, left for a year abroad [^allen1900][^lot1893][^benson1903]. On Sunday, 24 December, he rode from Jerusalem to Bethlehem [^lot1893]. Writing home on 30 December, he described a good-looking town on a ridge among terraced gardens, and a ride before dark to the field "where they say the shepherds saw the star" [^lot1893]. The service in the Church of the Nativity ran from about ten at night to three in the morning, and he found it long and tiring [^lot1893][^canterbury]. Julian's Dictionary of Hymnology (1907) puts the visit at Christmas 1866, but Brooks's letter is dated 1865 [^julian][^lot1893].

![[2]]

Two months later it looked different. Writing from Rome on 19 February 1866 to his Sunday-school children, he remembered standing "close to the spot where Jesus was born" while the church rang "hour after hour" with hymns, and hearing in them the voices of home [^lot1893][^allen1900].

**The words, 1868.** Brooks came home in September 1866 [^allen1900][^benson1903]. His brother Arthur told the hymnologist Louis F. Benson that the carol was not written until 1868 [^benson1903]. Brooks wrote it for the Christmas service of his Sunday school, and it was sung there for the first time at Christmas 1868 [^benson1903][^allen1900][^julian]. Benson printed facsimiles of two verses from Brooks's manuscript: the first stanza and the stanza later left out. He also printed the tune's opening line in Redner's own hand [^benson1903].

**The tune, 1868.** Redner was the church's organist and the Sunday school's superintendent [^benson1903][^hymnary-redner]. Years later Redner wrote down for Benson how Brooks asked him for a tune as Christmas approached, and came to him on a Friday asking, "Redner, have you ground out that music yet?" Late on the Saturday night, he said, he woke "hearing an angel-strain whispering in my ear", jotted down the melody, and filled in the harmony on Sunday morning before church. Neither man, he added, thought the carol would outlive that Christmas [^benson1903][^hymnarchive].

**In print.** Benson wrote that the carol was printed for the Sunday school's Christmas programme of 1868, its earliest known printing [^benson1903][^allen1900], and Redner remembered a Philadelphia bookseller, Richard McCauley, printing it on leaflets for sale [^benson1903][^hymnarchive]. Its earliest known printing in a book came in 1870, in the Sunday School Service and Hymn Book of the Episcopal Diocese of Ohio [^ohio1870][^hymnarchive]. There it is No. 63, under "Christmas Eve", with five stanzas and no author's name. It has no music of its own, only a line sending singers to two other books for a tune [^ohio1870].

![[3]]

In 1874 William Reed Huntington, rector of All Saints' Church in Worcester, Massachusetts, printed words and tune together in his Sunday-school book The Church Porch [^benson1903][^churchporch1874]. Redner said it was Huntington who named the tune ST. LOUIS [^benson1903][^hymnarchive]. In 1892 the carol entered the official hymnal of Brooks's own church, the Episcopal Church's Hymnal of 1892, where it is No. 58 [^benson1903][^hrgc1892]. Brooks, Bishop of Massachusetts from 1891, died on 23 January 1893 [^benson1903][^hymnarchive-brooks].

![[4]]

**The other tune.** In December 1903 Ralph Vaughan Williams noted down a folk tune from a singer named Mr. Garman at Forest Green, in Surrey. The Journal of the Folk-Song Society printed it in 1906 as "The Ploughboy's Dream" [^jfss1906][^hymnarchive]. That year Vaughan Williams, the musical editor of The English Hymnal, set Brooks's words to it as No. 15, named FOREST GREEN and credited as an "English Traditional Melody" [^eh1906][^hymnarchive].

## What the words mean

Brooks wrote five stanzas of eight lines [^ohio1870][^benson1903]. American books have usually sung four since The Church Porch left out the fourth in 1874 [^churchporch1874][^hymnarchive]. Benson noted that until its last stanza the carol is not a prayer at all: the singer speaks to the town itself, and some hymnologists doubted it belonged in church for that reason [^benson1903].

**Stanza 1.** The singer looks down on Bethlehem asleep at night, with the stars moving silently overhead. The prophet Micah had called Bethlehem "little among the thousands of Judah", yet the place a ruler would come from [^kjv]. The "everlasting Light" shining in its dark streets is Christ, the "true Light" of John's Gospel [^kjv][^hymnarchive]. In the last two lines, everything people have longed for and dreaded through all of history meets in this one night.

**Stanza 2.** While the town sleeps, the angels, gathered above, keep watch in wonder. "O morning stars, together" recalls the Book of Job, where "the morning stars sang together" at the making of the world; the last lines echo the angels' song in Luke, "on earth peace" [^kjv][^hymnarchive].

**Stanza 3.** The gift comes in silence. No one hears Christ arrive, but wherever a humble ("meek") person will take him in, he still comes in. Hymnology Archive suggests an echo of Revelation 3:20, "I stand at the door, and knock" [^hymnarchive][^kjv].

**The omitted stanza.** Children pray, Misery cries out, Charity stands watching and Faith holds the door wide open, and Christmas comes again. In Brooks's manuscript, and in the Ohio book of 1870, its fourth line calls Christ "Son of the undefiled", the son of a pure, unstained mother [^benson1903][^ohio1870]. Redner wrote that the line drew "some amusing criticism lest it should smack of the doctrine of the Immaculate Conception", the Roman Catholic teaching that Mary was conceived free of original sin. Brooks changed it to "Son of the Mother mild", Redner said, and later dropped the stanza [^benson1903][^newadvent].

**Stanza 4 as American books number it.** Now the singer prays to the child: come down, drive out our sin, "be born in us to-day". "Emmanuel" is the name in Matthew's Gospel, "which being interpreted is, God with us" [^kjv].

### The full words

These are the four stanzas as Charles L. Hutchins printed them under Redner's tune in Carols Old and Carols New (Boston, 1916), Carol 599, the printing we sing from [^hutchins1916]. The earliest known printing with the tune, The Church Porch of 1874, has the same words with lighter punctuation and fuller spellings ("gathered", "wondering", "heaven"), and misprints "Everlasting" as "Ev-ev-last-ing" [^churchporch1874]. After them comes the stanza Brooks left out, as the Ohio book printed it in 1870, the form of his manuscript [^ohio1870][^benson1903]. Brooks died in 1893, so all five are in the public domain.

**Verse 1**
O little town of Bethlehem!
How still we see thee lie;
Above thy deep and dreamless sleep
The silent stars go by;
Yet in thy dark streets shineth
The everlasting Light;
The hopes and fears of all the years
Are met in thee to-night.

**Verse 2**
For Christ is born of Mary,
And gather'd all above,
While mortals sleep the angels keep
Their watch of wond'ring love.
O morning stars, together
Proclaim the holy birth!
And praises sing to God the King,
And peace to men on earth.

**Verse 3**
How silently, how silently,
The wondrous gift is given!
So God imparts to human hearts
The blessings of His heav'n.
No ear may hear His coming,
But in this world of sin,
Where meek souls will receive Him still,
The dear Christ enters in.

**Verse 4**
O holy Child of Bethlehem!
Descend to us, we pray;
Cast out our sin, and enter in,
Be born in us to-day.
We hear the Christmas angels
The great glad tidings tell;
O come to us, abide with us,
Our Lord Emmanuel!

**The omitted stanza (1870)**
Where children pure and happy
Pray to the blessed Child;
Where Misery cries out to Thee,
Son of the undefiled;
Where Charity stands watching,
And Faith holds wide the door,
The dark night wakes, the glory breaks,
And Christmas comes once more.

## How it is sung

Brooks wrote it for children, and Benson thought that if Redner's tune had failed that Christmas, the carol would probably not have been reprinted or heard again in Brooks's lifetime [^benson1903][^allen1900].

Its meter is 8.6.8.6.7.6.8.6, which The English Hymnal calls "D.C.M. Words irreg.": double common meter, with shorter fifth lines [^umh1989][^eh1906].

The tune depends on the book. The United Methodist Hymnal (1989) sets it to ST. LOUIS [^umh1989]; the Episcopal Hymnal 1982 prints both, No. 78 to FOREST GREEN and No. 79 to ST. LOUIS [^eh1982-78][^eh1982-79]. The Church Hymnary (fourth edition), the Irish Presbyterian Hymnbook (2004) and the British Methodists' Singing the Faith (2011) use FOREST GREEN [^ch4][^iph2004][^stf2011].

We sing ST. LOUIS in G as Hutchins printed it, Carol 599 on p. 484, checked against the Episcopal Church's Hymnal of 1916, No. 78, which has the same tune [^hutchins1916][^hymnal1916]. The Church Porch of 1874 differs in one note, B where they have D on "and" in "deep and dreamless", and in places in the alto and tenor [^churchporch1874][^hutchins1916][^hymnal1916]. Our arrangement is read from Redner's four parts as Hutchins prints them: verses 1, 3 and 4 of his four, for two to four singers, soprano and alto, then bass for three and tenor for four [^hutchins1916]. The soprano sings Redner's tune, unchanged but for breaths written in for beginners. His chords are kept, and the lower parts are adapted for beginners: one note to a syllable, the alto and tenor revoiced in places, and the bass an octave lower from "by;" to "Light;" to stay in a beginner's range, holding a low B under "The everlast-" where Redner doubles the tune in all four voices.

## Versions and other words

FOREST GREEN is the main other tune. In The English Hymnal it carries all five stanzas, with the fourth in its "Mother mild" form and the two halves of the second stanza swapped [^eh1906][^eh1906words][^hymnarchive]. The Episcopal Hymnal of 1892 printed only words. Its rival musical editions wrote new tunes for the carol: one, edited by J. Ireland Tucker and W. W. Rousseau, gives two, by James C. Knox and W. H. Walter [^hrgc1892]. Benson wrote that they also drew attention afresh to Redner's tune [^benson1903].

## Did you know?

- The Ohio book of 1870 printed music for only two of its hymns, on one page at the back. Its preface says buying the rights to tunes and printing music would have priced it beyond most Sunday schools, so for the rest it named the book where a tune could be found [^ohio1870].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/o-little-town-of-bethlehem/soprano): the tune, most often on B. Its top E comes twice a verse, on "deep" and on "Are" ("parts" and "The", "sin," and "Our"), the second an octave leap up from the E on "years" after a quick breath; its low D comes only on "thee" ("is", "we"), before the leap up to B.
- [Alto](/songs/o-little-town-of-bethlehem/alto): mostly a sixth below the tune, most often on D, meeting the tune only on its low D ("thee", "is", "we"). Each third line climbs G, G-sharp, A to the top A on "dream-" ("hu-", "en-"); the low B comes on the second note of every even line and at the end of the fourth and last lines.
- [Tenor](/songs/o-little-town-of-bethlehem/tenor): the middle of the chord, most often on G, then B, sharing the bass's G on "O little town of" and "The hopes and fears of". Its top E comes only on "and dream-" ("to hu-", "and en-"), and its low D only on the last note of each verse.
- [Bass](/songs/o-little-town-of-bethlehem/bass): the bass line, most often on G, high and low, then D. Each verse opens on the high G and ends its second line on the low G; from "by;" it stays low, climbing from G to B under "Yet in thy dark streets shineth" and holding that B, but for a C on the "-ing" of "everlasting", to "Light;".

## Questions

### What is O Little Town of Bethlehem about?

It looks at Bethlehem asleep on the night Christ is born. The "everlasting Light" shines in its dark streets, and the hopes and fears of all the years meet there; angels keep watch, and the gift comes silently to anyone meek enough to receive him [^kjv][^hymnarchive]. The last stanza turns to prayer, asking the child to "be born in us to-day" [^hutchins1916]. Brooks wrote it in 1868, after spending Christmas Eve of 1865 in Bethlehem [^benson1903][^lot1893].

### What year was O Little Town of Bethlehem written?

In 1868. Phillips Brooks wrote it in Philadelphia for his Sunday school's Christmas service, and it was first sung there at Christmas 1868 [^benson1903][^allen1900]. It grew from his Christmas Eve in Bethlehem three years earlier [^lot1893][^canterbury][^benson1903].

### Who wrote the music for O Little Town of Bethlehem?

Lewis H. Redner (1831 to 1908), the organist of Brooks's church in Philadelphia, wrote the tune ST. LOUIS for it in 1868 [^benson1903][^hymnary-redner]. By his own account the melody came to him in the night [^benson1903].

### Is O Little Town of Bethlehem a hymn, and is it public domain?

It is a Christmas carol that hymnals print as a hymn: the Episcopal Church's Hymnal of 1892 as No. 58, The Hymnal 1982 as Nos. 78 and 79, and The United Methodist Hymnal as No. 230 [^hrgc1892][^eh1982-78][^eh1982-79][^umh1989]. Phillips Brooks wrote the words and died in 1893; Lewis H. Redner wrote the tune we sing, ST. LOUIS, and died in 1908 [^benson1903][^hymnarchive-brooks][^hymnary-redner]. Our arrangement is our own, read from Redner's four parts; other settings and modern arrangements can carry their own rights.

### What key is O Little Town of Bethlehem in?

It has no fixed key. Redner's tune was printed in G in The Church Porch of 1874, and The United Methodist Hymnal prints it in F [^churchporch1874][^umh1989]. FOREST GREEN is in F in The English Hymnal [^eh1906]. Our arrangement is in G, the key of The Church Porch and of Hutchins (1916) [^churchporch1874][^hutchins1916].
