Amazing Grace, whose opening line Newton printed as "Amazing grace! (how sweet the sound)", is a hymn by John Newton, a Church of England curate who had once captained slave ships [^olney1779][^dnb][^thoughts1788]. He wrote it for his parish at Olney, Buckinghamshire, for New Year's Day 1773, and printed it in 1779 in Olney Hymns as "Faith's Review and Expectation" [^sermonnotes][^canterbury][^cnbulletin][^olney1779]. Its tune, NEW BRITAIN, is American, by no known maker, and its earliest known printing is from Cincinnati in 1829 [^hymnary-tune][^motb-sound]. Words and tune met in 1835, and in 1972 a Scottish regiment's pipes and band took the hymn to number one in Britain [^southernharmony1835][^hymnarchive][^officialcharts][^scotsdg].

## Where it comes from

**John Newton.** Newton was born in London on 24 July 1725 and went to sea with his father at eleven [^dnb][^hymnary-newton]. As a young man he spent about a year and a half on the coast of Sierra Leone, held and badly treated by a slave trader [^thoughts1788][^dnb]. Sailing home in 1748, his ship was nearly lost in a storm on 10 March, "that is in the present stile the 21st" [^narrative1764][^dnb]. Worn out with cold and pumping, he said, "If this will not do, the Lord have mercy upon us", the first wish for mercy, he wrote, in many years. He marked the day every year after [^narrative1764][^dnb].

**The trade.** The storm did not take Newton out of the slave trade. From 1750 to 1754 he commanded three voyages to the Windward Coast of Africa to buy enslaved people [^thoughts1788][^dnb][^rmg-log]. "I never had a scruple upon this head at the time," he wrote later [^thoughts1788]. Illness ended his time at sea in 1754, and in 1764 he was ordained and became curate of Olney [^thoughts1788][^dnb][^hymnary-newton]. In 1788 he published Thoughts upon the African Slave Trade, which the Dictionary of National Biography says aided William Wilberforce's campaign [^thoughts1788][^dnb]. He described captives chained in pairs for months, and called the pamphlet a "public confession, which, however sincere, comes too late" [^thoughts1788]. Parliament abolished the British slave trade on 25 March 1807; Newton died in London that December [^tna][^rmg-end][^dnb][^hymnary-newton].

![[1]]

**The words.** Newton's notes for a sermon on "New Year's morning" 1773 survive at Lambeth Palace Library, headed "1 Chron. 17. 16, 17" [^sermonnotes]. Hymnologists tie the hymn to that sermon, and a Newton researcher has matched its lines to the sermon's points, not all with certainty [^hymnarchive][^canterbury][^motb-creation]. In those verses King David asks God "what is mine house, that thou hast brought me hitherto?" and gives thanks for a promise "for a great while to come" [^kjv]. The hymn, as its title says, looks back and ahead in the same way: "Faith's review and expectation" [^olney1779][^hymnarchive].

![[2]]

Newton made Olney Hymns with the poet William Cowper, whose hymns are marked C; this one is not [^olney1779][^dnb]. He dated its preface 15 February 1779 and gave it to his parish, "for whose use the hymns were originally composed"; No. 41 has no music [^olney1779][^hymnarchive].

![[3]]

**The tune.** No one knows who made NEW BRITAIN [^hymnary-tune][^bethel]. Its earliest known printing is in Columbian Harmony, compiled by Benjamin Shaw and Charles H. Spilman at Cincinnati in 1829, in two versions [^motb-sound][^hymnary-tune][^canterbury][^hymnarchive]. The Virginia Harmony (1831) called it HARMONY GROVE; neither book sets Newton's words to it [^hymnarchive][^flutopedia][^canterbury][^motb-sound]. Hymnals before 1991 often credited the 1831 book; that year the hymnologist Marion Hatchett pointed to the 1829 printings [^ct2003].

The earliest known pairing is on page 8 of William Walker's The Southern Harmony (Spartanburg, South Carolina, 1835) [^southernharmony1835][^hymnarchive][^canterbury]. It is headed "NEW BRITAIN. C. M.", in shape notes, whose heads show each note's place in the scale, with the tune in the middle line and all six stanzas [^southernharmony1835][^bethel]. Its corner note, "Baptist Harmony, p. 123", points to Walker's source for the words, Staunton S. Burdett's book of 1834 [^southernharmony1835][^hymnarchive]. The World (2018) gives 1847, the year of a later printing, for the pairing, but the 1835 book already has it [^theworld][^bethel][^southernharmony1835].

![[4]]

**The stanza that isn't Newton's.** "When we've been there ten thousand years", sung last in many hymnals, especially in the USA, was never Newton's [^canterbury][^hymnarchive]. Its oldest form we have read, spoken by Christ, is in A Choice Collection of Hymns (Philadelphia, 1782), catalogued under the preacher Elhanan Winchester: "When you've been there ten thousand Year" [^winchester1782]. A second Philadelphia Choice Collection, of 1784, ends its hymn "On the death of a Saint" with "When they've been there ten million years" [^winchester1784]. The Canterbury Dictionary and the Hymnology Archive both trace the stanza to that hymn, which Winchester later claimed as his own [^canterbury][^hymnarchive]. The "we" form, with "ten thousand years", ends "Jerusalem, my happy home" in A Collection of Sacred Ballads, gathered by Richard and Andrew Broaddus in Virginia, its preface dated 1 January 1790 [^broaddus1790][^hymnarchive]. In Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852), Tom, the enslaved hero, sings it after Newton's last two stanzas, in a changed order and changed words [^stowe][^canterbury]. The earliest hymnal known to join it to Newton's words is R. A. Torrey's World Renowned Hymns (1909), in E. O. Excell's arrangement: Newton's first three stanzas, then this one [^wrh1909][^hymnarchive]. Excell printed the same four in his Coronation Hymns (1910), and his arrangement is likely the version most people know [^coronation1910][^motb-sound].

**Myth and record.** Often told, online for many years: Newton wrote the hymn at sea, during the storm [^buzzkill]. What the record shows: the storm was in 1748; the hymn belongs to his Olney years, tied to a sermon of 1773 [^narrative1764][^sermonnotes][^hymnarchive].

Often told, in a gospel singer's talk filmed at Carnegie Hall and shared online: Newton set his words to a slave melody like a West African sorrow chant, on the piano's five black keys [^zwicky]. What the record shows: Newton printed the words with no music, and the tune's earliest known printing is in Ohio in 1829, twenty-two years after he died [^olney1779][^hymnary-tune][^motb-sound][^dnb]. Nor do the words mention the trade [^olney1779].

Often told, as the museum at Olney reports: it is an old Scottish tune, or an American plantation song, and a musicologist quoted there hears its five notes used in a markedly Scottish way [^cnbulletin]. What the record shows: the tune is pentatonic, and its earliest known printings are American [^hymnary-tune][^hymnarchive]. No document ties it to Africa, Scotland or Newton's ships.

## What the words mean

1. "Grace" is God's favour, given to someone who has not earned it; a "wretch" is a miserable, worthless person. "Lost" and "found" echo the prodigal son in Luke 15, and "Was blind, but now I see" the healed man in John 9 [^kjv].
2. Grace first taught the singer to fear, to see their danger, then took the fear away.
3. The look back: through dangers, hard work and traps, grace has brought the singer "safe thus far", as David thanks God for bringing him "hitherto" [^kjv][^motb-creation]. "Home" is heaven.
4. The look ahead: God's promise makes hope secure. A "portion" is a share of an inheritance; God himself is the singer's share.
5. "When this flesh and heart shall fail" takes the words of Psalm 73:26 [^kjv]. "Within the vail" means behind the veil of the Temple's holiest place, the image Hebrews 6:19 uses for hope that reaches God [^kjv].
6. The earth will melt like snow and the sun go dark, but God remains: an image like 2 Peter 3:12, where "the elements shall melt" [^kjv].

### The full words

Newton's six stanzas as printed in Olney Hymns (London, 1779), Book I, Hymn 41, the long s set as s [^olney1779]. Many hymnals print fewer: Excell's of 1900 has stanzas 1 to 4, and his arrangement of 1909 and 1910 stanzas 1 to 3 and the later stanza [^excell1900][^wrh1909][^coronation1910]. The last stanza is not Newton's; it is printed as in A Collection of Sacred Ballads (Virginia, 1790) [^broaddus1790].

**Verse 1**
Amazing grace! (how sweet the sound)
That sav'd a wretch like me!
I once was lost, but now am found,
Was blind, but now I see.

**Verse 2**
'Twas grace that taught my heart to fear,
And grace my fears reliev'd;
How precious did that grace appear,
The hour I first believ'd!

**Verse 3**
Thro' many dangers, toils and snares,
I have already come;
'Tis grace has brought me safe thus far,
And grace will lead me home.

**Verse 4**
The Lord has promis'd good to me,
His word my hope secures;
He will my shield and portion be,
As long as life endures.

**Verse 5**
Yes, when this flesh and heart shall fail,
And mortal life shall cease;
I shall possess, within the vail,
A life of joy and peace.

**Verse 6**
The earth shall soon dissolve like snow,
The sun forbear to shine;
But God, who call'd me here below,
Will be for ever mine.

**Later added stanza**
When we've been there ten thousand years
Bright shining as the sun,
We've no less days to sing God's praise
Than when we first begun.

## How it is sung

It is a congregational hymn in common meter, lines of eight and six syllables, which Walker marked "C. M." [^southernharmony1835][^hymnary-tune]. NEW BRITAIN is in three [^southernharmony1835][^coronation1910]. E. O. Excell's arrangement of 1900, which printed the tune under the title AMAZING GRACE, is still used in hymnals such as the Psalter Hymnal [^excell1900][^canterbury][^hymnary-tune]. In the 1960s it was among the hymns of the American civil rights movement [^motb-life][^theworld].

Our arrangement sings verse 1, in G, for 2 to 4 singers, with the tune in the soprano. Two singers are soprano and alto; a third adds the bass on "oo", and a fourth the tenor in the middle of the chord.

## Did you know?

- In 1892 John Julian's Dictionary of Hymnology found the hymn "unknown to modern collections" in Britain, though in America "its use is extensive", and judged it "far from being a good example of Newton's work" [^julian1892].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/amazing-grace/soprano): you carry the tune, built on five notes, and sing the first "A-" alone before the others join. Your top D comes on the held "me" ending line 2 and again on "once", early in line 3; give the last "see" its full length.
- [Alto](/songs/amazing-grace/alto): you sing a third or a sixth below the tune on almost every note, often holding a note while the tune moves, up to B on "once" and ending on a low B for "see".
- [Tenor](/songs/amazing-grace/tenor): you sing almost every word on G, moving only to A on "me" and the last "now", and to C on the last "I", before the final G.
- [Bass](/songs/amazing-grace/bass): you hold the bass line on "oo", one long note a bar, mostly low G, changing to C under "sweet" and "now am", B under "lost", and D under "me" and "now I".

## Questions

### Was Amazing Grace written by a slave ship captain?

Yes, by a former one. John Newton commanded three voyages from 1750 to 1754 to buy enslaved people in West Africa [^thoughts1788][^rmg-log]. He wrote the hymn as curate of Olney for New Year's Day 1773, almost twenty years after leaving the sea [^sermonnotes][^hymnarchive][^cnbulletin].

### Who wrote the melody of Amazing Grace?

No one knows. The tune, NEW BRITAIN, is American, and its earliest known printing, with no composer named, is in Columbian Harmony (Cincinnati, 1829) [^motb-sound][^hymnary-tune]. William Walker printed it with Newton's words, as NEW BRITAIN, in The Southern Harmony in 1835 [^southernharmony1835][^canterbury].

### What Bible verse is Amazing Grace based on?

1 Chronicles 17:16-17. Olney Hymns prints the hymn under "I. Chronicles" with "Chap. xvii. 16, 17", and Newton preached on those verses on New Year's Day 1773 [^olney1779][^sermonnotes]. There King David looks back on how far God has brought him and ahead to God's promise, as the hymn does [^kjv][^hymnarchive].

### Is Amazing Grace a Scottish song?

Not as far as the record shows. Its words were written by an Englishman for his parish in Buckinghamshire, and its tune's earliest known printing is American; where the tune was sung before that is unknown, and one musicologist hears a Scottish turn in its five notes [^olney1779][^hymnary-tune][^cnbulletin]. The bagpipe link comes from a Scottish regiment's pipes and band, whose record was number one in the UK for five weeks from April 1972 [^officialcharts][^scotsdg].
