Shall We Gather at the River?, called Beautiful River in its earliest known printings, is a gospel hymn by Robert Lowry, a Baptist pastor in Brooklyn, who wrote its words and tune in July 1864 [^burrage1888][^sankey1907][^canterbury-hymn]. Its first verse asks a question about heaven, and the chorus answers it: yes, we will meet at the river of life that flows from the throne of God in the Book of Revelation [^burrage1888][^brightjewels1869]. Its earliest known printing is in Happy Voices, a Sunday-school book of 1865 [^happyvoices1865][^julian], and Henry Burrage and Ira D. Sankey later wrote that forty thousand Brooklyn children sang it in 1865, the year after he wrote it [^burrage1888][^sankey1907]. Hymnary.org indexes it in more than 800 hymnals [^hymnary-text], and the film director John Ford used it in his westerns, where writers on film call it one of his favourite hymns [^afi-stagecoach][^afi-searchers][^filmsite-stagecoach][^bbcmusic][^ebert-clementine].

## Where it comes from

**The writer.** Robert Lowry was born in Philadelphia on 12 March 1826 [^burrage1888][^long1876][^canterbury-lowry]. His parents were Associate Presbyterians, Burrage says, but he joined the Baptists, studied at Lewisburg (now Bucknell University) and was ordained in 1854 [^burrage1888][^canterbury-lowry]. After churches in West Chester and New York City, he became pastor of the Hanson Place Baptist Church in Brooklyn in 1861 and stayed until 1869 [^burrage1888][^canterbury-lowry]. He later served at Lewisburg and at Plainfield, New Jersey [^burrage1888][^canterbury-lowry], and died at Plainfield on 25 November 1899 [^canterbury-lowry][^hall]. His other songs include Where Is My Wandering Boy Tonight?, words and music his, and the tune for Anna Warner's One More Day's Work for Jesus [^burrage1888][^julian][^sankey1907].

![[1]]

**The writing, in three tellings.** Three books tell the afternoon he wrote it, years afterwards, and they differ in detail. They agree on a hot summer day in 1864 in Brooklyn [^long1876][^burrage1888][^sankey1907]. The earliest is Edwin M. Long's Illustrated History of Hymns and Their Authors, printed in 1876 while Lowry was alive. Long has him seated in his parlour, and writes that an epidemic was sweeping through the city and many people were dying, so that a question rose in his heart: "We are parting at the river of death, shall we meet at the river of life?" Then Long quotes Lowry himself: "Seating myself at the organ, simply to give vent to the pent up emotions of the heart, the words and music of the hymn began to flow out, as if by inspiration" [^long1876].

Henry Burrage's Baptist Hymn Writers of 1888 tells it more fully, in the third person. Burrage sets it on a July afternoon, with Lowry lying on a couch, worn out by the heat, while the pictures of Revelation passed before his mind: the throne, the heavenly river, the saints gathering. He wondered why hymn writers had said so much about "the river of death" and so little about the river of the water of life. The words came first as a question, "Shall we gather?", then as a chorus that answered it, "Yes, we'll gather". The music came with them, and Lowry could never say which came first. "They are twins," Burrage writes. He got up, played it through at his organ, sang the first verse and chorus, and wrote it down [^burrage1888]. Ira D. Sankey's book of hymn stories, published after Lowry's death, has him writing the words at his study table in Elliott Place, Brooklyn, then composing the tune at his parlour organ: words first, then music, where Long and Burrage have them come together [^sankey1907][^long1876][^burrage1888]. Only Long tells of an epidemic [^long1876][^burrage1888][^sankey1907].

![[2]]

**The earliest printings.** Lowry gave it to the American Tract Society for a new songbook, Burrage says [^burrage1888]. In that book, Happy Voices (New York, 1865), "for the home circle and sabbath schools", it is No. 220 on page 153, headed "Beautiful River" and credited to "Rev. R. Lowry" [^happyvoices1865][^julian]. It has five verses, the last three printed in columns under the music [^happyvoices1865]. A year later William B. Bradbury printed it on page 25 of his New Golden Shower (1866), "By permission of the author, Rev. R. Lowry". The page shows the hymn grown: Revelation 22:1 printed above the music, the direction "Cheerful", a tenor singing from the first note, and six verses, with a new third verse, "On the bosom of the river", and "Rippling" and "shining" where Happy Voices had "Mirror" and "silver" [^ngs1866][^fenner]. Lowry's own Sunday-school book, Bright Jewels (New York: Biglow & Main, 1869), prints the same setting and words on page 110, signed "R. L." [^brightjewels1869][^burrage1888].

![[3]]

**The tune's name.** The tune is now usually called HANSON PLACE, after Lowry's Brooklyn church. The name is a late one: Hymnology Archive credits the editors of the Southern Baptists' Baptist Hymnal of 1956, where it is No. 481 [^fenner][^hymnary-bh1956]. Older books call the tune BEAUTIFUL RIVER [^hymnary-text].

**What happened next.** The next spring, Burrage says, the Brooklyn Sunday School Union asked to use it at its May anniversary, and forty thousand children sang it on parade and in their churches [^burrage1888]. Sankey puts it on Children's Day in Brooklyn, "more than forty thousand voices" [^sankey1907]. Sankey quotes Lowry as modest about the tune: "It is brass-band music, has a march movement, and for that reason has become popular, though, for myself, I do not think much of it." Yet he had been deeply moved by the singing of it [^sankey1907].

In the twentieth century it became a sound of the American West on film, in John Ford's westerns Stagecoach (1939) and The Searchers (1956) [^afi-stagecoach][^filmsite-stagecoach][^afi-searchers][^filmsite-searchers].

## What the words mean

The river is the one in the last chapter of the Bible. In Revelation 22:1 to 2, an angel shows John "a pure river of water of life, clear as crystal, proceeding out of the throne of God and of the Lamb". Beside it grows the tree of life, whose leaves heal the nations [^kjv]. "The Lamb" is Christ. Bright Jewels prints verse 1 of that chapter above the music, and the hymn takes up its words: "clear as crystal" becomes the "crystal tide", and "the throne of God" stays as it is [^brightjewels1869][^kjv]. That was the point of the story as Burrage tells it: Lowry felt hymns had said a great deal about "the river of death" and too little about the river of life [^burrage1888][^long1876].

1. Will we gather by the heavenly river, where angels walk, which flows for ever past God's throne? The chorus answers: yes, with the saints, the people of God.
2. On its "margin", its bank, where it throws up silver spray, we will walk and worship all through the happy, golden day.
3. On the river's "bosom", its surface, where we confess ("own") Christ as Saviour and king, we will meet and never grieve again, "'neath", beneath, the glory of the throne.
4. "Ere", before, we reach the river, let us lay down every burden; grace will set our spirits free and give us the robe and crown of the saved.
5. Where the river smiles, rippling with the Saviour's face, the saints, whom death can no longer part ("sever"), sing of the grace that saved them.
6. Soon we will reach it, soon our pilgrimage, the long journey of life, will end, and our hearts will "quiver", tremble like a sounding string, with the music of peace.

We sing verse 1 and the chorus.

### The full words

These are the six verses and the chorus as Lowry printed them in Bright Jewels (1869), page 110, the book our arrangement is read from. Happy Voices (1865) has five: it lacks the third verse here, has "Mirror of the Saviour's face" in the fifth, and "the silver river" in the last. The six verses as here are already in Bradbury's New Golden Shower (1866).

**Verse 1**
Shall we gather at the river
Where bright angel feet have trod;
With its crystal tide forever
Flowing by the throne of God?

**Refrain**
Yes, we'll gather at the river,
The beautiful, the beautiful river,
Gather with the saints at the river
That flows by the throne of God.

**Verse 2**
On the margin of the river,
Washing up its silver spray,
We will walk and worship ever,
All the happy, golden day.

**Verse 3**
On the bosom of the river,
Where the Saviour-king we own,
We shall meet, and sorrow never
'Neath the glory of the throne.

**Verse 4**
Ere we reach the shining river,
Lay we every burden down;
Grace our spirits will deliver,
And provide a robe and crown.

**Verse 5**
At the smiling of the river,
Rippling with the Saviour's face,
Saints, whom death will never sever,
Lift their songs of saving grace.

**Verse 6**
Soon we'll reach the shining river
Soon our pilgrimage will cease,
Soon our happy hearts will quiver
With the melody of peace.

## How it is sung

It began as a Sunday-school song and became a gospel hymn sung in churches of many kinds [^burrage1888][^hymnary-text]. Its meter is 8.7.8.7 with a refrain [^hymnary-text]. Lowry heard it as a march [^sankey1907], and Bright Jewels marks it "Cheerful" [^brightjewels1869]. Both early books mark the chorus's "beautiful, the beautiful river" to be sung softly [^happyvoices1865][^brightjewels1869].

The 1865 and 1866 printings set it differently. In Happy Voices the verse is in three parts, soprano and alto on the upper staff and the bass alone below, and a tenor joins only for the chorus [^happyvoices1865]. From the New Golden Shower on, all four parts sing throughout [^ngs1866][^brightjewels1869].

Our arrangement is read from the four parts in Lowry's Bright Jewels, a setting whose earliest known printing is in Bradbury's New Golden Shower of 1866 [^ngs1866][^brightjewels1869], in E-flat as printed and in 4/4, the first verse and the chorus for 2 to 4 singers. Where Lowry gives the alto or tenor two notes to a syllable, we sing one; the tenor stays on E-flat for the "-er" of "river" and "forever" instead of dropping back to B-flat, and sings C on "Yes, we'll", the note Happy Voices gives it. The alto ends on a low G instead of Lowry's E-flat, so the duet and trio end on a chord's third rather than bare E-flats, and breaths are written in.

## Versions and other words

Charles Ives's Fourth Violin Sonata, "Children's Day at the Camp Meeting", remembers the children's services at summer camp meetings in Connecticut, and its last movement brings in this hymn; in his own note on it, Ives gives thanks to Robert Lowry for "Gather at the River" [^ives-parlance][^ives-society]. He also made a song of it, "At the River" [^ives-society].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/shall-we-gather/soprano): you carry Lowry's tune, cheerful and steady, its dotted rhythm ("gath-er at the") giving it its march. The fun is the chorus: from the low E-flat on "God?" you jump up to C for "Yes, we'll", then climb to your top E-flat on "gather at", and again on "saints at".
- [Alto](/songs/shall-we-gather/alto): you sing mostly in thirds under the tune. In the chorus you hold one note, A-flat, through "Yes, we'll gather at the" and "Gather with the saints at the" while the tune leaps above you. You share the soprano's E-flat on the verse's "God?" and end on a low G under the last "God".
- [Tenor](/songs/shall-we-gather/tenor): you sing the words mostly on B-flat, rising to E-flat on "river", "forever" and "Gather with the". "Yes, we'll gather" sits on C, a sixth under the alto, with a quick dip to B natural on "-er"; solo it in the player to tune that half step.
- [Bass](/songs/shall-we-gather/bass): you sing the roots on just three notes, E-flat, B-flat and A-flat. The whole first line is on E-flat and the whole second line on the low B-flat. In the chorus you start high on A-flat for "Yes, we'll gather" and "Gather with the saints".

## Questions

### What Bible verse is Shall We Gather at the River based on?

Revelation 22:1, "a pure river of water of life, clear as crystal, proceeding out of the throne of God and of the Lamb" [^kjv]. Lowry printed that verse above the hymn in his Bright Jewels of 1869 [^brightjewels1869]. Its "crystal tide" echoes the verse's "clear as crystal" [^kjv].

### What movies is Shall We Gather at the River in?

It is best known from two of John Ford's westerns. In Stagecoach (1939) it plays as two outcasts, a dance-hall girl and a drunken doctor, are driven out of town [^filmsite-stagecoach][^mmuk-stagecoach], and in The Searchers (1956) settlers sing it at the graves of a murdered family [^filmsite-searchers][^mmuk-searchers]. The film catalogue of the American Film Institute lists it in both [^afi-stagecoach][^afi-searchers].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings Lowry's tune, as he printed it in Bright Jewels in 1869 [^brightjewels1869]. With two singers you have the tune and the alto, which moves mostly in thirds below it; with three, add the bass on the roots, and the tenor makes four.
