Come, Thou Fount of Every Blessing is a hymn of thanks for grace, written in England by Robert Robinson, a young preacher who had come to faith under George Whitefield [^julian-robinson][^dnb]. A list of his writings in his own hand, kept in his Cambridge church book, dates it to 1758, and its earliest surviving printing is in a London hymnbook of 1759 [^julian][^hymnarchive]. In America it found its tune, NETTLETON, printed in Pennsylvania in 1813 [^hymnary-nettleton][^hymnarchive]. Its second verse raises an "Ebenezer", a stone from the Book of Samuel [^madan1760][^kjv].

## Where it comes from

**The writer.** Robert Robinson was born at Swaffham in Norfolk on 27 September 1735 [^julian-robinson][^dnb]. In 1749, at about fourteen, he was apprenticed to a London hairdresser [^dyer][^dnb]. On 24 May 1752 he heard George Whitefield preach on "the wrath to come", and he dated his religious life from that day [^julian-robinson][^hatfield][^dnb]. He preached among the Calvinistic Methodists, then led an independent church in Norwich [^julian-robinson][^hatfield]. In 1759 he went to the Baptist church in Cambridge [^dnb][^hatfield][^julian-robinson].

**The words.** Robinson kept a handwritten list of his works in his Cambridge church book, which Samuel Duffield called a catalogue of his writings up to 1781 [^duffield]. John Julian, writing in 1892, when the book belonged to Robinson's biographer William Robinson, quoted one entry: "Mr. Wheatley of Norwich published a hymn beginning 'Come, Thou Fount of every blessing' (1758)" [^julian][^hymnarchive]. No copy of that Norwich printing is known [^julian][^hymnarchive]. The earliest surviving text is in A Collection of Hymns for the Use of the Church of Christ, Meeting in Angel-Alley, a London book of 1759, where it has four eight-line stanzas [^julian][^hymnarchive]. Its full title names the church "meeting in Angel-Alley, Whitechappel" and others in fellowship with it; Julian placed Angel-Alley in Bishopsgate [^canterbury][^julian]. His friend George Dyer's memoir of 1796 says he wrote it "when among the methodists" [^dyer][^julian], that is, before 1759, in his early twenties [^julian-robinson][^dnb]. In 1760 Martin Madan printed it without the fourth stanza in his Collection of Psalms and Hymns [^madan1760][^julian]. Julian reports no other change, but the two pages side by side show more: Robinson's "Hither by thy Grace I'm come" became "Hither by thine Help I'm Come", "So I hope" became "And I hope", "Shortly to arrive at Home" became "Safely", and in the third stanza "that God I love" and "Take my Heart" became "the God" and "Here's mine Heart" [^julian][^hymnarchive][^madan1760]. Most books have followed that three-stanza text since [^julian][^hymnarchive]. John Rippon printed it in his Baptist Selection of Hymns in 1787, and a letter from Rippon among Robinson's papers named it as his [^dyer][^julian][^hymnarchive].

**Who wrote it?** In 1858 and 1859 Daniel Sedgwick argued that Selina, Countess of Huntingdon, wrote it [^julian][^dnb], relying, Julian says, on a handwritten copy headed "Hymn by the Countess of Huntingdon" [^julian]. In 1892 Julian called that evidence worthless and settled the question for Robinson [^julian][^dnb][^canterbury].

**The tune.** NETTLETON's earliest known printing is in Wyeth's Repository of Sacred Music, Part Second, printed by John Wyeth at Harrisburg, Pennsylvania, in 1813 [^hymnary-nettleton][^hymnarchive][^wyeth1813]. There it is called HALLELUJAH, set in two parts to Robinson's words, with no composer named [^hymnary-nettleton][^hymnarchive][^psh]. On its page, 112, it stands in F, tune over bass, and its words still end "Mount of God's unchanging love" [^hymnarchive][^hymnary-nettleton]. Wyeth was a printer and bookseller, not a musician; his Part Second served revival and camp-meeting singers [^steel][^wyeth1813]. The tune was later named after Asahel Nettleton (1783 to 1844), a revival preacher; some books credit him, but his Village Hymns printed words only [^hymnary-nettleton][^hymnarchive].

**Myth and record.** Often told: late in life, Robinson rode in a stagecoach with a lady who praised this hymn to him, not knowing who he was. At last he said: "Madam, I am the poor, unhappy man who composed that hymn many years ago; and I would give a thousand worlds, if I had them, to enjoy the feelings I then had" [^belcher][^duffield]. What the record shows: the oldest printing of the story we have found is in Joseph Belcher's Historical Sketches of Hymns (Philadelphia, 1859) [^belcher]. Belcher says it came to him "some twenty years since" from a descendant of one of the two travellers [^belcher][^duffield]. In his telling the lady had been reading the hymn; in Hezekiah Butterworth's of 1875 she is singing it [^belcher][^butterworth]. Dyer's memoir, written from Robinson's papers, does not have it [^dyer]. It is a family story, printed almost seventy years after his death.

**What Robinson came to believe.** In 1776 he published A Plea for the Divinity of our Lord Jesus Christ [^dnb][^hatfield]. Later he moved away from it: the Dictionary of National Biography puts the change down to his research for a history of the Baptists and to the writings of Joseph Priestley [^dnb]. Dyer wrote that his last publication showed "some sentiments in common with unitarians", but that "he could not be tied down to creeds of any kind" [^dyer]. Julian's dictionary disagreed: Unitarians, it said, had made much of his meeting Priestley in Birmingham at the end of his life "on exceedingly slender grounds" [^julian-robinson]. Robinson died near Birmingham on 9 June 1790, and Priestley preached one of his funeral sermons there [^dnb][^dyer][^hatfield].

## What the words mean

Madan's 1760 printing marked lines with signs keyed to Bible references at the foot of the page [^madan1760].

1. A "fount" is a spring: God is the source of every blessing, and his endless mercy calls for the loudest praise. "Tune my heart" asks God to tune the singer like an instrument. A "sonnet" here is simply a song, one fit for the "flaming tongues above"; Madan's note points to Hebrews 1:7, where God makes "his ministers a flame of fire", so these are the angels [^madan1760][^kjv]. "I'm fixed upon it": I stand firm on this hill of God's love. Madan's note points to Hebrews 12, where believers come not to the burning mount of Moses's law but to "mount Sion", the city of the living God [^madan1760][^kjv]. The Hymnology Archive reads the mount instead as Golgotha, where Jesus died [^hymnarchive]. Robinson's own last line was "Mount of God's unchanging love", with Madan's note to Malachi 3:6, "I am the LORD, I change not" [^madan1760][^kjv]. Our 1905 hymnal sings "Mount of thy redeeming love", a change of the mid-1800s whose source is unclear [^methodist1905][^hymnarchive].
2. "Here I raise my Ebenezer": I set up a stone of thanks, as Samuel did, because God has helped me this far [^kjv][^madan1760]. Then the singer remembers being a lost sheep, "wand'ring from the fold of God", whom Jesus sought and rescued "with precious blood" [^madan1760].
3. I owe grace more every day. Let it bind my heart like a "fetter", a chain, because I am "prone to wander": I know I tend to stray. The singer offers their heart and asks God to "seal it" as his own, with Madan's notes to 2 Corinthians 1:22 and Ephesians 1:13 [^madan1760][^kjv].

The 1759 text had a fourth stanza looking ahead to the day the singer is free from sin [^julian][^hymnarchive].

### The full words

Verses 1 to 3 as The Methodist Hymnal printed them in 1905, with the dashes in the first verse's seventh line written as commas [^methodist1905]. They carry Madan's changes of 1760, "his precious blood" from the Countess of Huntingdon's hymnbook of 1764, and other later wording: "Let thy goodness" for "Let that grace now", "for thy courts above" for "from", and "Mount of thy redeeming love", which the Hymnology Archive dates to the mid-1800s and Temple Melodies prints in 1851 [^madan1760][^hymnarchive][^temple1851]. Robinson's fourth stanza is as the 1759 Angel-Alley collection printed it, read from its page in the Hymnology Archive, the long s set as s [^hymnarchive]. Some modern hymnals reword the Ebenezer line too [^hymnarchive].

**Verse 1**
Come, thou Fount of every blessing,
Tune my heart to sing thy grace;
Streams of mercy, never ceasing,
Call for songs of loudest praise.
Teach me some melodious sonnet,
Sung by flaming tongues above;
Praise the mount, I'm fixed upon it,
Mount of thy redeeming love.

**Verse 2**
Here I raise mine Ebenezer;
Hither by thy help I'm come;
And I hope, by thy good pleasure,
Safely to arrive at home.
Jesus sought me when a stranger,
Wandering from the fold of God;
He, to rescue me from danger,
Interposed his precious blood.

**Verse 3**
O to grace how great a debtor
Daily I'm constrained to be!
Let thy goodness, like a fetter,
Bind my wandering heart to thee:
Prone to wander, Lord, I feel it,
Prone to leave the God I love;
Here's my heart, O take and seal it;
Seal it for thy courts above.

**Verse 4, from 1759**
O that Day when freed from Sinning,
I shall see thy lovely Face;
Cloathed then in blood-wash'd Linnen,
How I'll sing thy sov'reign Grace:
Come, my Lord, no longer tarry,
Take my ransom'd Soul away;
Send thine Angels now to carry
Me to Realms of endless Day.

## How it is sung

It is a congregational hymn, in the hymnals of many churches [^hymnary-text][^hymnary-nettleton]. Its meter is 8.7.8.7 D: eight lines of eight and seven syllables in turn [^hymnary-nettleton][^temple1851]. NETTLETON is in three, and its shape is AABA: the first two lines' tune comes back for lines 3 and 4, new music lifts lines 5 and 6, and the opening returns to close [^psh][^methodist1905].

Our arrangement reads No. 19 of The Methodist Hymnal (New York, 1905), where the tune is credited to John Wyeth and the harmony is unsigned, in E-flat as printed [^methodist1905]. We sing verse 1 for 2 to 4 singers, bright and lilting in three, with every line one breath. For beginners we changed three bass notes to avoid a wide leap, sing one two-note tenor syllable on a single note, and changed six alto notes so that two singers move in thirds and end two lines on a fuller chord [^methodist1905].

## Versions and other words

The words' first tune was JEWIN STREET, in Thomas Knibb's The Psalm Singers Help (London, about 1765), and that pairing stayed popular in Britain into the early 1800s, in John Rippon's tune book from 1792 [^hymnarchive].

Wyeth's 1813 printing already gave the tune a refrain [^hymnarchive]. The Missouri Harmony (St. Louis, 1820) reprints HALLELUJAH with it, "Hallelujah, Hallelujah, We are on our journey home", after the verse [^missouri1820]. The name NETTLETON is in Darius E. Jones's tune book Temple Melodies, dated 1851 on its title page [^temple1851]. The Hymnology Archive finds the name in an 1852 printing of it; William J. Reynolds wrote in 1976 that no one knew where it began [^hymnarchive][^hymnary-nettleton].

In William Walker's shape-note book The Southern Harmony the words go to another tune, WARRENTON, whose earliest known printing, unnamed, is in The Young Convert's Pocket Companion (Boston, 1822) [^hymnarchive]. The Hymnology Archive traces the name and the pairing to Walker's revised edition of 1838 [^hymnarchive].

## Did you know?

- Writers on hymns in 1859 and 1886 knew Robinson by just two hymns: this one and a Christmas hymn, "Mighty God, while angels bless thee" [^belcher][^duffield]. Dyer says a Christmas hymn of his was set to music by Dr. Randall, Cambridge's professor of music [^dyer][^dnb].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/come-thou-fount/soprano): you carry NETTLETON, lilting in three. Lines 1 and 2 come back as lines 3 and 4 and again as 7 and 8, so two lines teach you six. The new music, "Teach me some melodious sonnet, Sung by flaming tongues above", climbs to your top E-flat on "some" and "flaming", and ends on that E-flat for "above".
- [Alto](/songs/come-thou-fount/alto): you sing close under the tune, mostly on E-flat, holding while the tune moves above you. In the middle lines, where every part sings higher, you reach your top B-flat on "melodious" and again on "flaming", just as the tune steps down from its E-flat. Twice you drop to a low G, on "grace" and on "love" at the very end, so that with two singers those lines end on a fuller chord.
- [Tenor](/songs/come-thou-fount/tenor): you sing the words in the middle of the chords, mostly on B-flat, rocking down to A-flat and G and back. Your two high moments are E-flat on "melodious" and on "tongues above"; solo those lines in the player until the leap feels easy.
- [Bass](/songs/come-thou-fount/bass): you sing the bass line, on the chord's root most of the time, mostly E-flat and the B-flat below it. "Tune my heart", "Call for songs" and "Mount of thy" each climb E-flat, G, A-flat at the start of the line; in the middle you reach A-flat again on "melodious" and "tongues".

## Questions

### What does Ebenezer mean in Come Thou Fount?

Ebenezer means "stone of help" [^nrsvue][^easton]. In 1 Samuel 7:12 the prophet Samuel set up a stone after a victory over the Philistines and named it Ebenezer, saying "Hitherto hath the LORD helped us" [^kjv][^easton]. To "raise my Ebenezer" is to set up a marker of thanks: God has brought me this far. Martin Madan's 1760 printing keys the line to 1 Samuel 7:12, and its next line, "Hither by thine help I'm come", echoes Samuel; Robinson's 1759 line had "grace" for "help" [^madan1760][^hymnarchive].

### Who wrote the music for Come Thou Fount of Every Blessing?

No one knows. NETTLETON was printed with no composer named, as HALLELUJAH, in Wyeth's Repository of Sacred Music, Part Second (Harrisburg, 1813) [^hymnary-nettleton][^hymnarchive][^psh]. Some hymnals print it unsigned or as a traditional American melody; others credit John Wyeth, its printer, or the preacher Asahel Nettleton, its namesake, but neither is known to have composed music [^steel][^hymnary-nettleton][^hymnarchive][^methodist1905].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings NETTLETON. With two singers you have the tune and the alto, close underneath it and mostly on one note. A third singer adds the bass, mostly on the roots of the chords, and the tenor fills the middle for four [^methodist1905].
