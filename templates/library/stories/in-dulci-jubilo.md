In dulci jubilo, also spelled In dulci iubilo, is sung here in Robert Lucas Pearsall's setting of the old German Christmas carol, made for a choral society at Karlsruhe and sung there in the autumn of 1834 [^pearsall1836][^ha]. The old carol mixed Latin and German. Pearsall kept the Latin and put the German into English of his own, so his carol begins "In dulci jubilo, Let us our homage shew!" [^novello296][^ha]. Its earliest known printing came from D'Almaine in London, with a title page dated 1836 [^pearsall1836][^ha]. Later Novello sold it for threepence a copy in its Part-Song Book, in W. J. Westbrook's reduction for four voices, the version we sing [^novello296]. In 1895 the Dictionary of National Biography counted it among the finest English part-writing [^dnb-pearsall].

## Where it comes from

**The carol.** In dulci jubilo, "in sweet rejoicing", is a German Christmas song whose lines take turns between Latin and German. Its words and tune are known from manuscripts of around 1400 [^ha][^obc]. Its long history, and the English carol John Mason Neale made from it in 1853, are told on our page for [Good Christian Men, Rejoice](/songs/good-christian-men-rejoice). This page is about one man's setting of it.

**The composer.** Robert Lucas Pearsall was born at Clifton, near Bristol, on 14 March 1795 [^dnb-pearsall][^grove-pearsall][^marshall1882]. His mother wanted him to be a lawyer, and he was called to the bar in 1821 [^dnb-pearsall][^marshall1882][^grove-pearsall]. In 1825, after a slight stroke, he left England for his health and settled at Mainz, where he studied composition with Joseph Panny [^marshall1882][^dnb-pearsall][^grove-pearsall][^canterbury-pearsall]. In 1829 he came home for a year to Willsbridge House, the family seat in Gloucestershire, and in 1830 he settled with his family at Karlsruhe [^grove-pearsall][^bmb][^marshall1882][^dnb-pearsall].

**Karlsruhe, 1834.** Pearsall's preface tells the rest. He found the tune, he wrote, in a German book of 1570 made for the Protestant congregations of Zweibrücken and Neuburg, which already called it "a very ancient song" for Christmas Eve [^novello296][^pearsall1836]. He believed it was "one of those old Roman Catholic melodies which Luther, on account of their beauty, retained in the Protestant service" [^novello296]. Of the words he wrote that they were "rather remarkable, being written half in Latin and half in the upper German dialect. I have translated them to fit the music" [^novello296][^ha]. He used the tune as a cantus firmus, a fixed melody that the other voices are built around [^novello296][^pearsall1836]. He wrote the piece for the choral society at Karlsruhe, which sang it in the autumn of 1834 [^novello296][^pearsall1836][^ha]. In its first form, Westbrook later wrote, it needed eight solo voices and a chorus [^novello296].

**London, 1836 and 1837.** In 1836 Pearsall visited England and heard the madrigal societies of Bristol and London, and from then on he wrote much in the old madrigal style [^marshall1882][^grove-pearsall]. In London, D'Almaine and Co. printed his carol as A Very Ancient German Christmas Carol, "the Melody Harmonized and the Words Translated for the Use of Madrigal and Choral Societies", his Opus X. Its title page is dated 1836, and its preface is signed at Willsbridge on 31 January 1837 [^pearsall1836][^ha]. Soon after, he sold Willsbridge and went back abroad. In 1842 he bought the castle of Wartensee, on Lake Constance, where he wrote most of his later music [^dnb-pearsall][^marshall1882]. British Musical Biography (1897) gives 1832 for Wartensee instead [^bmb]. He was received into the Roman Catholic Church, and he died suddenly at Wartensee on 5 August 1856; he was buried in a vault in its chapel [^dnb-pearsall][^marshall1882][^bmb].

**"De" Pearsall.** Novello's print calls him "R. L. De Pearsall, Esq. (Of Willsbridge.)" [^novello296]. The Dictionary of National Biography, Grove's Dictionary and an 1882 memoir by Julian Marshall all say he took the "de" himself, later in life [^dnb-pearsall][^grove-pearsall][^marshall1882]. The Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology says the opposite: he never used it, and his daughter added it after his death [^canterbury-pearsall].

**Westbrook's four voices.** William Joseph Westbrook was born in London on 1 January 1831 and became organist of St Bartholomew's Church, Sydenham, in 1851 [^grove-westbrook][^bmb]. British Musical Biography dates his time there to 1884; The Musical Times, on his death, said he had served nearly forty years [^bmb][^mt1894]. He died at his home in Sydenham on 24 March 1894 [^mt1894][^bmb]. His version is No. 296 of Novello's Part-Song Book, Second Series, priced 3d. on its first page [^novello296]. His footnote explains it: the piece "in its original form, requires eight solo voices and a chorus", but many solo parts only double the chorus, so he reduced it to four voices, keeping "the composer's parts with as little alteration as possible", and added a piano part "for use at rehearsal" [^novello296]. The print is undated. Julian's Dictionary of Hymnology puts that volume in 1887, and HathiTrust catalogues the series to the 1880s [^julian][^hathi]. Julian also says the carol appeared in The Musical Times before that; we have not found that printing [^julian].

**The page.** Page 82 is a whole history in one sheet. Under the title, "An Ancient Christmas Carol", comes Pearsall's preface, then "The original words are as follow", the four Latin and German stanzas in four columns, each last line marked "Chor." [^novello296]. Below, the music starts "Moderato" in three minims to a bar, for treble, alto, tenor "(8ve. lower)" and bass over the keyboard part, with verses 1 and 2 printed under the same notes. Verses 3 and 4, on pages 84 to 88, are written out in full, with "Solo" and "Chorus" over the parts [^novello296]. One detail differs from 1837: Novello's preface says only that the music was written for and performed by the choral society at "Carlsruhe", without the autumn of 1834 [^novello296][^ha].

**In English choirs.** In 1882 Julian Marshall, writing Pearsall's memoir for The Musical Times, listed "In dulci jubilo" (four voices) among works that delight "the most cultivated musicians". Compare it, he wrote, "with that of any former master (not even excepting Bach), and Pearsall will be found to have come nearest to the true intention of both words and air" [^marshall1882]. The Cowley Carol Book of 1912 printed the tune "harmonized by R. L. de Pearsall" [^cowley1912]. In 1928 the Oxford Book of Carols named Pearsall, with the Cowley book's editor, as one who follows the old tune correctly [^obc]. His tune has no "News! News!", the two extra notes of Neale's carol [^novello296][^obc].

## What the words mean

Pearsall's four stanzas are those of Babst's Leipzig hymnal of 1545, the last made for Luther, and of later books [^ha][^obc]. Julian's Dictionary of Hymnology, of 1907, matches his text to Klug's Wittenberg hymnal of 1529 instead [^julian]. He kept every Latin line and put each German line into English, so the lines still take turns, now English and Latin [^novello296][^ha]. Verses 1 and 2 end in a Latin refrain, as the old carol does. Verses 3 and 4 end in German in the old carol, "Eia wer wir da!", and Pearsall made them English: "O that we were there!" [^novello296]. His English is free, made "to fit the music", and in places he changed the picture [^novello296].

**Verse 1.** "Let us our homage shew": let us show our worship. "Shew" is the old spelling of show [^webster-show]. Where the German says "now sing and be glad", Pearsall writes homage [^novello296]. "Our heart's joy reclineth in præsepio": the one our hearts delight in lies in a manger; to recline is to lie at rest [^webster-recline][^obc]. The German says he shines like the sun in his mother's lap; Pearsall made the sun a bright star [^novello296][^obc]. The refrain, "Alpha es et O!", tells the child he is Alpha and Omega, the first and last letters of the Greek alphabet, as in Revelation: "I am Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the ending, saith the Lord" [^kjv][^obc].

**Verse 2.** A prayer to the child. "My heart is sore for Thee": I ache with longing for you, close to the German "Nach dir ist mir so Weh" [^novello296][^ha]. The Latin calls him "puer optime", best of boys, and "princeps gloriæ", prince of glory [^obc]. "Trahe me post te!" means "draw me after you!" [^obc].

**Verse 3.** The love of the Father and the gentleness of the Son. "Deeply were we stained per nostra crimina": our sins had stained us deeply; the German says we would all have been lost. "But Thou hast for us gained cœlorum gaudia": you have won for us the joys of heaven [^novello296][^obc]. "O that we were there!": if only we were there.

**Verse 4.** "Ubi sunt gaudia ... If that they be not there?": where are joys, if not there? Pearsall turns the German's statement, nowhere but there, into a question [^novello296][^ha]. There angels sing "nova cantica", new songs, and bells ring "in Regis curia", in the King's court [^obc].

### The full words

These are Pearsall's four verses as Novello's Part-Song Book No. 296 prints them under the music, pages 82 to 88, with its spellings, "shew", "præsepio" and "Cœlorum" [^novello296]. In verses 3 and 4 the parts do not all sing the same words: the treble sings "Deep were we stained", and the other voices repeat some words. The lines below follow the tenor in verse 3 and the treble in verse 4. Each refrain is printed twice in verses 1 and 2; in verses 3 and 4 the voices repeat theirs. The Latin lines are given with our own English. Pearsall died in 1856 and Westbrook in 1894, so all of it is in the public domain.

## How it is sung

It is a carol for Christmas. In Germany, Pearsall wrote, the old song had been sung in processions on Christmas Eve, and still was in remote parts of the country [^novello296][^pearsall1836]. His setting is not a hymn for a congregation but a piece for a choir. In verses 1 and 2 of Westbrook's print the old tune lies in the treble, in a steady triple time, without the "News! News!" of Neale's carol [^novello296][^obc]. Verses 1 and 2 are sung by all four voices to the same music, soft at "Our heart's joy" and swelling at "And like a bright star" [^novello296]. Verse 3 changes the sound: the tenor begins "O patris charitas" alone, the treble and alto follow as solos, and the bass waits until the chorus comes in, "O that we were there" [^novello296]. Verse 4 opens loudly, the alto first and then the other voices, "Ubi sunt gaudia". Soloists sing softly of the angels and the bells; the chorus answers loudly, "O that we were there"; the angels and the bells come round once more, and the piece ends, growing softer [^novello296].

Our arrangement sings verses 1 and 2, read from pages 82 to 84 of No. 296, in its key of F [^novello296]. Westbrook writes three minims to a bar; we write the same music in 3/4. The soprano sings the old tune note for note, and the bass is Westbrook's. The alto and tenor follow his chords, revoiced in places so that two or three singers still hear a full chord, and the long notes give up a beat for a breath at the end of each line. Verses 3 and 4, with their solos and voices entering one after another, are left out. It is for 2 to 4 singers: two take soprano and alto, a third adds the bass, and the tenor makes four.

## Did you know?

- "Trahe me post te" echoes the Song of Songs. In the Latin Bible a verse begins "Trahe me, post te curremus", draw me, we will run after you; the King James Version has "Draw me, we will run after thee" [^vulgate][^kjv].
- Pearsall translated more than this carol. He put Schiller's Wilhelm Tell, and Faust, into English verse [^dnb-pearsall][^marshall1882].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/in-dulci-jubilo/soprano): the old tune, from low D to top D, most often on F, A and C. On "lo!" and "shew!" ("le!" and "Thee!" in verse 2) it holds C, steps up to D and back on the one syllable. Its top D also comes on "heart's" and on "a" in "like a bright star" ("me" in "Hear me" and "-er" in "prayer" in verse 2). Its low D comes only at the start of the first refrain, "Al-pha" ("Tra-he"). At the end of that first refrain, "O!" ("te!") holds F for three beats, then leaps up to C on the same vowel.
- [Alto](/songs/in-dulci-jubilo/alto): under the tune, from low A to top A, most often on A and C. Its top A comes on "lo!", "shew!", "Our" and "like" ("le!", "Thee!", "Hear" and "pray-" in verse 2). In the first refrain it climbs by half steps, C, C-sharp, D, on "es et O!" ("me post te!"); the C-sharp is outside the key, so tune it carefully. It then rises to E while the tune leaps up.
- [Tenor](/songs/in-dulci-jubilo/tenor): the middle of the chord, on F nearly half the time. It dips to E on "pi-" and "mi-" ("ti-" and "ri-" in verse 2), then holds F for four beats on the "o!" of "In præsepio!" and five on the "o!" of "Matris in gremio!". Its top D comes on "bi-" in "jubilo" and "age" in "homage" ("vu-" and "for"). Its low C comes only on the song's last note, "te!".
- [Bass](/songs/in-dulci-jubilo/bass): the bass line, on F half the time. The first two lines, "In dulci jubilo! Let us our homage shew!", are twelve syllables on the same F; it moves to C only at the end of "shew!". In the first refrain it walks down by step, F, F, E, E, D, C, under "Alpha es et O!". Its low F comes only twice, on the last "O!" of verse 1 and the song's last "te!".

## Questions

### What does In dulci jubilo mean?

It means "in sweet rejoicing", or in sweet jubilation [^obc][^ha]. The old carol goes on in German and Latin: our hearts' delight lies in a manger and shines in his mother's lap, and he is Alpha and Omega [^obc][^novello296]. Pearsall's English keeps the Latin lines, so the whole carol, with our English for each Latin line, is above under "The full words".

### Who wrote In dulci jubilo?

Nobody knows who wrote the old carol; its words and tune are known from manuscripts of about 1400 [^ha][^obc]. This setting, with its English words, is by Robert Lucas Pearsall, an English composer living at Karlsruhe, where it was sung in the autumn of 1834 [^pearsall1836][^ha]. Its earliest known printing is D'Almaine's in London, with a title page dated 1836 [^pearsall1836]. The four-voice version is by W. J. Westbrook, organist at Sydenham [^novello296][^grove-westbrook].

### Is Pearsall's In dulci jubilo for SATB choir?

Westbrook's version is. Pearsall wrote it for eight solo voices and a chorus [^pearsall1836]; Westbrook reduced it for treble, alto, tenor and bass, the voices of an SATB choir, keeping Pearsall's parts "with as little alteration as possible" [^novello296]. Verses 1 and 2 are full four-part harmony; verses 3 and 4 add solos. Our arrangement takes verses 1 and 2 for 2 to 4 singers.

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano. It sings the old tune note for note as Westbrook's treble prints it [^novello296]. Pearsall built his setting around that tune, so the alto, tenor and bass move under it while it holds its long notes. With two singers, take soprano and alto; a third adds the bass, and a fourth the tenor.
