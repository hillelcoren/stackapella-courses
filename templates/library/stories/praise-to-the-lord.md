Praise to the Lord, the Almighty, the King of Creation is the English form of Lobe den Herren, den mächtigen König der Ehren, a German hymn of thanks by Joachim Neander, a Reformed preacher and teacher from Bremen [^julian][^ndb]. It was printed in Bremen in 1680, the year Neander died, aged about 30 [^neander1680][^adb][^suub]. Its tune, LOBE DEN HERREN, is older: its earliest known printing is in a Stralsund hymnbook of 1665 [^zahn][^dli]. Bach built a whole cantata on it in 1725 [^bachvereniging][^durr]. English speakers sing Catherine Winkworth's translation of 1863 [^cbe1863][^julian]. And the valley near Düsseldorf where Neander walked, later named after him, gave its name to the Neanderthals [^kuladig][^si].

## Where it comes from

**The poet.** Neander was born in Bremen in 1650 and studied at its Gymnasium illustre from 1666, where the preaching of the pietist Theodor Undereyck turned him toward a more personal faith [^julian-neander][^ndb]. He studied at Heidelberg as a tutor, and joined Spener's pietist circle in Frankfurt [^julian-neander][^ndb]. In 1674 he became rector of the Reformed Latin school in Düsseldorf, which also made him a preacher there [^julian-neander][^adb][^suub]. His private prayer meetings, and his staying away from public worship, led to a short suspension and a signed submission in February 1677; the NDB dates the suspension to 1676 [^julian-neander][^adb][^ndb]. In 1679 he went home to Bremen as an assistant preacher at St Martin's Church [^julian-neander][^adb][^suub]. Within a year he fell ill, with what, the ADB says, we do not know, and he died there on 31 May 1680, Whit Monday [^adb][^ndb][^suub]. The ADB gives his age as 30, the Bremen library as 29 [^adb][^suub].

**The words.** The hymn's earliest known printing is in Neander's book of 57 hymns, A & Ω. Joachimi Neandri Glaub- und Liebes-übung, printed in Bremen by Herman Brauer in 1680 [^julian-neander][^ndb][^neander1680]. Its title calls them covenant songs and "Danck-Psalmen", psalms of thanks, "Zu lesen und zu singen auff Reisen / zu Hauß oder bey Christen-Ergetzungen im Grünen": to read and sing on journeys, at home, or at Christian outings in the green [^suub][^neander1680]. Some later books date the hymn 1679, but the ADB knows of no edition before 1680 [^adb][^cbe1863].

**What the 1680 page shows.** The hymn fills pages 46 to 49 [^neander1680]. Page 46, headed "Der Lobende", the one who praises, prints the tune in triple time with a figured bass beneath it [^neander1680]. Facing it, page 47 gives the verse the hymn grows from, "Ps. CIII. 1.", in German: praise the Lord, my soul, and all that is in me his holy name [^neander1680][^kjv]. Then comes "Mel. Hastu dann JEsu dein Angesicht &c.", sing it to the tune of "Hast du denn, Jesu, dein Angesicht", and stanzas 1 and 2 [^neander1680][^hymnarchive]. Page 48 prints the music again under Psalm 103's first verse in Hebrew, and page 49 has stanzas 3 to 5 [^neander1680].

**The tune.** The melody was older. Its earliest known printing is in the second part of a Stralsund hymnbook of 1665, to a hymn Zahn gives as beginning "Hast du denn, Liebster, dein Angesicht gänzlich verborgen", have you then, dearest, wholly hidden your face [^zahn][^dli][^hymnarchive]. Neander adapted it to his words [^julian][^hymnary-text]. The NDB calls the hymn one Neander set to music himself, and he did compose some of the book's tunes, about half by the ADB's count; but this one, the hymnologists show, came from Stralsund [^ndb][^adb][^zahn]. No composer is known [^zahn][^hymnarchive].

**Bach.** In Leipzig, Johann Sebastian Bach made the hymn a cantata, BWV 137, for the twelfth Sunday after Trinity, 19 August 1725, setting all five stanzas unchanged, one to each movement [^bachvereniging][^durr].

**Into English.** Catherine Winkworth made the version we sing. It is No. 9 of her Chorale Book for England (London, 1863), in its section "Praise and Thanksgiving" [^cbe1863][^julian][^hc-winkworth]. The book gave each hymn its German tune, edited by William Sterndale Bennett and Otto Goldschmidt [^hymnarchive][^hc-winkworth].

**In America.** In 1744 Christoph Saur printed the German hymn in Germantown, Pennsylvania, as No. 286 of Das Kleine Davidische Psalterspiel der Kinder Zions [^psalter1744][^upenn]. American Lutheran and Reformed hymnals later printed several English versions, among them J. H. Good's (a full version by Julian's account) in the Ohio Lutheran hymnal of 1880 [^julian][^hymnarchive]. It was a hymn of thanks from the start: Julian files it under "Thanksgiving", and the NDB calls it a "Danklied", a song of thanks [^julian][^ndb]. The Augustana Lutheran hymnal of 1926, from Rock Island, Illinois, indexes it for "A Day of Thanksgiving or a Harvest Festival", and Lutheran Worship (1982) for the Day of Thanksgiving [^hoos1926][^lw1982]. The sources we read do not say when it first became a hymn for the American holiday.

**Myth and record.** Often told: Neander lived for months in a cave in a valley near Düsseldorf and wrote his hymns there. What the record shows: Julian thinks some may have been written in a cave there, but calls the months of living in one without foundation [^julian-neander]. The ADB calls the homeless summer a legend; walks in the valley may have inspired some hymns [^adb].

**The valley and the fossil.** That valley of the Düssel was later named Neanderthal after him [^ndb][^kuladig]. In August 1856 quarry workers in its Kleine Feldhofer Grotte found the bones named Homo neanderthalensis, the Neanderthals, after the valley [^kuladig][^si].

## What the words mean

**Two psalms.** Neander built the hymn on Psalm 103 and Psalm 150 [^julian][^neander1680]. Psalm 103 begins: "Bless the Lord, O my soul: and all that is within me, bless his holy name", then "forget not all his benefits" [^kjv]. Psalm 150, the last psalm, calls for praise on every instrument and ends "Let every thing that hath breath praise the Lord" [^kjv]. Neander's last stanza borrows from both almost word for word [^neander1680][^kjv].

**The English we sing.** Winkworth made four stanzas from Neander's five. Hymnology Archive and the Psalter Hymnal Handbook say she left out his third, about God's gift of health and his care in trouble; Julian says the fourth [^hymnarchive][^hymnary-text][^julian]. Set side by side, the texts favour the third: her third stanza's "Ponder anew What the Almighty can do" is his fourth's "Dencke daran, Was der Allmächtige kan", and nothing of his third is in hers [^cbe1863][^neander1680].

**Verse 1.** "Praise to the Lord! the Almighty, the King of creation!" Neander's König der Ehren is the King of glory [^neander1680]. "O my soul, praise Him, for He is thy health and salvation" speaks to the singer's own soul, as Psalm 103 does [^cbe1863][^kjv]. "All ye who hear, Now to His temple draw near" calls everyone to God's house. Neander instead wakes the "Psalter und Harffe", psaltery and harp, as in Psalm 108 [^neander1680][^kjv]. A psaltery is a plucked string instrument [^dli].

**Verse 2.** God "Shelters thee under His wings, yea so gently sustaineth": holds you up [^cbe1863]. Neander's God has led you safely on eagle's wings, the image of Deuteronomy 32, where the eagle carries its young [^neander1680][^kjv]. "Hast thou not seen" asks the singer to look back at prayers "Granted in what He ordaineth", in what God arranges [^cbe1863].

**Verse 3 (Neander's fourth).** God "doth prosper thy work and defend thee", and his goodness and mercy go with you daily, words from Psalm 23 [^cbe1863][^hymnary-text]. "Ponder anew": think again what the Almighty can do [^cbe1863].

**Verse 4.** "All that hath life and breath, come now with praises before Him" is Psalm 150's last verse [^cbe1863][^kjv]. Neander adds "mit Abrahams Samen", with Abraham's children, God's people; Winkworth leaves Abraham out [^neander1680][^hymnarchive]. Her "Let the Amen" ends where his does: "Lobende, schliesse mit Amen", you who praise, close with Amen, answering the page's heading, Der Lobende [^cbe1863][^neander1680].

### Neander's German

Here is Neander's German as printed in Bremen in 1680, on pages 47 and 49 of the Glaub- und Liebes-Übung, with our own English line by line [^neander1680]. The spelling is his printer's: HErren and GOtt mark God's name in capitals, and the slashes are commas.

**Verse 1**
> Lobe den HErren / den mächtigen König der Ehren/
Praise the Lord, the mighty King of glory,
> Meine geliebete Seele / das ist mein Begehren/
my dearly loved soul: that is what I long for.
> Kommet zu Hauff/
Come, gather together;
> Psalter und Harffe wach't auff/
psaltery and harp, wake up;
> Lasset die Musicam hören.
let the music be heard.

**Verse 2**
> Lobe den HErren / der alles so herrlich regieret/
Praise the Lord, who rules all things so gloriously,
> Der dich auff Adelers Fittigen sicher geführet/
who has led you safely on eagle's wings,
> Der dich erhält/
who keeps you
> Wie es dir immer gefällt/
in just the way you could wish:
> Hastu nicht dieses verspüret.
have you not felt this?

**Verse 3**
> Lobe den HErren / der künstlich und fein dich bereitet/
Praise the Lord, who made you with skill and care,
> Der dir Gesundheit verliehen / dich freundlich geleitet/
who gave you health and kindly led you.
> In wie viel Noht/
In how much trouble
> Hat nicht der gnädige GOtt
has not the gracious God
> Uber dir Flügel gebreitet.
spread his wings over you?

**Verse 4**
> Lobe den HErren / der deinen Stand sichtbahr gesegnet/
Praise the Lord, who has visibly blessed your place in life,
> Der aus dem Himmel mit Strömen der Liebe geregnet/
who has rained from heaven in streams of love.
> Dencke daran/
Think of it:
> Was der Allmächtige kan/
what the Almighty can do,
> Der dir mit Liebe begegnet.
who meets you with love.

**Verse 5**
> Lobe den HErren / was in mir ist / lobe den Namen/
Praise the Lord, all that is in me; praise his name.
> Alles was Othem hat / lobe mit Abrahams Samen/
All that has breath, praise with Abraham's children.
> Er ist dein Licht/
He is your light;
> Seele / vergiß es ja nicht/
soul, never forget it.
> Lobende / schliesse mit Amen.
You who praise, close with Amen.

### The full words

These are Winkworth's four stanzas as she printed them in The Chorale Book for England (1863), No. 9, a free version of Neander's stanzas 1, 2, 4 and 5.

**Verse 1**
Praise to the Lord! the Almighty, the King of creation!
O my soul, praise Him, for He is thy health and salvation!
All ye who hear,
Now to His temple draw near,
Join me in glad adoration!

**Verse 2**
Praise to the Lord! who o'er all things so wondrously reigneth,
Shelters thee under His wings, yea so gently sustaineth;
Hast thou not seen
How thy desires have been
Granted in what He ordaineth?

**Verse 3**
Praise to the Lord! who doth prosper thy work and defend thee,
Surely His goodness and mercy here daily attend thee;
Ponder anew
What the Almighty can do,
If with His love He befriend thee!

**Verse 4**
Praise to the Lord! Oh let all that is in me adore Him!
All that hath life and breath, come now with praises before Him!
Let the Amen
Sound from His people again,
Gladly for aye we adore Him!

## How it is sung

It spread through Reformed, Pietist and Lutheran hymnbooks in the 1700s and 1800s, and at last into Catholic ones [^suub][^julian]. Today it is No. 316 in the German Protestant hymnal, the Evangelisches Gesangbuch, and No. 392 in the Catholic Gotteslob [^eg][^dli]. English-speaking churches sing Winkworth's words, often with a newer stanza added [^hymnarchive][^hymnary-text]. Hymnals call the tune LOBE DEN HERREN; The English Hymnal of 1906 named it after the older song, HAST DU DENN, JESU [^hymnary-text][^eh1906]. Its meter is 14.14.4.7.8: two long lines, two short ones and a closing line [^zahn][^cbe1863].

We sing verses 1 and 2 of Winkworth's English, in four parts adapted from the harmony printed with them in The Chorale Book for England in 1863 [^cbe1863][^hymnarchive]. We keep the book's key, G, but write its broad 3/2 in 3/4. The soprano sings the tune as printed and the bass is mostly as printed; the alto and tenor are reworked so that two singers, soprano and alto, sing in thirds and sixths, and the bass joins them for three. Where the page's lower parts move under a held note of the tune, we keep one note, and breaths are written in at the ends of lines.

## Versions and other words

- **The 1744 Pennsylvania text:** Saur's Germantown book has "Seele und Hertze wach auf, lasset das Lob und Danck hören", soul and heart awake, let praise and thanks be heard, where Neander had psaltery and harp and music, and gives its tune as that of "Christe, mein Leben, mein Hoffen" [^psalter1744][^neander1680].
- **The English Hymnal (1906):** No. 536, credited to Winkworth "and others", with stanzas added to hers [^eh1906][^hymnarchive].

## Did you know?

- Bach later turned the cantata's alto aria into an organ piece, one of his six Schübler chorales, BWV 650. [^bachvereniging][^durr]

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/praise-to-the-lord/soprano): you sing the tune. The two long opening lines each start on G, leap up to D on "the" in "Praise to the Lord!" and walk down the scale to a low D on "the" before "King", then climb back to a held A on "cre-a-tion". Your top note is the E on "hear" (and "seen" in verse 2), after three Ds on "All ye who". "Join me in glad adoration" climbs step by step from low D.
- [Alto](/songs/praise-to-the-lord/alto): you sing mostly a third under the tune, following it down the scale to a low B on "the" before "King" and back up to a held F-sharp on "cre-a-", then dropping to B for "tion!". You rise to your top B on "All" and "who", and on "tem-" of "temple". Three of each verse's five lines start and end on that low B.
- [Tenor](/songs/praise-to-the-lord/tenor): you sing mostly B and D, with Gs in the middle of each long line, and hold a C on "cre-a-" and "sal-va-". Your top note is the E on "to" and "ple" in "Now to His temple", and on "thy" in verse 2. The end is the tricky part: you drop to a low D on "dain-" and hold it right through the last "eth", six beats; solo it in the player to keep it in tune.
- [Bass](/songs/praise-to-the-lord/bass): you sing the low line, moving on almost every word. The two long opening lines start and end on the high G, touching the low G on "the" before "King" and holding D on "cre-a-". "All ye who hear" falls from G to C on "hear", and verse 1 ends on the low G, held, under "tion!", as the whole song does under "eth?".

## Questions

### Which psalm is "Praise to the Lord, the Almighty" based on?

Mostly Psalm 103, with Psalm 150 at the end. Neander printed Psalm 103's first verse above the hymn, and Julian's Dictionary of Hymnology says it is founded on Psalm 103, verses 1 to 6, and Psalm 150 [^neander1680][^julian]. The last stanza's "All that hath life and breath" is Psalm 150's closing verse, and the eagle's wings recall Deuteronomy 32 [^neander1680][^kjv].

### What does "Lobe den Herren" mean?

"Lobe den Herren" means "praise the Lord". Lobe is a command, spoken by the singer to their own soul, as in Psalm 103 [^neander1680][^kjv]. The full first line, "Lobe den Herren, den mächtigen König der Ehren", means "praise the Lord, the mighty King of glory". Each of Neander's five stanzas begins with the same three words [^neander1680].

### Is "Praise to the Lord, the Almighty" a Catholic hymn?

It began as a Protestant hymn and is now sung by both. Neander was a Reformed, that is Calvinist, preacher touched by Pietism, and Catholic hymnbooks took the hymn up last [^ndb][^julian-neander][^suub]. Today it is No. 392 in the Gotteslob, the Catholic hymnal of the German-speaking dioceses, and No. 316 in the German Protestant hymnal [^dli][^eg].

### Is "Praise to the Lord, the Almighty" in the public domain?

Its old words and tune are. Neander's German was printed in 1680, the tune in 1665, and Winkworth's English in 1863; she died in 1878 [^neander1680][^zahn][^dli][^cbe1863][^hc-winkworth]. Check the version you sing, though: hymnals often add a newer stanza in place of the one she left out, and modern harmonies, arrangements and translations can be under copyright [^hymnarchive][^hymnary-text].
