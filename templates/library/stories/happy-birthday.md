Happy Birthday to You, often just called Happy Birthday, is the song a group sings to someone on their birthday, putting the person's name into the third line. Its tune began as Good Morning to All, a classroom greeting composed by Mildred J. Hill to words by her sister Patty Smith Hill, and published in Chicago in 1893 in their book Song Stories for the Kindergarten [^order2015][^filson][^brauneis]. The birthday words came later and nobody signed them: their earliest known printing, in a Methodist Sunday school handbook of 1911, names no author [^ew1911][^order2015]. For decades a music publisher charged fees for using the song [^brauneis][^order2015], until a US federal court ruled in 2015 that the copyright it claimed did not cover the words [^order2015][^kqed].

## Where it comes from

**Two sisters in Louisville.** Mildred Jane Hill was born in 1859 and Patty Smith Hill in 1868, two of the six children of William Wallace Hill and Martha Smith Hill of Kentucky [^brauneis][^wwh][^jstordaily]. Patty trained as a kindergarten teacher in Louisville under Anna E. Bryan and finished in 1889; in 1893 she succeeded Bryan at the head of the city's kindergarten training work [^brauneis][^wwh]. She later taught at Teachers College, Columbia University, until 1935 [^brauneis][^wwh][^tclib]. Mildred was a musician and composer, an accomplished pianist and organist [^brauneis][^wwh][^jstordaily].

![[1]]

**Songs tried on children.** The sisters began writing songs for the kindergarten in 1889 [^brauneis][^order2015]. In a 1935 deposition Patty described the method: she tried each new song with the children the next morning, and if it lay beyond their voices the sisters changed it that night, again and again, until even the youngest could learn it with ease [^order2015][^brauneis]. Good Morning to All was one of the earliest, she said [^order2015][^brauneis].

**The book.** Clayton F. Summy of Chicago published the songs in 1893 as Song Stories for the Kindergarten [^order2015][^filson]. One law professor's study gives 1894 in a few places but 1893 in most [^brauneis]. We found no scan of the 1893 printing, so we read the "New Edition, Revised, Illustrated and Enlarged", copyright 1896 by Clayton F. Summy [^hill1896]. Its title page credits "Music Composed and Arranged by Mildred J. Hill" and "Words Written and Adapted by Patty S. Hill", and Mildred thanks Calvin B. Cady for his careful revision of the songs [^hill1896]. The first song, on page 3, is "Good-Morning to All", marked "Brightly", in G major and 3/4 time with a piano part [^hill1896].

![[2]]

**The birthday words.** No one knows for certain who first sang "Happy birthday to you" to the tune. The earliest known printing of the full words is in The Elementary Worker and His Work, a teacher-training text by Alice Jacobs and Ermina C. Lincoln, copyright 1911 by the Board of Sunday Schools of the Methodist Episcopal Church [^ew1911][^order2015]. Its model program for a beginners' class has the children sing "Good morning to you", welcome newcomers with "A welcome to you", and then, after the class has counted aloud as a four-year-old named John drops his pennies in the bank, sing "Happy birthday, dear John", each to be sung "to the same tune as the Good Morning" [^ew1911]. It names no author for the words [^ew1911][^order2015]. A note after the program sends teachers to "Song Stories for the Sunday School," by Patty Hill, for its songs [^ew1911][^order2015].

**Songbooks and hymnals.** The Beginners' Book of Songs, a paperback from the Cable Company, a Chicago piano maker, copyright 1912, prints the tune as "Good-Morning to You" with "Good-bye to you" and "Happy birthday to you" as other titles beneath the heading [^bbos1912][^brauneis]. A 2009 law study named the Cable book as the earliest dated book pairing the tune with the birthday words; it does not mention the 1911 handbook [^brauneis]. In 1924 the Baptist hymnal editor Robert H. Coleman printed the birthday words as a second verse in Harvest Hymns, and his Modern Hymnal of 1926 does the same [^order2015][^brauneis][^mh1926]. None of these books names an author for the birthday words [^order2015][^brauneis].

**Claims and courts.** After the song was sung in the stage show As Thousands Cheer, their sister Jessica Hill sued its producers in 1934, claiming the tune of Good Morning to All; she made no claim to the words [^order2015][^brauneis]. In 1935 the Clayton F. Summy Company registered two arrangements titled "Happy Birthday to You" with words: E51988, an arrangement with what the application called "revised text", and E51990, an easy piano solo "with text" by Preston Ware Orem, arranging for the company [^order2015][^brauneis]. In 1944, settling a later suit, the Hill sisters' foundation assigned its rights to Summy, whose rights later passed to Warner/Chappell Music [^order2015][^brauneis].

**The 2015 ruling.** Film makers and a musician who had paid licence fees sued Warner/Chappell [^order2015][^kqed]. On 22 September 2015 the court's chief judge ruled that Summy had received from the Hill sisters the rights to the melody and to piano arrangements, never to the words, so Warner/Chappell did not own a valid copyright in them [^order2015][^ipwatchdog2016]. The order also notes that the copyright in Song Stories, and so in Good Morning to All, had expired in 1949 [^order2015][^brauneis]. In June 2016 the court approved a settlement in which Warner/Chappell agreed to pay back $14 million, and reports of the final judgment say it declared the song in the public domain [^abc7][^kqed][^ipwatchdog2016].

**Myth and record.** Often told: the Hill sisters wrote Happy Birthday to You, and a court confirmed their ownership in the 1930s. What the record shows: the tune and the Good Morning words are the Hills', from 1893, but the birthday words appear in print from 1911 with no author, and Patty Hill first claimed them in her 1935 deposition [^order2015][^brauneis]. The court read that testimony as her claim to have written them; one law professor reads the same passage as stopping short of saying she wrote the birthday words [^order2015][^brauneis]. No court in the 1930s ruled on who owned the song; none of the suits of those years reached a final judgment [^order2015][^brauneis]. In 2015 the court said a jury might believe Patty wrote the words, or that someone else did [^order2015].

## What the words mean

The song is a wish of happiness for one person on their birthday, sung by everyone else. Three of its four lines are the same; the third turns to the person by name.

It is a greeting song with its first words changed. Patty Hill's original, as printed in 1896, is a morning greeting between a kindergarten teacher and her class: good morning to you, good morning, dear children, good morning to all [^hill1896]. Sunday school teachers swapped in other first words to welcome a new child or wish one a happy birthday [^ew1911], and Patty Hill remembered her own school singing it as "Good-bye to you", "Happy Journey to You", "Happy Christmas to You" and "Happy Vacation to You" [^order2015][^brauneis].

Our words are the birthday verse as Robert H. Coleman printed it in 1926, with "dear friend" in place of his "dear children", so the song can be sent to anyone [^mh1926]. His page sets four verses to the tune; "'Tis" in the last verse means "it is".

### The full words

These are the four verses of "Good Morning to You!", No. 392 in Coleman's Modern Hymnal (Dallas, 1926), as printed. The page names no author; the book was printed before 1931, and the Hill sisters, who made the tune and the Good Morning words, died in 1916 and 1946.

**Verse 1**
Good morning to you,
Good morning to you,
Good morning, dear children,
Good morning to you!

**Verse 2**
Happy birthday to you,
Happy birthday to you,
Happy birthday, dear children,
Happy birthday to you!

**Verse 3**
A welcome to you,
A welcome to you,
A welcome, dear children,
A welcome to you!

**Verse 4**
'Tis love brings us here,
'Tis love brings us here,
'Tis love, dear children,
'Tis love brings us here.

## How it is sung

The 1911 Sunday school program shows the birthday custom early: the class counts the pennies the birthday child drops in the bank, claps the years "and one to grow on", and sings to the child by name, and a birthday card follows [^ew1911]. In the early 1930s it was heard in a run of films, and from 1933 in a Broadway revue [^order2015][^brauneis].

Our arrangement is one verse in G and 3/4, the key and time of both the 1896 and the 1926 printings [^hill1896][^mh1926]. It follows Coleman's tune note for note, with small changes: the pickups on "Hap-py" are dotted where his print has even notes, "you" at the end of the first two lines is shortened for a breath, "friend" takes two notes, F-sharp then E, and there are no held pauses, which his page marks on "chil-dren" and the last note [^mh1926]. It is for 2 to 4 singers: soprano and alto; then the bass; then the tenor.

## Did you know?

- The 1896 Song Stories prints a performing note above the tune: "To be sung standing" [^hill1896].
- In ruling that Summy's piano arrangements did not carry the words with them, the court wrote: "Obviously, pianos do not sing." [^order2015][^ipwatchdog2016]

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/happy-birthday/soprano): you carry the tune everyone knows, from the D above middle C up to the D an octave higher. The big moment is the third line: "Hap-py" sits on the low D and "birth-" leaps a full octave to the top D, before the line comes down to "dear friend", where "friend" takes two notes. Then the last line starts on C and settles home; hold the last "you" for a full bar.
- [Alto](/songs/happy-birthday/alto): you sing the words mostly a third or a sixth below the tune, keeping to B and C for most of it, low and easy. Your lowest note, the A below middle C, comes on "day" in the second line, and your highest, E, on "Hap-py" at the start of the last line.
- [Tenor](/songs/happy-birthday/tenor): you sing the words mostly on G, with F-sharp through most of the second line, an easy part to hold. In the third line you rise to B on "day, dear", and the last "you" drops to a low D, held for the whole bar.
- [Bass](/songs/happy-birthday/bass): you hold the bass line on "oo", just nine notes, mostly long, on G, C and D. You come in after the pickup, under "birth-", on a low G held for the bar; your only C comes under "friend", and you end on the low G.

## Questions

### Who owned the copyright to Happy Birthday?

The Clayton F. Summy Company of Chicago, publisher of the Hill sisters' songbook, registered arrangements titled "Happy Birthday to You" in 1935, and the company's rights later passed to Warner/Chappell Music, which charged fees to use the song [^order2015][^brauneis]. In 2015 a federal court in California ruled that Summy had only ever received rights to the melody and to piano arrangements, so Warner/Chappell did not own a valid copyright in the words [^order2015][^ipwatchdog2016].

### Is Happy Birthday still under copyright?

In the United States, the record says no: the copyright in Good Morning to All, which covered the tune, expired in 1949, and in 2015 a federal court ruled that the copyright Warner/Chappell claimed did not cover the words [^order2015][^brauneis]. In 2016 the court approved a settlement in which Warner/Chappell agreed to pay back $14 million, and reports say its final judgment declared the song in the public domain [^abc7][^kqed].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune all the way through. With two singers, the alto joins mostly a third or a sixth below; with three, the bass adds long low notes on "oo"; with four, the tenor fills in the middle, mostly on G. Everyone but the bass sings the words.
