Home on the Range, also sung as "Oh, Give Me a Home Where the Buffalo Roam", is the American song of the open plains and the state song of Kansas [^kansapedia][^kpr][^mechem]. Its words began as My Western Home, a poem by Brewster Higley, a doctor who had homesteaded in Smith County, Kansas, in 1871; the tune is credited to his neighbour Daniel E. Kelley [^mechem][^kansapedia][^kpr]. Cowboys carried it along the cattle trails, and John Lomax printed it as A Home on the Range in 1910, with no author named [^lomax1910][^mechem]. Twenty-four years later an Arizona couple sued for half a million dollars, saying it was theirs. The search that followed led back to Higley's poem [^mechem][^kansapedia][^cooper].

## Where it comes from

**A doctor on Beaver Creek.** Brewster Higley, born in Ohio, had practised medicine in Indiana before he took up a homestead in Smith County, Kansas, in 1871 [^mechem][^kansapedia][^ohs-marker]. He lived first in a dugout and later in a log cabin on Beaver Creek [^kansapedia][^mechem]. There, about 1873, he wrote a poem in praise of the prairie, My Western Home [^mechem][^kansapedia-higley]. The local paper, the Smith County Pioneer, printed it, but that issue no longer survives. The paper's own reprint of 1914 dates it to 1873, as does the Kansas Historical Society's entry on Higley; its entry on the song says early 1874 [^mechem][^kansapedia-higley][^kansapedia].

![[2]]

**The tune.** Daniel E. Kelley, a Civil War veteran, had settled at Gaylord, in Smith County, in 1872 [^mechem][^kansapedia][^iagenweb]. He is credited with setting Higley's words to music [^kansapedia][^kpr][^mechem]. Kelley played in the Harlan orchestra with his wife and her brothers, and the song became a favourite at their dances [^kansapedia][^kpr][^mechem]. Nobody is known to have written the tune down: as Kirke Mechem of the Kansas Historical Society put it in 1949, there is no reason to think Kelley ever did [^mechem].

**In the papers.** The Kirwin Chief, of Kirwin in Phillips County, printed the poem on 21 March 1874 [^kansapedia][^kansapedia-higley][^mechem]. On 26 February 1876 it ran the poem again on its front page [^kansapedia][^mechem]. The heading was "PLAGIARISM": another paper had printed the poem under someone else's name, and the Chief set the record straight, crediting "Dr. B. Higley, of Beaver creek, Smith county, Kansas" [^mechem]. That 1876 page is the earliest copy Mechem could find, titled simply Western Home [^mechem]. In it the line we know as "Where seldom is heard a discouraging word" reads "never is heard", as in the Pioneer's 1914 reprint [^mechem].

![[3]]

**Down the trail.** In 1908 the Texas collector John Lomax recorded it in San Antonio from a Black man who kept a beer saloon there and had been a cook on cattle drives up the Chisholm Trail [^mechem][^kansapedia][^texascoop]. Henry Lebermann, a blind music teacher at the state school for the blind in Austin, took the tune down from Lomax's recording, listening through earphones [^mechem][^corcoran]. Lomax printed words and tune as A Home on the Range in Cowboy Songs and Other Frontier Ballads, published in November 1910, naming no author or composer [^lomax1910][^lomax1929].

![[4]]

**A radio hit and a lawsuit.** Sheet music followed: one edition from San Antonio in 1925, and in 1930 a new arrangement by David Guion of Dallas [^mechem][^kansapedia]. By the early 1930s it was a radio favourite, said to be President Franklin D. Roosevelt's too [^mechem][^kansapedia]. Then, in 1934, William and Mary Goodwin of Tempe, Arizona, sued for $500,000, against the National Broadcasting Company and many publishers [^mechem][^ljworld]. They said it copied a song of theirs registered for copyright in 1905, which Mechem calls An Arizona Home and the Historical Society calls My Arizona Home [^mechem][^kansapedia][^cooper].

**The lawyer's search.** The publishers' trade body, the Music Publishers Protective Association, sent a New York lawyer, Samuel Moanfeldt, to find out where the song came from [^mechem][^kansapedia][^kpr]. His own report says he began on or about 15 February 1935; a later scholar places his journey in the spring of 1934 [^moanfeldt][^cooper]. In Smith County, Clarence Harlan, then 86, sang the song for him from memory, as he had sung it with the Harlan orchestra some sixty years before [^mechem][^kansapedia]. Moanfeldt traced it to Higley and Kelley in Kansas, and the Goodwins' suit never came to trial [^mechem][^kpr][^ljworld].

**Myth and record.** Often told: the song was born in Colorado's mining camps as Colorado Home, or in Arizona. What the record shows: Colorado Home was sung by 1885 and the Goodwins' song dates from 1905, but the Kirwin Chief printed Higley's poem in 1874 [^mechem][^kansapedia].

Kansas made Home on the Range its state song in 1947 [^kansapedia][^kpr][^mechem]. Higley had died in Shawnee, Oklahoma, in 1911, and Kelley at Waterloo, Iowa, in 1905 [^mechem][^ohs-marker][^iagenweb]. Sources differ in small ways: Higley's grave marker gives his birth year as 1822, and one Historical Society page gives his death as 1909 [^ohs-marker][^kansapedia-higley].

## What the words mean

The song is a settler's wish for a home in open country, among wild animals and birds, where nobody complains.

1. Give me a home where the buffalo, the American bison [^webster-buffalo][^nps-bison], roam free, where deer and antelope play, where you hardly ever hear a discouraging word, and the sky is clear all day. The "antelope" of the plains is the pronghorn, which is not a true antelope at all [^nps-thro][^nps-yell].
2. The air is pure and the "zephyrs", soft breezes (the word first meant the west wind) [^webster-zephyr], are free and mild. The singer would not trade this home for all the bright cities.
3. Native Americans, called by the old term "red man", have been pushed out of this part of the West and are unlikely to return to the banks of Red River, where their campfires seldom burn now. The verse tells the taking of the land from the settlers' side. Mechem thought it "a stray", picked up on the cattle trail, not part of Higley's poem [^mechem].
4. On bright nights the singer has stood under the stars and wondered whether their glory is greater than ours.
5. The singer loves the wild flowers, the cry of the curlew, the white rocks and the herds of antelope grazing on green hilltops. Mechem thought the bird most likely the long-billed curlew, a large shorebird of the dry western grasslands with a long down-curved bill [^mechem][^audubon].
6. Give me a land where bright sand sparkles in a slow stream and a white swan glides like a girl in a dream.
7. The last verse turns the opening into a vow: I would not trade my home on the range.

### The full words

These are the seven verses and the refrain as printed in Lomax's Cowboy Songs and Other Frontier Ballads (1910), pages 39 to 40, which prints the refrain after the first and the last verse. Higley died in 1911 and the book was printed in 1910, so all of it is in the public domain.

**Verse 1**
Oh, give me a home where the buffalo roam,
Where the deer and the antelope play,
Where seldom is heard a discouraging word
And the skies are not cloudy all day.

**Refrain**
Home, home on the range,
Where the deer and the antelope play;
Where seldom is heard a discouraging word
And the skies are not cloudy all day.

**Verse 2**
Where the air is so pure, the zephyrs so free,
The breezes so balmy and light,
That I would not exchange my home on the range
For all of the cities so bright.

**Verse 3**
The red man was pressed from this part of the West,
He's likely no more to return
To the banks of Red River where seldom if ever
Their flickering camp-fires burn.

**Verse 4**
How often at night when the heavens are bright
With the light from the glittering stars,
Have I stood here amazed and asked as I gazed
If their glory exceeds that of ours.

**Verse 5**
Oh, I love these wild flowers in this dear land of ours,
The curlew I love to hear scream,
And I love the white rocks and the antelope flocks
That graze on the mountain-tops green.

**Verse 6**
Oh, give me a land where the bright diamond sand
Flows leisurely down the stream;
Where the graceful white swan goes gliding along
Like a maid in a heavenly dream.

**Verse 7**
Then I would not exchange my home on the range,
Where the deer and the antelope play;
Where seldom is heard a discouraging word
And the skies are not cloudy all day.

**Refrain**
Home, home on the range,
Where the deer and the antelope play;
Where seldom is heard a discouraging word
And the skies are not cloudy all day.

## How it is sung

Home on the Range has been many things: a dance tune in Smith County in the 1870s, a cowboy song on the trail, a radio favourite in the 1930s, and since 1947 the state song of Kansas [^kansapedia][^mechem][^kpr].

Our arrangement is read from Lomax's 1910 printing, which sets it in E-flat in 6/8; we sing it in G in 3/4, as a gentle waltz [^lomax1910]. We sing the first verse and the refrain, for 2 to 4 singers: soprano and alto, then the bass, then the tenor. The soprano carries the tune, the alto sings the words a third or a sixth under it, the tenor fills the middle of the chord, and the bass holds long notes on "oo", singing words only for "Home, home on the range".

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/home-on-the-range/soprano): you carry the tune, from low D to the D an octave above. "Oh, give me a home" leaps up to G and climbs to B on "home"; then "buffalo" and "discouraging" each leap a sixth, from E up to C. Your top D comes on "deer" in the verse's "Where the deer" and again on the refrain's first "Home," held for a full bar. Give the long notes their time: "play," holds for more than a bar and "day." for almost two.
- [Alto](/songs/home-on-the-range/alto): you sing the words a third or a sixth under the tune, starting on the B below middle C and living mostly on B, D and E. You rise to your top B on "deer" under the tune's D. In "And the skies are not cloudy all day" you walk down from G to low B; then, after a breath, you leap an octave to B for the refrain's first "Home,".
- [Tenor](/songs/home-on-the-range/tenor): you stay in the middle of the chord, almost half the time on G below middle C; the whole of "Oh, give me a home" is on G. You rise to your top D on the verse's "the deer" and on "the skies are", and on "on" in "Home, home on the range". Before each final "day." you dip to F-sharp on "all" and settle back on G.
- [Bass](/songs/home-on-the-range/bass): you come in under "give" and hold long notes on "oo", mostly G, C, D and B, most of them a bar or more. Your highest note, E, comes under "lope", just before the held D under "play,". In the refrain you sing "Home, home on the range": low G, up to D, down through B and C, and back to a held low G, where the song ends too.

## Questions

### Who wrote the music for Home on the Range?

The tune is credited to Daniel E. Kelley, a musician in Gaylord, Kansas, who set Brewster Higley's poem in the 1870s [^kansapedia][^kpr]. The evidence is the memory of people who played it with him, above all Clarence Harlan, who sang it for the investigator Samuel Moanfeldt [^mechem][^kansapedia]. Kelley probably never wrote it down, and every later version of the tune goes back to Lomax's printing of 1910 [^mechem][^kansapedia][^lomax1910].

### Why is Home on the Range the Kansas state song?

Because it was written in Kansas, by Brewster Higley in Smith County. Several earlier state song contests had picked no winner; in the 1940s its supporters argued for it on those Kansas roots, and the legislature adopted it in 1947 [^kansapedia][^kpr][^mechem]. Critics said it was out of date, and wrong because antelope no longer roamed the plains [^kansapedia]; the plains "antelope" is in fact the pronghorn, not a true antelope [^nps-thro][^nps-yell].

### What does "home on the range" mean?

It means a home in open grazing country. In the American West a range is open country where cattle or sheep wander and graze [^webster-range]. The song praises that country: free-roaming buffalo, clear skies, pure air and wild flowers. "Home on the range" is the phrase from Lomax's 1910 printing; Higley called his poem My Western Home [^lomax1910][^kansapedia].

### Is Home on the Range public domain?

Yes, as printed in its old sources. Higley died in 1911 and Kelley in 1905, and in the United States anything published before 1931 is now in the public domain, as Lomax's 1910 printing was [^mechem][^iagenweb][^cornell][^lomax1910]. Later arrangements, such as the sheet music of 1925 and 1930, are separate works [^mechem].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings it throughout, as Lomax printed it [^lomax1910]. The alto sings the same words a third or a sixth below, so two singers already have the song in harmony.
