Once in Royal David's City is a Christmas hymn by the Irish poet Cecil Frances Alexander, written for children to explain one line of the Apostles' Creed: that Christ was "born of the Virgin Mary" [^hlc1850][^julian]. It appeared in her Hymns for Little Children, published in London in 1848 [^julian][^dnb-alexander][^ha]. Its tune, IRBY, is by the English organist Henry John Gauntlett, who set it as No. 1 of a series of Christmas Carols from the same London publisher, Joseph Masters [^gauntlett1849][^ha]. Since a revision of the order of service in 1919, it has opened A Festival of Nine Lessons and Carols, the Christmas Eve service in the chapel of King's College, Cambridge, with its first verse sung by a single treble [^kings][^summerly2019][^methodist].

## Where it comes from

**The poet.** Cecil Frances Humphreys was born in Ireland in 1818, a daughter of Major John Humphreys [^dnb-alexander][^rpo]. The reference books disagree on the details: the Dictionary of National Biography places her birth in County Wicklow, Representative Poetry Online in Dublin, and Julian's Dictionary of Hymnology gives the year as 1823 [^dnb-alexander][^rpo][^julian-person]. The hymns came out without her name: the title page calls her only the author of "Verses for Holy Seasons" and two other books [^hlc25][^dnb-alexander].

**A book for her godsons, 1848.** She dedicated Hymns for Little Children "To my little godsons", hoping that verse "which children love" might help to impress on their minds "what they are, what I have promised for them, and what they must seek to be" [^hlc1850][^hlc25]. That is the language of the Church of England's catechism, in which a child says that the godparents "did promise and vow three things" in the child's name: to renounce evil, to believe the articles of the faith, and to keep God's commandments [^bcp]. The book follows that plan. After hymns for morning, evening, the Trinity and baptism come hymns on each promise, and under the second, "To believe all the Articles of the Christian Faith", there is a hymn for each clause of the Creed, thirteen in all [^hlc1850][^ha]. This one is the fourth of them, No. 11 in the book, headed with the Creed's words "Who was conceived by the Holy Ghost, born of the Virgin Mary" [^hlc1850][^hlc25]. Julian's dictionary gives its place as page 30 of the 1848 book [^julian]. The next hymn, for "Suffered under Pontius Pilate", is "There is a green hill far away" [^hlc1850][^dnb-alexander].

**Keble's notice.** The book opens with a short notice signed "J. K." and dated "Hursley, March 28, 1848" [^hlc25]. The Dictionary of National Biography names its writer as John Keble [^dnb-alexander]. He explains that the writer of the hymns "wished for some kind of clerical imprimatur", a clergyman's approval, and adds that the profits "will be applied to the support of a school for deaf and dumb children", kept up by gifts, "in a small town in the north of Ireland" [^hlc25][^hlc1887]. The book sold for decades: a London copy of about 1870 calls itself the twenty-fifth edition, and a London copy of 1887 the sixty-sixth [^hlc25][^hlc1887].

**Mrs Alexander.** In 1850 she married the Reverend William Alexander, who later became Bishop of Derry and Raphoe [^dnb-alexander][^julian-person][^rpo]. The change shows in the book. The Philadelphia printing of 1850 signs the dedication "C. F. H.", for Humphreys; later London printings sign it "C. F. A." [^hlc1850][^hlc25]. She died at Londonderry on 12 October 1895 [^dnb-alexander][^julian-person].

**The tune.** Henry John Gauntlett was born at Wellington, Shropshire, on 9 July 1805, the son of a clergyman who became vicar of Olney [^dnb-gauntlett][^hymnary-irby]. He trained as a solicitor, played the organ in London churches and wrote a great many hymn tunes [^dnb-gauntlett]. His tune for Alexander's hymn came out in a series headed "Christmas Carols", published in London by Joseph Masters, with this hymn as No. 1 [^gauntlett1849][^imslp]. The pages we have seen carry no date, and the accounts differ: the Hymnology Archive and IMSLP give 1849, Gauntlett's entry in the Dictionary of National Biography lists his "Christmas Carols" under 1848, and Hymnary.org says the tune first came out in an 1850 pamphlet [^ha][^imslp][^dnb-gauntlett][^hymnary-text]. The sheet is headed "Christmas Carols" and "No. 1" in black letter, and marked "Allegro Moderato", in 4/4 time and G major: one vocal line with the first three verses printed under it, and a piano part on two staves below. The last three verses follow as text on the next page [^gauntlett1849]. Its words differ in places from the book: "Jesus Christ her only child" where the book has "her little Child", "Fears and smiles" for "Tears and smiles", and "wond'rous" [^gauntlett1849][^hlc25].

**Hymns Ancient and Modern, 1868.** The Appendix to Hymns Ancient and Modern, whose preface is dated September 1868, printed the hymn as No. 361 among the hymns "For the Young", headed "The child Jesus", with all six verses and an Amen [^ham1868][^ha]. The preface thanks Gauntlett for a new tune and adds "a second, No. 361, harmonized for them, being inserted by leave of Mr. Masters" [^ham1868]. So the four-part harmony is Gauntlett's own, made for that book, and its index gives the tune the name it still has, Irby [^ham1868][^ha]. The book does not say where the name comes from. Its title page names William Henry Monk as its musical editor, and the Dictionary of National Biography calls Sir Henry Williams Baker, who died in 1877, the "promoter and editor" of Hymns Ancient and Modern [^ham1868][^dnb-baker].

**King's College, Cambridge.** The first Festival of Nine Lessons and Carols was held in King's College Chapel on Christmas Eve 1918. The college's dean, Eric Milner-White, adapted a service that Edward White Benson had drawn up for Truro in 1880 [^kings][^summerly2019]. The order was revised in 1919, and from then on the service has begun with Once in Royal David's City [^kings][^summerly2019]. The choir sings it in procession [^summerly2020][^hymnary-text]. (Hymnary.org says the hymn has been the processional since 1918, but the college dates it from the 1919 revision [^hymnary-text][^kings].) Its harmony there is by Arthur Henry Mann, the college's organist from 1876 to 1929, and takes elements from Gauntlett's [^kings][^summerly2020][^ha]. The service was first broadcast in 1928 [^kings][^summerly2019].

**Myth and record.** Often told: the solo treble who sings the first verse alone has been part of the service since 1919, when the hymn began to open it [^churchnear]. What the record shows: the 1919 revision made the hymn the opening, as the college says [^kings]. But Gresham College lectures on the history of the King's service say that at first all the choristers sang the first verse, and that the solo was brought in by the choir's director, Boris Ord, in 1935 [^summerly2019][^summerly2020].

## What the words mean

Alexander's six verses fall into pairs: the birth, the child as an example, and the hope of heaven [^hymnary-text].

**Verse 1.** "Royal David's city" is Bethlehem. Luke's Gospel says Joseph went "unto the city of David, which is called Bethlehem", because he was of David's family, and that Mary "laid him in a manger; because there was no room for them in the inn" [^kjv]. The Gospel names the manger, a feeding trough, but not a shed or a stable; that is the picture the hymn adds, and some writers have questioned it [^kjv][^ha].

**Verse 2.** The child is "God and Lord of all", and yet he was born among animals. A "stall" is the place in a stable where a horse or an ox is kept and fed [^webster1913]. "Mean" here has its old sense of humble or low in rank, not unkind [^webster1913].

**Verse 3.** Luke says that the boy Jesus went home to Nazareth with his parents "and was subject unto them" [^kjv]. Alexander turns that into a lesson for children: "Christian children all must be Mild, obedient, good as He". This is the verse most often left out of modern hymnals [^hymnary-text][^methodist].

**Verse 4.** A "pattern" is an example to copy. Jesus grew "day by day like us", as Luke says he "increased in wisdom and stature", and he knows tears and smiles [^kjv]. "He feeleth for our sadness" echoes the Letter to the Hebrews, which says Christ can be "touched with the feeling of our infirmities" [^kjv].

**Verse 5.** The child of Bethlehem is now Lord in heaven, and he leads his children "to the place where He is gone", as in John's Gospel: "I go to prepare a place for you" [^kjv].

**Verse 6.** The singers will see him not in the stable but "Set at God's right Hand on high", close to the Creed's words that he "sitteth at the right hand of God", which the book also teaches [^hlc1850]. The children "like stars" recall Daniel's promise that the righteous shall shine "as the stars for ever and ever", and "all in white" recalls the crowd "clothed with white robes" before the throne in Revelation [^kjv].

### The full words

The words below are Alexander's six verses as printed in the twenty-fifth edition of Hymns for Little Children (London: Joseph Masters), with its spelling and capitals. Hymns Ancient and Modern (1868) prints the same words [^hlc25][^ham1868]. The Philadelphia printing of 1850 has "lovely maiden" in verse 3 [^hlc1850].

**Verse 1**
Once in royal David's city
Stood a lowly cattle shed,
Where a mother laid her Baby,
In a manger for His bed.
Mary was that mother mild,
Jesus Christ her little Child.

**Verse 2**
He came down to earth from Heaven,
Who is God and Lord of all,
And His shelter was a stable,
And His cradle was a stall;
With the poor, and mean, and lowly,
Lived on earth our Saviour Holy.

**Verse 3**
And through all His wondrous childhood,
He would honour and obey,
Love and watch the lowly maiden
In whose gentle arms He lay.
Christian children all must be
Mild, obedient, good as He.

**Verse 4**
For He is our childhood's Pattern,
Day by day like us He grew,
He was little, weak, and helpless,
Tears and smiles like us He knew,
And He feeleth for our sadness,
And He shareth in our gladness.

**Verse 5**
And our eyes at last shall see Him,
Through His own redeeming love,
For that Child so dear and gentle
Is our Lord in Heaven above;
And He leads His children on
To the place where He is gone.

**Verse 6**
Not in that poor lowly stable,
With the oxen standing by,
We shall see Him; but in Heaven,
Set at God's right Hand on high;
When like stars His children crowned,
All in white shall wait around.

## How it is sung

It is a Christmas hymn for Christmas Eve and Christmas Day, and the Psalter Hymnal Handbook commends it as a processional [^hymnary-text][^summerly2020]. Hymns Ancient and Modern placed it among its hymns for the young in 1868 [^ham1868][^ha]. Its tune is IRBY, and its meter is 8.7.8.7.7.7: four lines of eight and seven syllables, then two of seven [^hymnary-irby][^hlc25].

Many books now print fewer than six verses, and the text is edited from book to book [^hymnary-text][^methodist]. The Psalter Hymnal leaves out the third, and the British Methodist Singing the Faith (No. 214) prints four [^hymnary-text][^methodist]. Some books also change words, such as "maiden" to "mother" [^ha].

Our arrangement is read from Gauntlett's own four parts for No. 361 of Hymns Ancient and Modern, in G major, each line starting on two pickup beats [^ham1868]. That book prints the words apart from the music, so the fit of words to notes is ours: one syllable to each note, or to each pair of quick notes joined by a beam [^ham1868]. His tune and key are kept, with most of his chords; the lower parts are revoiced for beginners, one note to each syllable. We sing verses 1, 2 and 6, the birth, the stable and the closing sight of heaven, in the 1868 book's words [^ham1868]. It is for two to four singers: two take soprano, on the tune, and alto, a third adds the bass, and the tenor makes four.

## Versions and other words

- **Hymns for Little Children with music.** Gauntlett also set the whole book for unison voices with piano or organ. The Dictionary of National Biography dates his Hymns for Little Children to 1853; the Hymnology Archive describes an edition of 1858 [^dnb-gauntlett][^ha].
- **Mann's harmony for King's.** Arthur Henry Mann's harmony was long kept for King's alone. A Gresham College lecture says it was not published until the last year of Boris Ord's life; the Hymnology Archive gives a Novello edition of 1957 and Carols for Choirs of 1961 [^summerly2020][^ha].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/once-in-royal-davids-city/soprano): Gauntlett's tune, from low D to top E, most often on G, then B. Its low D comes only on the first note of the first and third lines ("Once", "Where"). Many syllables take two quick notes, as "Da-vid's" and "cat-tle" do, so keep them smooth. The top E opens the last two lines, "Ma-ry" and "Je-sus" in verse 1 ("With the" and "Lived on" in verse 2, "When like" and "All in" in verse 6).
- [Alto](/songs/once-in-royal-davids-city/alto): under the tune, one note to each syllable, most often on its low B, then D. The first four lines all start on that B. Its top G comes on the quick note after the long one in lines 2 and 4 ("ly" in "lowly", "ger" in "manger"), at the end of line 5 ("mild") and on "Je-sus" and "her" in the last line; verses 2 and 6 have it in the same places.
- [Tenor](/songs/once-in-royal-davids-city/tenor): the middle of the chord, most often on B, with G close behind. Its one note outside the key is the C-sharp on "laid her" ("was a", "but in"): tune it carefully. Its top D is a single quick note in the second, fourth and last lines ("ly" in "lowly", "ger" in "manger", "her" in "her little Child"), and its low D comes in the fifth line, on "ther mild" ("and low-ly", "dren crowned").
- [Bass](/songs/once-in-royal-davids-city/bass): the bass line, most often on D, then G, A and B. It starts on low G and ends every verse there, and lines 2, 4 and 5 end on it too. Its top G is a single short note at the end of the first and third lines ("ty" in "city", "by" in "Baby").

## Questions

### Who wrote the music for Once in Royal David's City?

The tune, IRBY, is by Henry John Gauntlett (1805 to 1876), an English organist and composer. It came out as No. 1 of his Christmas Carols, published in London by Joseph Masters and usually dated 1849 [^ha][^imslp]. Gauntlett made a four-part harmony of it for Hymns Ancient and Modern in 1868 [^ham1868][^ha]. At King's College, Cambridge, the choir sings a harmony by Arthur Henry Mann, the college organist from 1876 to 1929 [^kings][^summerly2020].

### What does Once in Royal David's City mean?

It tells the birth of Jesus for children, to explain the Creed's words "born of the Virgin Mary" [^hlc1850][^bcp]. "Royal David's city" is Bethlehem, which Luke's Gospel calls "the city of David" [^kjv]. The first two verses tell of the manger and the poor surroundings, the middle two hold up the child Jesus as an example for children, and the last two look to heaven, where his followers will see him [^hlc25][^hymnary-text].

### How many verses does Once in Royal David's City have?

Six, each of six lines, as Cecil Frances Alexander wrote it and as Hymns Ancient and Modern printed it in 1868 [^hlc25][^hymnary-text][^ham1868]. Many hymnals now print fewer, and our arrangement sings verses 1, 2 and 6 [^ham1868]. The third verse, which tells children to be "mild, obedient, good as He", is the one most often left out: the Psalter Hymnal omits it, and the Methodist Singing the Faith prints four of the six [^hymnary-text][^methodist].
