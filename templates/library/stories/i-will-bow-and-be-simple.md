I Will Bow and Be Simple is a song of the Shakers, the celibate religious community that worshipped by singing and dancing [^shakermuseum][^andrews]. In two short strains a singer promises to bow: to be simple, free and humble, to bow like the willow tree, to wear the "easy yoke" and to fall upon the rock [^hazzard][^andrews]. It has no known author. The copy we sing from, in Mary Hazzard's handwritten songbook, carries no title and no date, only the words "North Family" at its foot [^hazzard]. The Shaker scholar Edward Deming Andrews printed its words in 1937, and twice more in 1940, in The Gift to Be Simple, once among the songs of humility and once with its tune, calling it a "bowing song" [^andrews1937][^andrews].

## Where it comes from

**The community.** The Shakers, the United Society of Believers, lived in villages that shared work and property [^shakermuseum][^andrews]. Each village was divided into "families", households that lived and worked together under their own elders [^andrews][^shakerheritage], often named for where they stood from the Church Family at the centre: the North Family, the South Family and so on [^andrews][^nps]. From 1837 a long revival, which they called Mother Ann's Work, filled their meetings with "gift songs", songs a worshipper believed were given by the spirit [^andrews][^wintcoll]. Andrews wrote that through the 1840s the songs kept returning to gospel love, union, freedom from bondage and, above all, meekness and humility [^andrews].

**The songbook.** Mary Hazzard, a sister at New Lebanon, New York, the Shakers' leading village, began her book on 7 February 1847 and titled it "A Collection of Extra Songs of Various Kinds Written and Pricked For the Purpose of Retaining them" [^hazzardbook][^wintcat][^andrews][^shakermuseum]. "Pricked" means the tunes are written down as well as the words [^webster1828][^hazzard]. The Winterthur Library in Delaware holds it as ASC 893, catalogued with dates of 1847 to 1856 [^wintcat]. Our story of [Simple Gifts](/songs/simple-gifts) tells more about the book, which holds that song too [^hazzardbook].

![[1]]

**The page.** On the book's page 108 the song has no heading: it starts straight in with "I will bow & be simple" [^hazzard]. The words run across the page in a neat slanting hand, and under each line is the tune in Shaker letteral notation, small letters such as c, e and g for the pitches, with strokes and joining lines for the rhythm [^hazzard][^wintcoll]. Both strains are marked to repeat. At its end, after the double bar, is written "North Family", with no village, no giver and no date. A wordless tune marked "Watervliete" fills the rest of the page [^hazzard].

**Where it sits in the book.** Page 105 ends "Canterbury 1851", and page 116 ends "Watervliet. 1852" [^hazzardbook]. Our page itself is undated, between songs dated 1851 and 1852, and since the book is not kept strictly in date order, that is as near as it can be placed [^hazzard][^hazzardbook].

**Which North Family?** Several Shaker villages had a North Family, Harvard's as well as New Lebanon's, and the page does not say which this was [^harvardhist][^andrews][^hazzard]. Two wordless tunes a few pages on, on pages 110 and 111, are signed "North Family" too, next to songs marked "Shirley to P. S." and "Harvard", two Shaker villages in Massachusetts; they name no village either [^hazzardbook][^andrews]. Andrews took it for New Lebanon's. On the page where he prints the words among the humility songs he wrote "North family. New Lebanon?", with a question mark, and with the tune he wrote "from the North family, New Lebanon, recorded by Mary Hazzard in 1847" [^andrews]. The Shaker Museum's record of a portrait of Hazzard says she herself lived both at the North Family and at the Church Family at Mount Lebanon, as New Lebanon was called from 1861 [^smportrait][^smpostmark][^andrews].

**Myth and record.** Often told: the song was written in 1847 by the North Family at New Lebanon [^riseup]. What the record shows: the only early copy we know of names no village and no year [^hazzard]. The 1847 and New Lebanon come from Andrews's book of 1940 [^andrews]. His first printing, in 1937, gave no place or date at all [^andrews1937]. 1847 is the year the book was begun, and Andrews captioned his photograph of another of its pages "Song-book of Mary Hazzard. New Lebanon, 1847" [^hazzardbook][^wintcat][^andrews].

### Timeline

- 1837: Mother Ann's Work begins, the revival that filled Shaker songbooks with gift songs [^andrews][^wintcoll]
- 1847: Mary Hazzard begins her book of extra songs, on 7 February [^hazzardbook][^wintcat]

## What the words mean

The song is all one promise, "I will bow", made five times over. Bowing was something Shakers did as well as sang about. Andrews, from a Hancock record begun in 1842, describes worshippers at an outdoor feast bowing low, turning and reeling, and he notes that in many songs the singers acted out their words, with shaking, turning, bowing and reeling [^andrews]. The Canterbury Shakers' own hymnal of 1852 sings "To bow and bend be not ashamed, For angels bow low down" in a hymn about the dance [^sacredrep]. A song from Enfield, New Hampshire, dated April 1848, asks "Can't you bend your necks a little", for it is not the haughty but "those who're truly simple" who receive "Mothers love" [^andrews].

![[2]]

**"I will bow and be simple, I will bow and be free."** Simplicity was one of the twelve Christian virtues the Shakers taught, beside meekness and humility [^summaryview][^andrews]. Their Summary View of the Millennial Church, in its edition of 1848, says that "True gospel simplicity implies a godly sincerity, and a real singleness of heart" [^summaryview]. Andrews called simplicity one of the most pervasive Shaker virtues, and wrote that in its more mature forms it appeared as Christian humility, as singleness of purpose and as purity [^andrews]. "Free" is a word the Shaker songs of these years return to again and again: Andrews counts "freedom from bondage" among their constant themes [^andrews].

![[3]]

**"I will bow and be humble, yea bow like the willow tree."** "Yea" means yes, or indeed. In Shaker speech, Andrews notes, it was said "ye", with the e of "greet" [^andrews]. A willow bends in the wind without breaking, so to bow like the willow tree is to give way without being broken by pride.

**"I will bow, this is the token."** A token is a sign: bowing is the outward sign of a humble heart.

**"I will wear the easy yoke."** This is Jesus's saying in Matthew 11: "Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart", and "my yoke is easy, and my burden is light" [^kjv]. A yoke is the wooden frame that harnesses an ox to its work. Wearing it means taking up Christ's way, which he calls easy.

**"I will bow and be broken, yea I'll fall upon the rock."** This echoes another saying of Jesus, about the stone the builders rejected: "whosoever shall fall on this stone shall be broken" (Matthew 21:44, and Luke 20:18) [^kjv]. The song seems to turn the warning into a choice: the singer falls on the rock willingly, to have pride broken.

### The full words

These are the words on page 108 of Mary Hazzard's book, with her spelling and her ampersands, set out by the music's phrases rather than her line breaks [^hazzard]. Each strain is sung twice. The page has no other verses.

I will bow & be simple I will bow and be free,
I will bow and be humble yea bow like the willow tree

I will bow this is the token
I will wear the easy yoke
I will bow & be broken yea I'll fall upon the rock.

## How it is sung

Andrews called it a "bowing song" and placed it among the songs of humility, or "low" songs, as he groups them [^andrews]. Such songs were sung in worship [^andrews][^sacredrep], and Andrews wrote in 1937 that the singers bowed low for songs of simplicity like this one [^andrews1937].

Our arrangement is read from Hazzard's page, in C, with every pitch as written [^hazzard]. Shaker letters named the steps of the scale, not fixed pitches: c was the home note of any major tune, at whatever pitch it was sung [^andrews]. Her page gives no time figure, but every bar holds two quarter notes; we sing it in 4/4 with every note value doubled, and both strains twice, as her repeat signs mark them. Breaths are written in for beginners, and her "&" is sung "and". In the second strain one note, on "be" in "bow & be broken", is an e written over a scraped-out g, and we sing the e.

## Versions and other words

Andrews printed the tune in 1940 as song No. 29, in C and in 2/4, and suggested it sounded well sung in E flat, at a quarter note of 91 [^andrews]. Shaker letteral notation shows how high a note is only by writing its letter higher or lower on the page, so the octave is not always plain, and his reading differs from ours in places [^andrews][^wintcoll][^hazzard]. He set "humble, yea bow like the willow tree" and much of the second strain an octave lower than we do, ending on middle C where we end on the C above, and he gives some different notes, as on "broken, yea I'll" [^andrews].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/i-will-bow-and-be-simple/soprano): you carry the tune, which opens on middle C and climbs C, E, G on "I will bow". The first strain leaps up a sixth to its top E on "hum-ble". In the second strain you sing that high E five times running, on "be broken, yea I'll", then fall away to "fall upon the rock", which ends on the C above middle C.
- [Alto](/songs/i-will-bow-and-be-simple/alto): you start on middle C with the tune, then sing the words almost always a third or a sixth below it. Under the soprano's five high Es you hold G five times. In the second strain your one low step is the D on "-ken" of "token".
- [Tenor](/songs/i-will-bow-and-be-simple/tenor): you sing the words mostly on G and C. The second strain takes you higher, to D on "to-ken" and on "bow" in "bow and be broken", and dips to B on "I will" and "yea I'll"; your lowest note is the F on "will" in "I will wear".
- [Bass](/songs/i-will-bow-and-be-simple/bass): you sing "oo", coming in after the two-note pickup and holding a low C for two whole bars. The first strain is long held notes; in the second you move more, dropping to your lowest note, an A, under "is the" in "this is the token", then going to and fro on E, D and C under "I will bow and be broken".

## Questions

### Who wrote I Will Bow and Be Simple?

No writer is known. The copy in Mary Hazzard's songbook names no maker, giver or date, only "North Family" [^hazzard]. Andrews, who printed it in 1937 and 1940, gave no author either; in 1940 he called it a bowing song from the North Family at New Lebanon, though on another page he put a question mark after the place [^andrews1937][^andrews].

### What does I Will Bow and Be Simple mean?

It is a promise of humility. The singer will bow, be simple and humble, and bow like the willow tree, the Shaker virtues of simplicity, meekness and humility [^summaryview][^andrews]. "The easy yoke" is Jesus's yoke in Matthew 11:29 to 30, and falling on the rock to be broken echoes Matthew 21:44 [^kjv]. Here the fall seems chosen, so that pride is broken.

### Is I Will Bow and Be Simple the same song as Simple Gifts?

No. They are two different Shaker songs with different tunes, and both are in Mary Hazzard's book, Simple Gifts on page 37 and this song on page 108 [^hazzard][^hazzardbook]. They share their words' ideas: Simple Gifts sings of the gift to be simple and free and of bowing and bending without shame, and this song promises to bow and be simple and free [^hazzardbook][^hazzard].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune all the way through. Two singers take the soprano and alto; three add the bass on "oo"; the tenor makes four. The alto and tenor sing the same words beneath the tune. Hazzard's page has the tune alone, so the harmony is ours [^hazzard].
