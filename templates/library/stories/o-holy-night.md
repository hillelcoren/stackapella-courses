O Holy Night is a Christmas solo that choirs have made their own. Its French words, Minuit, chrétiens ("Midnight, Christians"), are by Placide Cappeau, a wine merchant of Roquemaure in southern France. Adolphe Adam, a Paris composer of comic opera and ballet, set them in 1847 [^canterbury-fr][^musimem]. It was sung at Midnight Mass in Roquemaure that Christmas, and within twenty years a French church-music journal was complaining that it was heard in streets, salons and café-concerts [^musimem][^lefort][^ivry]. The English most people sing is by John Sullivan Dwight, a Boston music critic and former Unitarian minister, and dates from 1855 [^canterbury-en][^canterbury-dwight][^hymnary-stw].

## Where it comes from

**The words.** Cappeau was born in Roquemaure in 1808, the son of a cooper [^canterbury-cappeau][^musimem][^nemausensis]. As a boy he lost a hand in an accident, so he could not follow his father's trade; he studied instead, read law in Paris, and went home to sell wine [^canterbury-cappeau][^nemausensis]. He wrote poems in French and Provençal, and had a name as a socialist and freethinker [^canterbury-fr][^musimem][^ivry]. By most accounts the parish priest of Roquemaure asked him for a Christmas poem, to help raise money for the church's stained-glass windows by one account, or to mark the end of work on it by another [^canterbury-fr][^musimem][^lefort]. He wrote three verses [^canterbury-fr][^musimem][^ivry].

The date is told two ways. Cappeau said he wrote them in a stagecoach on the way to Paris, and a history by a later parish priest of Roquemaure, followed by Musica et Memoria, dates that journey 3 December 1847, between Mâcon and Dijon [^canterbury-fr][^musimem][^nemausensis]. Antoine Chansroux, in the Academy of Nîmes's Mémoires for 1895 (which we read only in a modern transcription), tells it differently: at a gathering on 3 December 1843 the hostess asked him for verses, he wrote them the next day in his carriage driving home, and they were sung that Christmas [^nemausensis].

**The tune.** The link to Adam was Emily Laurey, a former opera singer who had sung in his work, and whose husband Pierre was building a suspension bridge over the Rhône at Roquemaure. By one account she sent Adam the poem; by another Cappeau carried it to him in Paris [^musimem][^nemausensis]. Adam set it within days [^musimem][^nemausensis]. By the 1847 account, Emily Laurey sang it for the first time at Midnight Mass in Roquemaure on 24 December 1847 [^musimem][^lefort][^nemausensis]. Copies were dedicated to Emily Laurey, and an 1895 note describes covers headed, in French, Cantique de Noël, words by M. Cappeau of Roquemaure, set to music and composed expressly for Madame Emily Laurey by her friend Adolphe Adam [^nemausensis][^ddb]. Adam, born in Paris in 1803 and dead there in 1856, is remembered most for his ballet Giselle (1841) [^musimem][^delage]. He called the carol "la Marseillaise religieuse", the Marseillaise of religion [^canterbury-fr][^musimem][^ivry].

**How it travelled.** By about 1852, the date cataloguers give their earliest copies, Paris publishers had issued a "second" and a "sixth" edition, and Schott was selling it in Mainz, Brussels and London with French and German words [^momh][^imslp][^ddb]. In 1857 a young Quebec musician, Ernest Gagnon, heard it at Midnight Mass in Paris; the next year he brought it home [^bronze][^ivry].

**The English.** Dwight was born in Boston in 1813 [^cooke][^canterbury-en]. Ordained a Unitarian minister at Northampton, Massachusetts, in 1840, he left in 1841 for the Brook Farm community, and in 1852 founded Dwight's Journal of Music, which he edited for almost thirty years [^ripley][^cooke][^canterbury-dwight]. His English dates from 1855, but the sources we read do not say where it first appeared [^canterbury-en][^hymnary-stw]. He died in Boston on 5 September 1893 [^cooke][^canterbury-dwight].

**Myth and record.** Often told: church leaders in France banned the carol once they learned what kind of man had written the words. What the record shows: Catholic critics and church-music journals disliked its martial music and its words [^musimem][^ivry]. In 1864 the Revue de musique sacrée suggested dropping it as a song of streets and cafés [^musimem][^ivry]. One Catholic reference book says many dioceses dropped it, but none of the sources we read names a ban by France's bishops as a whole or by Rome [^ivry]. Catholics in France and Quebec kept singing it at Midnight Mass [^coyne][^bronze].

Often told: abolitionists in America took up Dwight's third verse, "Chains shall he break, for the slave is our brother". What the record shows: the line comes from Cappeau, whose third verse says the Redeemer sees a brother where there was only a slave, and that love joins those whom iron chained [^musimem][^nemausensis][^dann]. Dwight did hate slavery: in November 1861 his Journal said the Union was fighting "a treacherous and arrogant pro-Slavery rebellion" [^dwj1861][^cooke]. But none of the sources we read shows abolitionists singing the carol or quoting the verse; a 2023 article says it suited their cause and was popular in the North in the Civil War, but cites nothing from the time to show it [^coyne].

## What the words mean

Cappeau wrote three verses, each ending with a call to the people; his French survives in slightly different versions, and we follow Havard de la Montagne's [^musimem][^nemausensis]. Dwight's three verses are a free version: they keep each verse's shape and some images, and say much in their own way [^musimem][^hutchins][^dann].

**Verse 1, the one we sing.** It is the night of Jesus' birth. The world lay long in sin and error until he came; then "the soul felt its worth", knowing its value. A shiver of hope runs through the worn-out world as a new morning breaks over there, "yonder" [^hutchins].

**The refrain.** "Fall on your knees! O hear the angel voices!": kneel, and listen to the angels of the Christmas story. "Night divine": a holy night, the night when Christ was born [^hutchins]. Cappeau's first verse is plainer theology: at midnight, the solemn hour, the God-man came down to wash away original sin and calm his Father's anger; the whole world trembles with hope; people, on your knees, "Peuple, à genoux", wait for your deliverance [^musimem][^nemausensis]. Dwight's "thrill of hope" and "fall on your knees" echo those lines [^musimem][^hutchins].

**Verses 2 and 3 (not sung here).** Cappeau's second verse has the light of faith guide everyone to the cradle, as the kings of the East were once guided, and tells the proud to bow before a King born in a manger; Dwight keeps the wise men and the manger and adds a friend in our trials [^musimem][^hutchins]. Cappeau's third says the Redeemer has broken every bond and ends: people, stand up and sing your deliverance. Dwight's makes it a teaching of love, an end to chains and oppression, and praise [^musimem][^dann].

## How it is sung

Adam's printed copies are for one voice with piano, organ or harmonium [^ddb][^imslp]. In French Canada, singing it at Midnight Mass was an honour for a fine singer or the parish soloist [^bronze][^ivry]. English hymnals call the tune CANTIQUE DE NOEL [^hymnary-stw]. Choirs sing it in arrangements such as Dudley Buck's for solo, choir and organ (New York, copyright 1881) [^buck][^imslp], or the 1916 Boston carol book's, with a soprano or tenor soloist in the verse and a choir in the refrain [^hutchins].

Our arrangement is read from that 1916 book, written a minor third lower, in C, for 2 to 4 singers: the first verse and the refrain. We sing it slow and lilting in 6/8, the lilt of the book's triplet accompaniment [^hutchins]. In the verse, the alto and tenor sing our own quiet chords on the book's organ harmony, and the bass takes the organ's lowest notes. In the refrain they sing the book's choir parts, with a few notes changed so the lines move by step.

## Versions and other words

- **Two verses or three:** Carols Old and Carols New (1916) prints only Dwight's first two verses, leaving out "Truly he taught us", the verse with the chains [^hutchins]. Hollis Dann's Christmas Carols and Hymns for School and Choir (New York, 1910) prints all three [^dann].

## Did you know?

- Adam never mentioned the carol in his memoirs, published after his death [^musimem][^ivry][^adam1857].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/o-holy-night/soprano): the tune, slow and lilting, from a low C on "night" and "birth" up to its top E in "O night divine!". The refrain climbs, a long C on "Fall", D on "hear", E on "night".
- [Alto](/songs/o-holy-night/alto): quiet chords under the verse, mostly on E; on "soul felt its" it slips to E-flat, then back to E on "worth". In the refrain it sings the choir's part, up to G on "knees!", on the "O" of "O hear", and on "night" and "divine!" in "O night divine!".
- [Tenor](/songs/o-holy-night/tenor): the middle of the chords, mostly on C and G. From the first "night!" to "shining" it stays on C while the tune moves, then drops to a low E on "night of the" and "birth". Its top note is D on the last "night".
- [Bass](/songs/o-holy-night/bass): the organ's lowest notes in the verse and the choir's bass in the refrain, mostly on C and G, down to a low G under "night of the dear Saviour's", "thrill of hope" and "yonder breaks a". Under the first "O night divine!" it steps down A, G, F, E, one note to a syllable.

## Questions

### What does "long lay the world in sin and error pining" mean?

It means the world lay for a long time in sin and error, wasting away and longing for help: to "pine" is to languish, or to waste away with longing [^webster1828]. The next line ends the waiting: "till he appeared", when Christ was born [^hutchins]. The line is Dwight's; Cappeau's French speaks instead of original sin washed away and a world trembling with hope [^musimem].

### Was O Holy Night written by an atheist?

Not quite. Placide Cappeau, who wrote the French words, had a name as a socialist and a freethinker, and showed little interest in religion [^canterbury-fr][^nemausensis]. Later he rewrote the first verse, noting, as Havard de la Montagne quotes him, that he did not believe in original sin but did accept Jesus as a redeemer, of inequality, injustice and slavery [^musimem][^nemausensis]. An 1895 note from Nîmes says instead that the doubt soon passed [^nemausensis]. The composer, Adolphe Adam, wrote masses and other church music [^musimem][^delage].

### Was O Holy Night the first song on the radio?

Perhaps, but there is no record from the time, and even the piece is uncertain. The story rests on a letter the inventor Reginald Fessenden wrote in January 1932: on Christmas Eve 1906, from Brant Rock, Massachusetts, he played a record of Handel's Largo, then on the violin "a composition of Gounod called 'O, Holy Night'", ending with the words "Adore and be still", which are not in Adam's carol, and sang one verse [^hfessenden][^oneal][^dann]. No newspaper, radio log or calendar book of his from 1906 mentions it [^oneal][^halper].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune all through, in the verse and the refrain. With two singers you have the tune and the alto; with three, add the bass; with four, the tenor too. The tune runs from low C up to E, so take the low notes softly and the high ones gently, or move the key.
