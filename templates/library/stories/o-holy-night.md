O Holy Night is a Christmas solo that choirs have made their own. Its French words, Minuit, chrétiens ("Midnight, Christians"), are by Placide Cappeau, a wine merchant of Roquemaure in southern France, and its music is by Adolphe Adam, a Paris composer of comic opera and ballet. Both date from December 1847 [^cholvy][^musimem]. It was first sung at Midnight Mass in Roquemaure that Christmas, and within twenty years a French church-music journal was complaining that it was heard in streets, salons and café-concerts [^musimem][^lefort][^ivry]. The English most people sing, which begins "O holy night! the stars are brightly shining", is by John Sullivan Dwight, a Boston music critic and former Unitarian minister [^canterbury-dwight][^cooke][^gems1875].

## Where it comes from

**The words.** Cappeau was born in Roquemaure in 1808 (the Nîmes account says 25 October 1807), the son of a cooper [^canterbury-cappeau][^cholvy][^nemausensis]. As a boy he lost his right hand in an accident, so he could not follow his father's trade; the family of the boy who had caused it paid for his schooling instead [^cholvy][^nemausensis]. He read law in Paris and went home to sell wine, travelling to Paris often by stagecoach on business [^cholvy][^nemausensis]. He wrote poems in French and Provençal, and was a republican and a socialist in the sense of 1848 [^cholvy][^musimem][^ivry]. The parish priest of Roquemaure asked him for a Christmas poem, to raise money for the church's stained-glass windows by one account, or to mark the end of work on it by another [^canterbury-fr][^musimem][^lefort]. He wrote three verses [^canterbury-fr][^musimem][^cholvy].

**1843 or 1847?** Two dates are given for the poem, and the record favours 1847.
- The 1847 account comes from the history of the carol that René Durieu, parish priest of Roquemaure for sixteen years, built from the parish archives and private papers (1984). The historian Gérard Cholvy follows it: Cappeau wrote the verses on 3 December 1847 in the stagecoach to Paris, between Mâcon and Dijon, took them to Adam, and the carol was sung in Roquemaure that Christmas [^cholvy][^nemausensis][^musimem].
- The 1843 account is a memoir by the organist Antoine Chansroux, printed by the Academy of Nîmes in 1895, from what he had been told "several times". A hostess asks Cappeau for verses on 3 December 1843, he writes them next day in his carriage, and Adam's setting is sung at Midnight Mass that Christmas [^nemausensis]. The Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology says the poem "is believed" to date from 1843, and a Fondation Napoléon article says "about 1843" [^canterbury-fr][^lefort].

The 1843 story also puts Adam's music in 1843. Yet the reference books that give 1843 for the poem date the music to 1847, as every other source does, and neither explains the gap [^canterbury-fr][^lefort][^musimem][^imslp]. No document of 1843 is known, and both accounts start the story on 3 December. We give 1847.

**The tune.** The link to Adam was Emily Laurey, a former opera singer who had sung in his work, and whose husband, an engineer, was building a bridge over the Rhône at Roquemaure [^musimem][^nemausensis][^cholvy]. Adam had just founded his own opera house in Paris, the Opéra-National, and by Cholvy's account he hesitated at being given so few days; but he set the poem quickly [^delage][^musimem][^cholvy]. Emily Laurey sang it for the first time at Midnight Mass in Roquemaure, on the night of 24 to 25 December 1847 [^musimem][^lefort][^cholvy]. Adam, born in Paris in 1803 and dead there in 1856, is remembered most for his ballet Giselle (1841) [^musimem][^delage]. The carol has been called "la Marseillaise religieuse", the Marseillaise of religion, and the sources differ on who said it: some give the phrase to Adam, Cholvy to the poet Lamartine [^canterbury-fr][^musimem][^ivry][^cholvy].

**The earliest printing.** The earliest edition we know was published in Paris by the widow Canaux. It is undated; the Bavarian State Library catalogues its copy as about 1847 [^canaux]. The cover gives the words to "M. Pde Cappeau de Roquemaure", set to music "expressly for Madame Emily Laurey" by Adolphe Adam, with an optional reed organ, at 3 francs 50 [^canaux]. Inside, marked Andante maestoso, one voice sings over a piano part, in E-flat major [^canaux]. This copy was the publisher Schott's: German words are written in ink under the French, words on the cover are struck through, among them "son ami", his friend, before Adam's name, and it is marked "Aurora No 125", the number of Schott's own edition [^canaux][^ddb]. An 1895 note from Nîmes quotes covers reading "par son ami Adolphe Adam" [^nemausensis].

**How it travelled.** By about 1852, the date cataloguers give them, Paris publishers had issued a "sixth" edition, and Schott was selling it in Mainz, Brussels and London with French and German words [^momh][^imslp][^ddb]. In 1857 a young Quebec musician, Ernest Gagnon, heard it at Midnight Mass in Paris; the next year he brought it home [^bronze][^ivry].

**The English.** Dwight was born in Boston in 1813 [^cooke][^canterbury-en]. Ordained a Unitarian minister at Northampton, Massachusetts, in 1840, he left in 1841 for the Brook Farm community, and in 1852 founded Dwight's Journal of Music, which he edited for almost thirty years [^ripley][^cooke][^canterbury-dwight]. His English is usually dated 1855 [^canterbury-en][^hymnary-stw]. But no printing of that year has been found. We searched the text of every issue of his Journal from 1853 to 1860 and did not find it, and a carol historian who read the Journal from 1854 to 1858 found nothing either [^dwj][^hcc]. The earliest printing we have seen is a sheet-music "Christmas Song" of 1868, with "English words by J.S. Dwight" [^uw1868]. A carol historian reports an earlier one, a "Christmas Song" registered for copyright in 1858 by J. H. Hidley of Albany, New York, which we have not seen [^hcc]. Gems of English Song (Boston: Oliver Ditson, 1875) prints it as "Christmas Song", "English Words by J. S. Dwight, Esq.", "Music by Adolphe Adam", in E-flat with piano or reed organ, with all three verses [^gems1875]. He died in Boston on 5 September 1893 [^cooke][^canterbury-dwight].

**Myth and record.** Often told: church leaders in France banned the carol once they learned what kind of man had written the words. What the record shows: Catholic critics and church-music journals disliked its martial music and its words [^musimem][^ivry][^cholvy]. In 1864 the Revue de musique sacrée suggested dropping it as a song of streets and cafés [^musimem][^ivry]. In the 1920s reformers of church music called it theatrical, and a Metz choirmaster set out to drive it from the churches; in 1930 the Bishop of Metz asked his city's choirs to give it up [^cholvy]. One Catholic reference book says many dioceses dropped it, but none of the sources we read names a ban by France's bishops as a whole or by Rome [^ivry][^cholvy]. Catholics in France and Quebec kept singing it at Midnight Mass [^cholvy][^bronze].

Often told: abolitionists in America took up Dwight's third verse, "Chains shall he break, for the slave is our brother". What the record shows: the line comes from Cappeau, whose third verse says the Redeemer sees a brother where there was only a slave, and that love joins those whom iron chained [^cholvy][^musimem][^dann]. Dwight did hate slavery: in November 1861 his Journal said the Union was fighting "a treacherous and arrogant pro-Slavery rebellion" [^dwj1861][^cooke]. But none of the sources we read shows abolitionists singing the carol or quoting the verse; a 2023 article says it suited their cause and was popular in the North in the Civil War, but cites nothing from the time to show it [^coyne].

## What the words mean

Cappeau wrote three verses, each ending with a call to the people; his French survives in slightly different versions, and we give it as Schott printed it in about 1852, from the Paris edition [^ddb][^canaux][^musimem]. Dwight's three verses are a free version: they keep each verse's shape and some images, and say much in their own way [^musimem][^hutchins][^dann].

**Verse 1, the one we sing.** It is the night of Jesus' birth. The world lay long in sin and error until he came; then "the soul felt its worth", knowing its value. A shiver of hope runs through the worn-out world as a new morning breaks over there, "yonder" [^hutchins].

**The refrain.** "Fall on your knees! O hear the angel voices!": kneel, and listen to the angels of the Christmas story. "Night divine": a holy night, the night when Christ was born [^hutchins]. Cappeau's first verse is plainer theology: at midnight, the solemn hour, the God-man came down to wash away original sin and stop his Father's anger; the whole world trembles with hope; people, on your knees, "Peuple, à genoux", wait for your deliverance [^canaux][^cholvy]. Dwight's "thrill of hope" and "fall on your knees" echo those lines [^musimem][^hutchins].

**Verses 2 and 3 (not sung here).** Cappeau's second verse has the light of faith guide everyone to the cradle, as the chiefs of the East were once guided, and tells the proud to bow before a King born in a manger; Dwight keeps the wise men and the manger and adds a friend in our trials [^cholvy][^hutchins]. Cappeau's third says the Redeemer has broken every bond and ends: people, stand up and sing your deliverance. Dwight's makes it a teaching of love, an end to chains and oppression, and praise [^cholvy][^dann].

### Cappeau's French

Cappeau's three verses are given here as Schott's edition of Adam's carol, printed in Mainz in about 1852, sets them under the notes of its voice part; that copy is digitised by the Bavarian State Library [^ddb]. Its first verse matches the Paris edition word for word [^canaux][^ddb]. Its words and spelling are kept: it has "Chrétien" where later copies have "chrétiens", and "qu'un Dieu prêche" where some have "que Dieu prêche" [^ddb][^musimem]. The voice part has almost no punctuation, so the punctuation here is ours, and its "puis sans" in the second verse, a misprint, is given as "Puissants". The last line of each verse is sung twice. Below each French line is our own plain English.

when the God-man came down to us,

to wash away the stain of original sin
> Et de son père arrêter le courroux.
and to stop his Father's anger.

The whole world trembles with hope

on this night that gives it a Saviour.
> Peuple, à genoux, attends ta délivrance.
People, on your knees, wait for your deliverance.
> Noël ! Noël ! Voici le rédempteur.
Noël! Noël! Here is the Redeemer.

**Verse 2**

### The full words

These are John Sullivan Dwight's words. Verses 1 and 2 are as Carols Old and Carols New (Boston, 1916) prints them, the book our arrangement is read from, each ending with the solo's added last line [^hutchins]. That book leaves out Dwight's third verse, "Truly he taught us", given here as Hollis Dann's Christmas Carols and Hymns for School and Choir (New York, 1910) prints it [^dann]. Dann's first and second verses differ in places: in the second, where the 1916 book has "He guardeth us from danger", it has "To our weakness is no stranger" [^dann][^hutchins].

**Verse 1**
O holy night! the stars are brightly shining,
It is the night of the dear Saviour's birth;
Long lay the world in sin and error pining,
Till He appeared and the soul felt its worth.
A thrill of hope, the weary world rejoices,
For yonder breaks a new and glorious morn!
Fall on your knees, O hear the angel voices!
O night divine! O night when Christ was born!
O night divine, O night, O night divine!

**Verse 2**
Led by the light of faith serenely beaming,
With glowing hearts by His cradle we stand;
So led by light of a star sweetly gleaming,
Here came the wise men from the Orient land.
The King of kings lay thus in lowly manger,
In all our trials born to be our friend;
He knows our need, He guardeth us from danger,
Behold your King! Before the Lowly bend.
Behold your King! Before the Lowly bend!

**Verse 3**
Truly he taught us to love one another;
His law is love, and his gospel is peace;
Chains shall he break, for the slave is our brother,
And in his name all oppression shall cease.
Sweet hymns of joy in grateful chorus raise we,
Let all within us praise his holy name;
Christ is the Lord, Oh, praise his name forever!
His pow'r and glory evermore proclaim!
His pow'r and glory evermore proclaim!

## How it is sung

Adam wrote it for one voice, a woman's, with piano and an optional reed organ, and his Paris edition is in E-flat [^canaux][^cholvy]. Soon the great churches of Paris had it sung by leading baritones and tenors, and in French Canada singing it at Midnight Mass was an honour for a fine singer or the parish soloist [^cholvy][^lefort][^bronze][^ivry]. English hymnals call the tune CANTIQUE DE NOEL [^hymnary-stw]. Choirs sing it in arrangements such as Dudley Buck's for solo, choir and organ (New York, copyright 1881) [^buck][^imslp], or the 1916 Boston carol book's, with a soprano or tenor soloist in the verse and a choir in the refrain [^hutchins].

Our arrangement is read from that 1916 book, which keeps Adam's key of E-flat; we write it a minor third lower, in C, for 2 to 4 singers: the first verse and the refrain [^hutchins][^canaux]. We sing it slow and lilting in 6/8, the lilt of the book's triplet accompaniment [^hutchins]. In the verse, the alto and tenor sing our own quiet chords on the book's organ harmony, and the bass takes the organ's lowest notes. In the refrain they sing the book's choir parts, with a few notes changed so the lines move by step.

## Versions and other words

- **Two verses or three:** Carols Old and Carols New (1916) prints only Dwight's first two verses, leaving out "Truly he taught us", the verse with the chains [^hutchins]. Gems of English Song (1875) and Hollis Dann's Christmas Carols and Hymns for School and Choir (1910) print all three [^gems1875][^dann].
- **Cappeau's own rewrite:** In 1876, in a long poem of his own, Le Château de Roquemaure, Cappeau printed a new first verse with no God-man and no original sin, noting that he had changed what had "escaped" him at first about original sin, "in which we do not believe". Singers kept the first words [^cholvy][^musimem].

## Did you know?

- The earliest Paris printing gives the poet two names: "M. Pde Cappeau de Roquemaure" on its cover, and "Mary Cappeau" over the music inside [^canaux].
- Adam never mentioned the carol in his memoirs, published after his death [^musimem][^ivry][^adam1857].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/o-holy-night/soprano): the tune, slow and lilting, from a low C on "night" and "birth" up to its top E in "O night divine!". The refrain climbs, a long C on "Fall", D on "hear", E on "night".
- [Alto](/songs/o-holy-night/alto): quiet chords under the verse, mostly on E; on "soul felt its" it slips to E-flat, then back to E on "worth". In the refrain it sings the choir's part, up to G on "knees!", on the "O" of "O hear", and on "night" and "divine!" in "O night divine!".
- [Tenor](/songs/o-holy-night/tenor): the middle of the chords, mostly on C and G. From the first "night!" to "shining" it stays on C while the tune moves, then drops to a low E on "night of the" and "birth". Its top note is D on the last "night".
- [Bass](/songs/o-holy-night/bass): the organ's lowest notes in the verse and the choir's bass in the refrain, mostly on C and G, down to a low G under "night of the dear Saviour's", "thrill of hope" and "yonder breaks a". Under the first "O night divine!" it steps down A, G, F, E, one note to a syllable.

## Questions

### When was O Holy Night written?

In December 1847. Placide Cappeau wrote the French words, Minuit, chrétiens, on 3 December 1847, and Adolphe Adam set them within days for Midnight Mass that Christmas in Roquemaure [^cholvy][^musimem]. Some reference books give 1843 for the poem, but the earliest source we know for that year is a memoir of 1895, written down more than fifty years later from what its writer had been told [^canterbury-fr][^nemausensis]. Dwight's English is usually dated 1855; the earliest printing of it we have seen is of 1868, and a copyright entry of 1858 is reported [^canterbury-en][^uw1868][^hcc].

### What does "long lay the world in sin and error pining" mean?

It means the world lay for a long time in sin and error, wasting away and longing for help: to "pine" is to languish, or to waste away with longing [^webster1828]. The next line ends the waiting: "till he appeared", when Christ was born [^hutchins]. The line is Dwight's; Cappeau's French speaks instead of original sin washed away and a world trembling with hope [^canaux][^cholvy].

### Was O Holy Night written by an atheist?

No, though he was no churchman. Placide Cappeau, who wrote the French words, was a republican and a freethinker, and his critics in the church called him an unbeliever [^musimem][^cholvy]. In 1876 he wrote that he did not believe in original sin, and accepted Jesus as a redeemer from inequality, injustice and slavery [^cholvy][^musimem]. The historian Gérard Cholvy calls him a Christian on the margins, not an unbeliever [^cholvy]. The composer, Adolphe Adam, wrote masses and other church music [^delage][^musimem].

### Was O Holy Night the first song on the radio?

Perhaps, but there is no record from the time, and even the piece is uncertain. The story rests on a letter the inventor Reginald Fessenden wrote in January 1932: on Christmas Eve 1906, from Brant Rock, Massachusetts, he played a record of Handel's Largo, then on the violin "a composition of Gounod called 'O, Holy Night'", ending with the words "Adore and be still", which are not in Adam's carol, and sang one verse [^hfessenden][^oneal][^dann]. No newspaper, radio log or calendar book of his from 1906 mentions it [^oneal][^halper].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune all through, in the verse and the refrain. With two singers you have the tune and the alto; with three, add the bass; with four, the tenor too. The tune runs from low C up to E, so take the low notes softly and the high ones gently, or move the key.
