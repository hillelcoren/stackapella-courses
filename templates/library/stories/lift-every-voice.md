Lift Every Voice and Sing (often printed Lift Ev'ry Voice and Sing) is a song of faith and freedom made by two brothers from Jacksonville, Florida. James Weldon Johnson wrote the words, and his younger brother, J. Rosamond Johnson, wrote the music [^fenner][^loc-blog]. James wrote it for a celebration of Abraham Lincoln's birthday in 1900, when he was principal of the Stanton School, and a chorus of 500 schoolchildren sang it [^fenner][^naacp][^canterbury][^jaxson]. Its sheet music of that year already called it a "National Hymn for the Colored People of America" [^stern1900][^fenner]. The NAACP later took it up as its own song [^loc-blog][^pitt], and it is still widely known as the Black national anthem [^naacp][^yale-career]. Its three verses move from praise, to the memory of a hard road, to a prayer.

## Where it comes from

**Jacksonville, 1900.** James Weldon Johnson was born in Jacksonville in 1871 and his brother Rosamond in 1873 [^yale-early][^poets][^pitt]. Both went to the Stanton school there, where their mother taught, and James came back from college to be its principal [^yale-early][^canterbury]. Early in 1900 a group of young men in the city planned a celebration for Lincoln's birthday, 12 February, and asked James to speak [^fenner][^canterbury][^jaxson]. He wanted a song as well, for a chorus of 500 schoolchildren [^fenner][^jaxson].

**How it was made.** Johnson told the story himself in his autobiography, Along This Way (1933) [^fenner][^loc-blog]. He wrote the opening line, then the rest of the first stanza, and handed it to Rosamond, who set it to music [^fenner][^loc-blog]. The other two stanzas he made up without pen or paper, going over the lines in his head while his brother worked at the music [^fenner][^loc-blog], and he wrote that he wept as he made the last one [^fenner]. Copies were mimeographed in New York for the chorus, and the children sang it at the celebration on 12 February 1900, at the Stanton School [^fenner][^canterbury][^jaxson]. Johnson's manuscript dates it 1900, for the celebration; one essay and one local history place the writing in the winter of 1899 [^yale-origin][^peretti][^jaxson].

**The 1900 sheet music.** That same year Jos. W. Stern & Co., of New York, published it [^stern1900][^fenner]. Its title page reads: "National Hymn for the Colored People of America", then "Lift Every Voice and Sing", "Words by J. W. Johnson. Music by Rosamond Johnson" [^stern1900]. Over the music is a dedication to Booker T. Washington, misprinted "Booker J. Washington" [^stern1900][^fenner]. The music is in A-flat and 6/8, "Allo. mod.e maestoso" (moderately quick and majestic) [^stern1900]. Two staves of voice parts, for four voices, are marked "Simple way", above a fuller part for "Piano or Organ", with the note "If accompaniment is too difficult play voice parts" [^stern1900]. At "Sing a song full of the faith" the music turns "Grandioso", every voice sings the tune in octaves, and the organ part starts very soft ("pp", on the Swell's Bourdon stop) and breaks out very loud on "taught us" ("ff", "Gt. with Trumpets") [^stern1900]. It is marked "Copyright 1900 by Jos. W. Stern & Co.", plate 3071-3, and the copy now in the British Library was stamped by the British Museum on 3 November 1900: its earliest known printing [^stern1900][^fenner].

![[2]]

**Myth and record.** Often told: the music came only in 1905, it was not published until 1921, and Booker T. Washington heard the children first sing it. What the record shows: the Stern sheet, with Rosamond Johnson's music, is marked "Copyright 1900", and the Library of Congress has it registered that year [^stern1900][^loc-blog][^fenner]. It is dedicated to Washington, but Johnson's own account of the day never says he was there [^fenner][^loc-blog].

![[3]]

**How it spread.** In 1901 the brothers went to New York to write songs and shows with Bob Cole, though James went back to Jacksonville for a time: Yale has him principal of Stanton until 1902 [^loc-blog][^peretti][^pitt][^yale-origin]. Once they had moved away for good, Johnson wrote, they all but forgot the hymn [^fenner]. Jacksonville's children kept singing it, and within about twenty years it was sung in schools and churches across the South and beyond, often from copies pasted into hymnals [^fenner][^loc-blog]. In 1918 it opened a benefit concert at Aeolian Hall, New York, for the Music School Settlement for Colored People, which Rosamond Johnson directed. *The New York Times* and the *Musical Courier* both called it a national anthem for Black Americans [^nyt1918][^mc1918]. James joined the NAACP's staff in 1916 and from 1920 led it as executive secretary for ten years [^yale-career][^poets][^pitt]. The NAACP made Lift Every Voice and Sing its official song, but accounts differ on when. A Library of Congress blog gives 1919 [^loc-blog]. A Library of Congress essay says Johnson's NAACP colleagues proclaimed it "the Negro national anthem" in 1920 [^peretti]. *Mother Jones* gives 1921 [^motherjones], and a University of Pittsburgh guide says only the 1920s [^pitt]. The Hymnology Archive says only that it was later adopted, with no year [^fenner]. James died in 1938, and Rosamond in 1954 [^poets][^loc-blog][^pitt].

## What the words mean

Johnson wrote three stanzas. We sing the first.

- **Stanza 1.** A call to everyone to sing, until earth and heaven ring with liberty. Let the joy rise as high as the listening sky and sound as loud as the sea, a song of the faith a dark past has taught and the hope the present has brought. Facing the sunrise of a new day, march on until the victory is won.
- **Stanza 2.** The road behind. "Stony the road we trod": the way was hard. "Bitter the chast'ning rod": the punishment was cruel ("chast'ning" is chastening, punishing), in days when hope died before it was born. Yet with weary, steady feet the people have come to the place their forebears longed for, by a path watered with tears and through the blood of those who were killed, out of a gloomy past into the light of their hope.
- **Stanza 3.** A prayer to the "God of our weary years" and "silent tears", who has led the people this far, into the light. It asks God to keep them from straying, or from forgetting God when the world's good things go to their heads ("drunk with the wine of the world"). It ends asking that they may stand "True to our God, true to our Native land" [^stern1900].

### The full words

Here are all three stanzas as the 1900 Stern sheet prints them, with its spelling and punctuation, in the poem's lines [^stern1900].

Lift ev'ry voice and sing
Till earth and heaven ring,
Ring with the harmonies of Liberty;
Let our rejoicing rise
High as the list'ning skies,
Let it resound loud as the rolling sea.
Sing a song full of the faith that the dark past has taught us
Sing a song full of the hope that the present has brought us;
Facing the rising sun
Of our new day begun,
Let us march on till victory is won.

Stony the road we trod,
Bitter the chast'ning rod
Felt in the days when hope unborn had died;
Yet, with a steady beat,
Have not our weary feet
Come to the place for which our fathers sighed?
We have come over a way that with tears has been watered
We have come, treading our path thro' the blood of the slaughtered,
Out from the gloomy past,
Till now we stand at last
Where the white gleam of our bright star is cast.

God of our weary years,
God of our silent tears,
Thou who hast brought us thus far on the way;
Thou who hast by Thy might,
Led us into the light,
Keep us forever in the path, we pray,
Lest our feet stray from the places, our God, where we met Thee,
Lest our hearts, drunk with the wine of the world, we forget Thee:
Shadowed beneath Thy hand,
May we forever stand,
True to our God, true to our Native land.

## How it is sung

It is often sung to open or close a meeting, first at Lincoln's birthday celebrations, then at civic, school and church gatherings [^yale-impact][^nyt1918]. In letters now at Yale, Johnson answered people who asked how to sing it, down to how loudly to sing each part [^yale-impact]. Later printings set it for mixed quartet and for three-part chorus [^yale-origin], and today it is in the hymnals of many churches, in an irregular meter [^hymnary].

Our arrangement is read from Rosamond Johnson's own four voice parts in the 1900 Stern sheet [^stern1900], checked against a later "Mixed Quartette" printing at Yale, signed by Rosamond Johnson [^yale-quartet]. We sing the first stanza a semitone lower, in G, in 6/8, broad and stately, with a breath before every line of the poem but "Of our new day begun", which runs straight on from "Facing the rising sun". His pause on "us" is written out as a held note and an extra bar before "Facing the rising sun". The tune is his, sung one note to a syllable (two of his slurred notes are left out). His alto, tenor and bass are kept, with some notes changed for beginners, most of them in the quick pickups. Where his voices all sing the tune in octaves, at "Sing a song full of the faith" and "full of the hope", our alto, tenor and bass hold his organ part's chords under the tune instead. Two singers sing the soprano and alto, three add the bass, and the tenor makes four.

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/lift-every-voice/soprano): you carry Rosamond Johnson's tune. It climbs to its top E twice, on "rise, High" and on "sun, Of". In "full of the faith that the dark past has" it drops to a low B; keep those quick notes light. The next line ends with a leap of nearly an octave, from C on "has" up to B on "brought", then a held C on "us"; take a full breath at the start of the line.
- [Alto](/songs/lift-every-voice/alto): you sing under the tune, mostly on B, E and D. In "Sing a song full of the faith that the dark past has" you hold one B for every syllable, and in the next line slip down a half step to B-flat. Your top G-sharp comes on "rise, High" and "sun, Of", a sixth under the tune.
- [Tenor](/songs/lift-every-voice/tenor): you sing in the middle of the chords, mostly on G, A and B, up to D on "loud" and "till". Through both "Sing a song" lines you hold a steady G while the tune moves above you; solo it in the player.
- [Bass](/songs/lift-every-voice/bass): you sing the bass line, mostly G, D and E. You hold a low G through both "Sing a song full of" lines, the floor under the middle of the song, and land on low G again on "sea" and "won".

## Questions

### Who wrote the music for Lift Every Voice and Sing?

J. Rosamond Johnson (1873 to 1954), the younger brother of James Weldon Johnson, who wrote the words. James gave him the first stanza and he set it to music in Jacksonville in 1900, for a Lincoln's birthday celebration [^fenner][^loc-blog]. The Stern sheet music of that year reads "Music by Rosamond Johnson" and is marked "Copyright 1900", not 1905, as is often said [^stern1900][^fenner].

### Why is Lift Every Voice and Sing called the Black national anthem?

Because Black Americans took it up as their own national song. Its 1900 sheet music already called it a "National Hymn for the Colored People of America", and by 1918 a New York newspaper and a music weekly called it a national anthem [^stern1900][^nyt1918][^mc1918]. The NAACP then made it its official song [^loc-blog][^pitt]. Long called the Negro National Anthem, it is now known as the Black national anthem [^yale-impact][^naacp].

### What is the Lift Every Voice and Sing sculpture?

It is a sculpture by Augusta Savage, made for the New York World's Fair of 1939 and named after the song; it is also called The Harp [^studiomuseum][^saam]. It stood 16 feet tall, in plaster, with a row of singers rising in height like a harp's strings. With no money to cast it, it was destroyed when the fair closed [^saam][^studiomuseum].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune all the way through, from "Lift ev'ry voice and sing" to "till victory is won". In Rosamond Johnson's own setting every voice joins the tune in octaves for both "Sing a song" lines [^stern1900]. In ours the alto, tenor and bass hold chords there instead, so the tune stays clear on top.
