Loch Lomond, also called The Bonnie Banks o' Loch Lomond or known by its chorus, "Ye'll tak' the high road", is a Scottish song of two parted lovers. Its singer remembers walking with a true love on the banks of Loch Lomond, the largest stretch of fresh water in Britain, and its chorus ends "me and my true love will never meet again" [^sotn][^ecn][^visitscot]. Nobody knows who wrote it. Its earliest known printing is an Edinburgh sheet of about 1840, titled Bonnie Loch Loman [^browns][^tbi]. By the 1890s, after years half forgotten, it was popular again, and by 1904 a story was in print that its chorus was a Jacobite soldier's farewell before his execution at Carlisle [^greig][^ford].

## Where it comes from

**The earliest printing.** The earliest known printing is a song sheet titled Bonnie Loch Loman, published by Paterson & Roy of 27 George Street, Edinburgh, in about 1840. In 1911 an Aberdeen bookseller's magazine, Brown's Book-Stall, reprinted its words with that imprint, quoting the folk-song collector Gavin Greig: its seven verses were given as "written by a lady", and the music as arranged by Finlay Dun [^browns]. The Ballad Index gives 1841, naming The Vocal Melodies of Scotland, the firm's series of Scottish songs arranged by Dun and John Thomson [^tbi][^thirty]. Alfred Moffat had given the same account in the 1890s: Paterson's firm, Finlay Dun and a lady [^moffat].

![[1]]

**A Jacobite lament.** That first text already looks back on the 1745 rising. Its chorus runs "But trouble it is there, and mony hearts are sair", and later verses mourn the hopes that "On Culloden field they perish'd" and recall the looks of Prince Charlie [^browns]. The song sung now, Brown's Book-Stall noted, keeps only its first, second and seventh verses, reworded [^browns].

**The lady and the street singer.** The song was often credited to Lady John Scott, who wrote the tune of Annie Laurie [^moffat][^ford][^thirty]. She said otherwise. Moffat's Minstrelsy of Scotland reports a letter from her: she and her husband "picked up both words and air from a poor little boy who was singing on the streets of Edinburgh", and she did not think the song was known before that [^moffat][^browns]. Moffat was also told she had been credited with it while it was still passed around in manuscript, before anyone printed it [^moffat][^browns]. She died in 1900, and the memoir of her life printed with her Thirty Songs in 1910 never mentions the song [^thirty][^berwick].

**The tune.** Moffat thought the air grew from an old Scottish tune, Kind Robin lo'es me, which William McGibbon printed in 1742 as Robin Cushie, a printing that John Glen, the historian of early Scottish tunes, also records [^moffat][^glen]. The Ballad Index, weighing a list of possible older tunes, finds noticeable differences in each and calls the question unsettled [^tbi].

**Songs of the North.** The earliest printing with this tune that we have seen is in Songs of the North, a book of Scottish songs edited by A. C. Macleod and Harold Boulton, with the piano parts by Malcolm Lawson [^sotn]. Our copy, a later Cramer reprint, carries no date. The book first came out in London from Field and Tuer in 1885: that July the Illustrated London News and Chambers's Journal both noticed it as a handsome new volume [^iln1885][^chambers1885]. The song's music page, headed "Loch Lomond", reads "Old Scottish Song" on one side and "Traditional Melody arranged by Malcolm Lawson" on the other. It is in F, marked "With much feeling and rather slow", and the refrain is marked "Brisker" [^sotn]. A page of its own then prints the whole poem, three verses each with the chorus, as The Bonnie Banks o' Loch Lomond [^sotn].

![[2]]

**Myth and record.** Often told: a Jacobite soldier of the 1745 rising, condemned to die at Carlisle, spoke the chorus to his sweetheart at the scaffold. She would go home by the high road; his spirit would reach Scotland first by the low road, the road of the dead [^ford][^tbi]. What the record shows: no copy of the song is known from before about 1840, nearly a century after the rising [^browns][^tbi]. That earliest text already mourns Culloden, but it has no prisoner, no Carlisle and no scaffold [^browns]. The story grew in print later. In the 1890s Scots Minstrelsie said the song was "supposed to have been written about 1746", and linked it to Prince Charles Edward Stuart's retreat from Derby; in 1904 Ford gave the Carlisle scaffold as "the tradition" [^greig][^ford]. The novelist William Black thought the whole song recent, Ford reports [^ford].

### Timeline

- 1742: William McGibbon prints Robin Cushie, the old air Moffat thought Loch Lomond grew from [^glen][^moffat]
- About 1840: Paterson & Roy of Edinburgh publish Bonnie Loch Loman, seven verses "written by a lady", the earliest known printing [^browns][^tbi]
- 1885: Songs of the North prints it as an "Old Scottish Song" [^sotn][^iln1885][^chambers1885]
- 1890s: Scots Minstrelsie ties the song to the Jacobite retreat of 1745 [^greig]
- 1904: Robert Ford prints the Carlisle story and the verses credited to Lady John Scott's street singer [^ford]
- 1921: Ralph Vaughan Williams's setting for baritone and men's chorus is published in London [^imslp-vw]

## What the words mean

The song is in English with Scots words in it. Bonnie means beautiful, yon means that one over there, and a brae is a hillside [^hh4][^gebbie6].

**Verse 1.** By those lovely banks and slopes, where the sun shines bright on Loch Lomond, where my true love and I were always used to go (wont to gae: accustomed to go), on the banks of Loch Lomond [^sotn][^hh4]. Lomon' is Lomond with its last sound dropped, as the book spells it for singing [^sotn].

![[3]]

**Chorus.** You will take the high road and I will take the low road, and I will be in Scotland before (afore) you. But my true love and I will never meet again on the banks of Loch Lomond [^sotn][^hh4]. The words never say what the two roads are; the road of the dead is the later reading told above [^ford][^tbi].

**Verse 2.** It was there that we parted, in that shady glen, a deep valley, on the steep side of Ben Lomond, the mountain above the loch. There we see the Highland (Hieland) hills in purple, and the moon coming out in the gloaming, the twilight [^moffat][^gebbie6][^greig].

![[4]]

**Verse 3.** The little birds sing and the wild flowers spring up, and the waters sleep in the sunshine. But a broken heart knows (kens) no (nae) second spring, though the sorrowful (waefu') may stop their weeping (greetin') [^gebbie6][^hh4]. Under the notes the book has "frae their greeting"; on the page of words, "from their greetin'" [^sotn].

### The full words

These are the words as Songs of the North prints them on their own page, headed The Bonnie Banks o' Loch Lomond and signed "Old Scottish Song", with spelling and punctuation as printed; the chorus is printed in full after each verse [^sotn]. We sing the first two verses.

**Verse 1**
By yon bonnie banks and by yon bonnie braes,
Where the sun shines bright on Loch Lomon',
Where me and my true love were ever wont to gae,
On the bonnie, bonnie banks o' Loch Lomon'.

**Chorus**
O ye'll tak' the high road and I'll tak' the low road,
And I'll be in Scotland afore ye,
But me and my true love will never meet again
On the bonnie, bonnie banks o' Loch Lomon'.

**Verse 2**
'Twas there that we parted in yon shady glen,
On the steep, steep side o' Ben Lomon',
Where in purple hue the Hieland hills we view,
And the moon coming out in the gloamin'.

**Chorus**
O ye'll tak' the high road and I'll tak' the low road,
And I'll be in Scotland afore ye,
But me and my true love will never meet again
On the bonnie, bonnie banks o' Loch Lomon'.

**Verse 3**
The wee birdies sing and the wild flowers spring,
And in sunshine the waters are sleepin';
But the broken heart it kens nae second spring again
Though the waefu' may cease from their greetin'.

**Chorus**
O ye'll tak' the high road and I'll tak' the low road,
And I'll be in Scotland afore ye,
But me and my true love will never meet again,
On the bonnie, bonnie banks o' Loch Lomon'.

## How it is sung

It is a slow song of parting, sung with feeling, and Songs of the North asks for a little more movement in the chorus [^sotn]. In the 1890s Scots Minstrelsie called it a song "comparatively forgotten for many a long day" that had lately become very popular, and a few years later Ford wrote of its "vogue in the highest circles" [^greig][^ford]. Then it went to choirs and dance bands: Ralph Vaughan Williams set it for baritone and men's chorus, published in 1921, and a swing recording made in August 1937 was a big hit [^imslp-vw][^synco][^tbi].

Our arrangement is read from John Reid's Songs of Scotland (New York, 1908), moved from E up to G, and checked against Scots Minstrelsie, whose even rhythm on "By" and "bonnie" and whose "Lomon'" we sing [^reid1908][^minstrelsie3]. We sing the first two verses, each with the chorus, for 2 to 4 singers, slow and tender, everyone on the words.

## Versions and other words

- **The Jacobite verses.** Ford printed a longer song, Oh! whither away, my bonnie, bonnie May, as the verses Lady John Scott picked up in the streets [^ford][^browns]. In it a girl's Ranald marches "To fecht for the King an' Prince Charlie", and the chorus turns: "Oh! he'll gang the hie road an' I'll gang the low, But I'll be in Heaven afore him" [^ford]. Here it is the girl, not the soldier, who expects to die.
- **A novelist's own words.** In William Black's novel Wild Eelin (1898), the heroine sings her own verses to the chorus and calls the usual words "spurious" [^black].

## Did you know?

- The writer Andrew Lang made his own Jacobite poem on the chorus, headed 1746, in which the singer has just come out "Free o' Carlisle gaol in the dawing" [^lang].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/loch-lomond/soprano): you carry the tune, from the D above middle C up to the E a ninth higher. Your top E comes on "banks" in every "On the bonnie, bonnie banks", on "me and" in each "me and my true love", on "afore" in both choruses, on "Lomon'" in "bright on Loch Lomon'" and "side o' Ben Lomon'", and on "purple" and "out" in the second verse. In the chorus the tune sinks to its lowest note, D, on "low road".
- [Alto](/songs/loch-lomond/alto): you sing the words a sixth or a third under the tune, low and mostly on B and C. You go above F-sharp only in the second verse, on "Ben Lomon', Where in purple hue the", climbing to your top B on "Lomon', Where in": practise that phrase on its own.
- [Tenor](/songs/loch-lomond/tenor): you sing in the middle of the chord, mostly on G, with E and A. You dip to low D on "shines bright" in the first verse and on "Scotland" in each chorus, and reach your top D only in the second verse, on "Ben Lomon', Where in".
- [Bass](/songs/loch-lomond/bass): you sing the words on the roots, apart from five passing B's, and sit on a low G for more than half the song, like a drone. You move to C and D where the chords change: each verse and chorus ends on C, then G, then D on "Lo-" (on "gloam-" in the second verse) before home to G.

## Questions

### What does "the high road and the low road" mean in Loch Lomond?

The words do not say. Read plainly, two people go home to Scotland by different roads, and the one on the low road arrives first [^sotn]. The popular reading, in print by 1904, is that a condemned Jacobite of 1745 is speaking, and the low road is the road of the dead [^ford][^tbi]. No copy of the song is known from before about 1840, so that story cannot be traced back to 1745, though the earliest text already mourns Culloden [^browns][^tbi].

### Who wrote "Loch Lomond", and when?

Nobody knows. Its earliest known printing is a sheet titled Bonnie Loch Loman, published in Edinburgh by Paterson & Roy in about 1840, and the song may be older [^browns][^tbi][^moffat]. It was often credited to Lady John Scott, the composer of Annie Laurie, but she said she and her husband learned the words and tune from a boy singing in the streets of Edinburgh [^moffat][^browns]. The idea that it was written in 1746 came later, though the earliest text already mourns Culloden [^greig][^browns].

### How do you pronounce "Loch Lomond", and what is a loch?

A loch is a lake, the word used for natural lakes all over Scotland [^dsl-loch]. The Dictionary of the Scots Language gives loch as [lɔx]: the ch is the breathy sound made at the back of the mouth, as in German Bach, not a k [^dsl-loch]. In the song the name is sung "Lomon'", the last d dropped, as Songs of the North spells it [^sotn].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, from the D above middle C up to the E a ninth higher. With two singers the alto sings the same words a sixth or a third beneath it; three adds the bass on the roots, and four adds the tenor in the middle of the chord. Everyone sings the words.
