See Amid the Winter's Snow, often printed See, Amid the Winter's Snow, is an English Christmas hymn about the child in the manger who made the stars, and a prayer to learn his humility. Edward Caswall, a member of John Henry Newman's Oratory in Birmingham, wrote the words; the earliest printing we have seen is in his book The Masque of Mary of 1858 [^masque1858][^julian]. John Goss, organist of St Paul's Cathedral, wrote its tune, HUMILITY, and its four-part chorus for the second series of Bramley and Stainer's Christmas Carols New and Old, on sale by December 1870 [^mt1870][^praise][^imslp]. A century and more later, Common Praise, the 2000 edition of Hymns Ancient and Modern, sets it to Goss's tune [^cp2000][^praise].

## Where it comes from

**The poet.** Edward Caswall was born at Yateley in Hampshire on 15 July 1814, the son of the vicar [^julian][^dnb-caswall]. He took his degree at Brasenose College, Oxford, in 1836, was ordained in the Church of England and took charge of the parish of Stratford-sub-Castle, near Salisbury [^julian][^dnb-caswall]. He gave up the parish and became a Roman Catholic: the Dictionary of National Biography dates his reception to January 1847, while Julian's Dictionary of Hymnology puts it in 1850 [^dnb-caswall][^julian]. His wife died in 1849, and in 1850 he joined Newman at the Oratory in Birmingham, where, Julian says, most of his own poems and hymns were written [^julian][^dnb-caswall][^praise]. He was best known for his translations of Latin hymns, collected in Lyra Catholica in 1849; Julian ranked their reach in hymnals second only to John Mason Neale's [^julian][^praise].

**The words, 1858.** The Masque of Mary, and Other Poems came out in London from Burns and Lambert in 1858, its title page naming the author as "Edward Caswall, of the Oratory, Birmingham" [^masque1858][^dnb-caswall]. In its section of hymns and meditative pieces, No. XLII is headed simply "Hymn for Christmas", on pages 259 and 260 [^masque1858][^julian]. It has seven stanzas of four lines, and the chorus, "Hail, thou ever-blessed morn!", is printed in full after the first and cued as "Hail, &c." after the rest [^masque1858][^julian]. The poem just after it, No. XLIII, "To the Infant Jesus Asleep", is another Christmas poem for the same child [^masque1858]. Caswall reprinted the hymn in his collected Hymns and Poems of 1873 [^hp1873][^julian].

**Which year?** Julian's dictionary gives The Masque of Mary of 1858 as the place it was published [^julian][^hymnary-text]. The Praise! hymnal's notes, and the Cyber Hymnal entry quoted on Hymnary.org, say it was also in a book of Easy Hymn Tunes for Catholic schools in 1851 [^praise][^hymnary-text]. We have not found a copy of that book, so 1858 is the earliest printing we have seen.

**The tune, 1870.** John Goss was born at Fareham in Hampshire on 27 December 1800 [^dnb-goss][^stpauls][^ha-goss]. He sang as a boy in the Chapel Royal, studied with Thomas Attwood, and in 1838 followed Attwood as organist of St Paul's Cathedral [^dnb-goss][^ha-goss][^hymnary-goss]. Henry Ramsden Bramley, a Fellow of Magdalen College, Oxford, and John Stainer, the college's organist, had already brought out twenty carols as Christmas Carols New and Old [^ha-carols][^mt1870][^hymnary-bramley][^dnb-stainer][^ha-stainer]. On 1 December 1870 Novello's advertisement in The Musical Times listed a second series of twenty-two more, with "30. Christmas Hymn ... John Goss" among them [^mt1870][^ha-carols]. In the same issue the paper's reviewer welcomed the new names of Goss, Henry Smart and Arthur Sullivan, though he found that "the old traditional airs bear away the palm" over the modern ones, and he did not mention No. 30 [^mt1870].

**Which year for the tune?** IMSLP and the Praise! notes give 1871 for the tune's first publication [^imslp][^praise]. The Hymnology Archive dates the second series to 1870, and The Musical Times shows it on sale by 1 December 1870 [^ha-carols][^mt1870].

**The page we sing from.** We read the hymn in the complete edition of all seventy carols, in a printing whose title page calls the music editor "Sir John Stainer", so after his knighthood in 1888 [^bs-complete][^ha-stainer][^dnb-stainer]. This copy belonged to the Rev. Louis FitzGerald Benson, who left it to Princeton Theological Seminary [^bs-complete]. On page 64, No. XXX is headed "Hymn for Christmas Day", Moderato, with the verse for "Solo" and a footnote, "Treble or Tenor, or alternately", over a quiet keyboard part marked p [^bs-complete]. Page 65 turns to "ff Chorus", "Hail! Thou ever blessed morn!", in four voices on two staves, then prints verses 2 to 6, each ending "Hail, thou ever-blessed, &c." [^bs-complete]. The index on page 183 lists it as "See amid the Winter snow", words by "The Rev. E. Caswall", air by "Sir John Goss" [^bs-complete].

**After.** The year 1872 brought Goss a knighthood and his retirement from St Paul's, and Stainer became its organist in his place [^ha-goss][^ha-stainer][^dnb-stainer]. Caswall died at the Oratory in Edgbaston on 2 January 1878 and was buried at Rednall, near Bromsgrove, in the Oratory's burial ground; Julian adds that Newman himself buried him [^julian][^dnb-caswall]. Goss died at Brixton on 10 May 1880 [^dnb-goss][^stpauls][^ha-goss].

## What the words mean

**Verse 1.** Luke's Gospel says nothing of the season or of snow; the winter scene is the poet's [^kjv]. "The tender Lamb" is Christ, whom John the Baptist greets in John's Gospel with "Behold the Lamb of God" [^kjv]. "Promised from eternal years" means promised from before time began.

**The chorus.** The birth is greeted as the morning of the world's redemption, "Redemption's happy dawn". The news is to be sung through Jerusalem, the holy city, but the child is born in Bethlehem, the town where Luke's shepherds go to find him [^kjv].

**Verse 2.** The child in the manger is the one "who built the starry skies", as the psalm calls the heavens "the work of thy fingers, the moon and the stars" [^kjv]. The same child is "throned in height sublime", enthroned in heaven among the cherubim, the heavenly beings between whom, in the psalm, God dwells: "thou that dwellest between the cherubims" [^kjv].

**Verses 3 and 4, which we leave out.** The singer asks the shepherds why they have left their sheep on the hillside, and they answer, in quotation marks, that at dead of night they saw a light and heard angels sing of peace on earth and a Saviour's birth, as in Luke 2:8 to 14 [^bs-complete][^kjv]. The two verses are a question and its answer, so we leave out the pair together.

**Verse 5.** "What a tender love" it was to come from "highest bliss", the joy of heaven, down to "such a world as this". Saint Paul says the same of Christ, that he "made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant" [^kjv].

**Verse 6.** The hymn ends as a prayer: teach us to be like you. "Meek and mild" echoes Christ's "I am meek and lowly in heart", and "humility" Paul's "he humbled himself" [^kjv]. The verse's last word gave Goss's tune its name [^praise][^bs-complete].

**The seventh stanza.** Caswall's 1858 text ends with a prayer to Mary, "Virgin Mother, Mary blest", asking her to pray that we may prove worthy of the Saviour's love [^masque1858]. Bramley and Stainer print six verses and leave it out, and the Praise! notes say later editors have usually dropped it too [^bs-complete][^praise].

**Small changes.** Against the 1858 page, Bramley and Stainer print "thro'" for "through", "a Saviour's Birth" for "the Saviour's birth", and "Holy Child" with a capital, and they drop the commas after "See" in verse 1 [^bs-complete][^masque1858]. Under the music their chorus begins "Hail! Thou ever blessed morn!", where Caswall wrote "Hail, thou ever-blessed morn!" [^bs-complete][^masque1858].

### The full words

These are the six verses and the chorus as Christmas Carols New and Old prints them at No. XXX, verse 1 and the chorus under the music and verses 2 to 6 below it, each of which ends with a cue for the chorus [^bs-complete]. Caswall died in 1878, so every verse is in the public domain. Caswall's seventh stanza, of 1858, is described above.

**Verse 1**
See amid the winter's snow,
Born for us on earth below,
See the tender Lamb appears,
Promised from eternal years.

**Chorus**
Hail! Thou ever blessed morn!
Hail, Redemption's happy dawn!
Sing thro' all Jerusalem,
Christ is born in Bethlehem.

**Verse 2**
Lo, within a manger lies
He who built the starry skies;
He, who throned in height sublime,
Sits amid the Cherubim!

**Verse 3**
Say, ye holy Shepherds, say,
What your joyful news to-day;
Wherefore have ye left your sheep
On the lonely mountain steep?

**Verse 4**
"As we watched at dead of night,
Lo, we saw a wondrous light;
Angels singing peace on earth,
Told us of a Saviour's Birth."

**Verse 5**
Sacred Infant, all Divine,
What a tender love was Thine;
Thus to come from highest bliss
Down to such a world as this!

**Verse 6**
Teach, O teach us, Holy Child,
By Thy face so meek and mild,
Teach us to resemble Thee,
In Thy sweet humility!

## How it is sung

It is a hymn for Christmas, in seven-syllable lines, 77 77 with a refrain of the same shape, and its tune is HUMILITY, which some books call HUMILITY (OXFORD) [^cp2000][^hymnary-tune][^masque1858]. Hymnary.org lists other tunes with these words, such as CHRISTMAS MORN and WINTER'S SNOW, but the Praise! notes say Goss's tune has "as yet no rivals" [^hymnary-text][^praise]. The Praise! notes also say that it was not in The English Hymnal, and that Hymns Ancient and Modern took it in only with Common Praise in 2000, in five stanzas [^praise][^cp2000].

Bramley and Stainer meant it to be sung in turns: a single voice, a treble or a tenor, or the two taking verses in turn, sings each verse quietly over the keyboard, and the chorus follows in four parts, marked ff, very loud [^bs-complete].

Our arrangement is read from No. XXX of Christmas Carols New and Old, in the book's key of G, in common time at a moderate pace [^bs-complete]. We sing verses 1, 2, 5 and 6, each followed by the chorus, with the book's words and spellings. The soprano sings Goss's tune note for note. The chorus keeps Goss's four parts and nearly all his chords, with some notes of the alto and tenor exchanged and the bass's first two bars an octave lower, so that two or three singers still have a full chord. The book gives the verse to one voice, so the lower parts of the verses are our own, built on its keyboard part. It is for 2 to 4 singers: two take soprano and alto, a third adds the bass, and the tenor makes four.

## Did you know?

- The poem after this hymn in Caswall's 1858 book, "To the Infant Jesus Asleep", begins "Sleep, Holy Babe"; Bramley and Stainer set it too, as No. 9 of their first series, to a tune by J. B. Dykes [^masque1858][^mt1870][^bs-complete].
- The hymn's two musicians were linked at St Paul's: when Goss gave up the post of organist in 1872, John Stainer, the book's music editor, took his place [^ha-stainer][^ha-goss][^dnb-stainer].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/see-amid-the-winters-snow/soprano): Goss's tune, from low D to top D, most often on G. Each verse's first and third lines are the same tune, opening with a long "See" and sinking to the low D at the line's end ("winter's snow", "Lamb appears"); its highest notes in the verse are the C's on "us" in "Born for us" and on "e-" in "eternal". The top D comes only in the chorus, on "Hail! Thou" and "Hail, Re-", and the two lines from there step down the same way, note by note, to F-sharp. It comes once more on "Christ".
- [Alto](/songs/see-amid-the-winters-snow/alto): under the tune, most often on B, then D. Its low G comes on "win-" in "winter's" and on "Lamb", and its top F-sharp on "for" and "below" in the second line. In the chorus, "all Jerusalem" brings two C-sharps, outside the key, on "all" and "ru-"; then the alto climbs above the tune for "-sa-lem", ending on F-sharp over the tune's low D. Tune those notes carefully.
- [Tenor](/songs/see-amid-the-winters-snow/tenor): the middle of the chord, on G well over half the time. In the chorus "Hail! Thou ever" is all on one G, and so is "Christ is born in"; keep them steady. Its top C comes in each verse's second line, on "for us" in verse 1. Its low D comes on "the" in "the winter's", on "-der" in "tender", on "Je-" in "Jerusalem", and on the very last note of the song, where the tenor holds the D while the others end on G and B.
- [Bass](/songs/see-amid-the-winters-snow/bass): the bass line, most often on G, then D and B. It opens each verse climbing by step from G, "See amid the win-". Its low F-sharp comes only on "bless-" in "blessed morn", after four G's on "Hail! Thou ever". Its top E comes on "us" in "Born for us", at the start of the last line of each verse ("Promised"), and on "Sing thro'" in the chorus.

## Questions

### What does See Amid the Winter's Snow mean?

It says that the child born at Bethlehem is the one who made the stars and sits among the angels of heaven, now lying in a manger out of love [^bs-complete][^masque1858]. The chorus hails his birth as "Redemption's happy dawn" [^masque1858]. Its last verse turns to prayer: "Teach us to resemble Thee, In Thy sweet humility", echoing Christ's words "I am meek and lowly in heart" [^bs-complete][^kjv].

### Why is the tune called Humility?

Because "humility" is the last word of the verses, at the end of the prayer "Teach us to resemble Thee, In Thy sweet humility!", and the Praise! hymnal's notes say the tune is named from it [^praise][^bs-complete]. Bramley and Stainer gave the tune no name: their page is headed "Hymn for Christmas Day", and their 1870 list calls it "Christmas Hymn" [^bs-complete][^mt1870]. Hymnals now print it as HUMILITY, some as HUMILITY (OXFORD) [^hymnary-tune][^cp2000].

### Is See Amid the Winter's Snow for SATB choir?

The chorus is: Goss wrote "Hail! Thou ever blessed morn!" for four voices, printed on two staves [^bs-complete]. The verses are not. Christmas Carols New and Old gives each verse to one voice, "Treble or Tenor, or alternately", over a keyboard part, so a choir sings the verse in unison or gives it to a soloist [^bs-complete]. Our arrangement adds lower parts to the verses so that 2 to 4 singers can harmonize the whole hymn.

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings HUMILITY, note for note as Christmas Carols New and Old prints it: the verse from the solo line and the chorus from its top part [^bs-complete]. In the book the verse could also go to a tenor [^bs-complete]. With two singers, take soprano and alto; a third adds the bass, and a fourth the tenor.
