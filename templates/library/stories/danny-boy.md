Danny Boy is a song of parting. Its words are by Frederic Weatherly, an English barrister and lyricist, who wrote them in 1910 and published them in 1913 set to the Londonderry Air, an Irish tune that George Petrie had printed in 1855 without a name.[^dillane][^mann][^imslp][^petrie] Someone at home tells Danny that the pipes are calling him away, and promises to be waiting when he comes back, in summer or in snow. It is sung on St Patrick's Day and at funerals.[^dillane][^odowd][^ap][^golway] In his memoir of 1926, as Aileen Dillane of the University of Limerick quotes it, Weatherly wrote that it was sung "all over the world by Sinn Féiners and Ulstermen alike".[^dillane][^ballad][^odowd]

## Where it comes from

**The tune.** In 1855 the antiquarian George Petrie published The Petrie Collection of the Ancient Music of Ireland in Dublin. On page 57 is an air headed "Name unknown".[^petrie][^minerva] Petrie thanked Miss J. Ross of New Town, Limavady, in the County of Londonderry, who had made a large collection of the county's unpublished tunes and sent him this one. "The name of the tune unfortunately was not ascertained by Miss Ross," he wrote. She called it "very old", and he said he agreed.[^petrie][^minerva] Because it came from County Londonderry, it came to be called the Londonderry Air; hymnals also call it the Air from County Derry.[^ballad][^hymnary][^methodist]

![[1]]

**The words.** Weatherly wrote the words for well over a thousand songs.[^mann][^ballad] In words of his that Aileen Dillane quotes, he recalled: "It so happened that I had written in March of 1910 a song called Danny Boy with a different melody in mind."[^dillane] That first setting came to nothing.[^ballad] Then the air reached him from America. His great-grandson's account says his sister-in-law in America sent it in 1912, and an older songbook account also names a sister-in-law; one recent account says his sister, and puts his rewriting in 1911.[^mann][^ballad][^dillane] By lucky chance, he said in the same account, the words needed only "a few alterations" to fit.[^dillane]

**The pairing.** Boosey & Co. published Danny Boy in 1913, in London and New York, for voice and piano, adapted from an old Irish air.[^imslp][^conncoll] It went with soldiers into the First World War, and a modern collection of the war's soldiers' songs includes it.[^dillane][^ballad] In America it became an Irish American song above all, tied to St Patrick's Day, and one Irish American editor called it the "Irish American anthem".[^dillane][^odowd]

![[2]]

**Myth and record.** Often told: a blind fiddler, Jimmy McCurry, played the tune in Limavady on a market day in 1851 while Jane Ross wrote it down; or the tune goes back to the harper Rory Dall O'Cahan.[^niarchive][^rte1980][^odowd][^ballad] What the record shows: Petrie names no player and gives no date for when it was noted.[^petrie] A study of 2000 notes that the date 1851 may come from Ross's own manuscripts.[^audley] The McCurry story is a Limavady tradition, and the tellings disagree even on the instrument: a fiddler in one, a piper in another.[^niarchive][^methodist] The Ballad Index finds the O'Cahan "evidence" unexplained, and notes that some doubt Rory Dall existed.[^ballad] The tune's age has been argued too. Anne Gilchrist in 1934, and later Hugh Shields, suspected that its collector shaped it. The same study, in the Journal of the Royal Musical Association, argued that it really is old and was shaped by tradition.[^audley][^ballad][^dillane]

### Timeline

- 1855: George Petrie prints the air in Dublin as "Name unknown", sent by Miss J. Ross of Limavady. [^petrie][^minerva]
- 1882: Songs of Old Ireland, by Alfred Perceval Graves and Charles Villiers Stanford, includes Emer's Farewell, an earlier set of words to the air. [^lieder][^imslpsoi][^ballad][^howell]
- 1895: The second edition of Graves's Irish Song Book prints Katharine Tynan's words to the "Londonderry Air". [^isb]
- 1910: Weatherly writes Danny Boy, with a different melody in mind. [^dillane][^mann]
- 1913: Boosey & Co. publishes Danny Boy, Weatherly's words fitted to the air. [^imslp][^conncoll]
- 2000: A study in the Journal of the Royal Musical Association argues that the air is old and shaped by tradition. [^audley]

## What the words mean

Weatherly wrote two verses. Our arrangement sings the first.[^wikisource]

**Verse 1, first half (sung).** Danny, the pipes are calling, from one glen to the next and down the mountainside. A glen is a narrow valley; the pipes are bagpipes, summoning him away. Summer is over and the roses are dropping. It is Danny who must go, and the singer who must bide, which means stay behind and wait.[^wikisource]

![[3]]

**Verse 1, second half (sung).** Come back when summer is in the meadow, or when the valley lies quiet under snow. Whatever the weather, sunshine or shadow, the singer will be here. The verse ends on the plainest line in the song: Danny, I love you so.[^wikisource]

**Verse 2 (not sung).** The singer thinks ahead to dying before Danny comes home. If so, Danny will find the grave, kneel there and say an Ave, the Hail Mary prayer. The singer will hear him even through the ground, the grave will feel warmer for his love, and the singer will sleep in peace until Danny comes too.[^wikisource]

The song never says who is singing, or what the pipes are calling Danny to. It can be a mother, a father or a sweetheart. Some printings tell a man who sings it to use other words, printed in italics, and the song becomes Eily Dear.[^imslp][^cf] In the war years it fitted soldiers and the people waiting for them at home; Irish Americans heard their own emigrants' partings in it.[^dillane][^odowd]

## How it is sung

Danny Boy was published as a song for one voice and piano.[^imslp] It is sung at Irish American gatherings and on St Patrick's Day.[^odowd][^dillane] It is asked for at funerals, but it is not a hymn, and some Catholic dioceses in the United States have kept it out of the funeral Mass, which they reserve for liturgical music. In New York, mourners answered with religious words sung to the same tune.[^ap][^golway]

In hymnals the tune is LONDONDERRY AIR. Hymnary.org lists it as 11.10.11.10 D, but hymnals that keep the long last line print it as 11.10.11.10.11.10.11.12, as Danny Boy's own lines run. Churches sing it to several texts.[^hymnary][^methodist]

Our arrangement takes the tune as Petrie printed it in 1855 and fits Weatherly's first verse to it ourselves, rather than following Boosey's 1913 sheet note for note.[^petrie] It sings that verse once, slowly, in D major, for 2 to 4 singers. The soprano sings the tune. The alto and tenor sing the words with it, and the bass hums the low notes on "oo". With two singers it is soprano and alto; with three the bass joins; with four the tenor fills the middle.

## Versions and other words

**Other words to the air.** The Concise Oxford Dictionary of Music credits Alfred Perceval Graves with the first two sets of words for the tune.[^odm] One is Emer's Farewell to Cucullain, the second song in Songs of Old Ireland, which Graves made with Stanford in 1882.[^lieder][^imslpsoi][^ballad][^howell] Katharine Tynan's "Would God I were the tender apple blossom" is printed to the "Londonderry Air" in Graves's Irish Song Book, in its second edition of 1895.[^isb]

**A hymn.** William Young Fullerton's hymn "I cannot tell why He, whom angels worship" is sung to this tune.[^methodist][^hymnary]

**For a man.** Eily Dear is the same song with the name changed, printed in italics in some editions for a man to sing.[^imslp][^cf]

## Did you know?

- A Boosey printing of 1913 says the song "may be sung in public without fee or license", but forbids any parody of it being performed in public.[^conncoll]
- That same cover names the singers already performing it, among them Ernestine Schumann-Heink, who went on to record it.[^conncoll][^dillane][^mann]

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/danny-boy/soprano): the tune, slow and wide. The first half stays lower, dipping to a low A sharp on the first "It's you". The second half climbs: "come ye back" and "the valley's" reach D, and "I'll be here" peaks on the top F sharp. Take it gently.
- [Alto](/songs/danny-boy/alto): the words under the tune, often repeating one note: all of "the pipes, the pipes" sits on D, and "summer's in the meadow" on F sharp. Its lowest note, a low G, comes on "are calling" and again from the end of "falling" through "It's you".
- [Tenor](/songs/danny-boy/tenor): A for more than two thirds of the song. The moves away are the moments to learn: down to low D on "you, it's you must", on "hushed and white with", and on the last "oh Danny", and up once to C sharp on "when summer's".
- [Bass](/songs/danny-boy/bass): long notes on "oo", mostly D and A, coming in only on "boy". It drops to its low G under "the roses falling" and rises to F sharp under "when summer's"; solo it in the player to hear the bass line move there.

## Questions

### Is Danny Boy a funeral song?

It was not written as one, but it is often sung at funerals. Its second verse is about dying and being visited at the grave. Because it is a secular song, some Catholic dioceses in the United States have ruled it out of the funeral Mass itself. In New York, families answered by singing religious words to the same tune.[^wikisource][^ap][^golway]

### Is Danny Boy Irish or Scottish?

The tune is Irish: George Petrie printed it in 1855 from Jane Ross of Limavady, in County Londonderry. The words are English, by Frederic Weatherly, a barrister from England. Its pipes and glens can sound Scottish, but the tune was collected in Ireland and the words were written in England. It became an Irish song by use, and above all an Irish American one.[^petrie][^mann][^dillane][^odowd]

### Is Danny Boy Catholic or Protestant?

Neither: it is a secular song by an English writer. Its second verse asks for an Ave, the Catholic Hail Mary, to be said at the grave. Weatherly wrote in his memoir, as later writers quote it, that it was sung "by Sinn Féiners and Ulstermen alike", nationalists and unionists, and its tune comes from County Londonderry in Ulster.[^wikisource][^dillane][^petrie]

### Is Danny Boy in the public domain?

Yes, the words and the tune are. The tune was printed in 1855. Weatherly's words were published in 1913, and works published in the United States before 1931 are now in the public domain there. He died in 1929, and in the UK copyright lasts 70 years after the author's death. Later arrangements and recordings can have their own copyright, as a 1946 orchestral arrangement still does in the United States.[^petrie][^imslp][^cornell][^mann][^govuk]

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune throughout. The alto and tenor sing the words with it, the alto close under it and the tenor mostly on one note, A. The bass sings no words: it holds the low notes on "oo". With two singers it is soprano and alto; the bass joins third.
