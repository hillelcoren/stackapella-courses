In the Bleak Midwinter, also printed In the Bleak Mid-Winter, is a Christmas carol made from a poem by the English poet Christina Rossetti, who called it simply "A Christmas Carol" [^scribners1872][^pw1904]. Its earliest known printing is in Scribner's Monthly, a New York magazine, in January 1872 [^scribners1872][^canterbury]. In 1906 The English Hymnal set it to CRANHAM, a tune Gustav Holst composed for that book, and that is the tune congregations still sing [^eh1906][^massey1984]. The Episcopal Church's Hymnal 1982 and The United Methodist Hymnal both print it to CRANHAM, and so do Lutheran, Presbyterian and British Methodist books [^eh1982][^umh1989][^hymnary-text]. Its five short verses move from a frozen winter to a stable and end on a question: what can I give him?

## Where it comes from

**The poet.** Christina Georgina Rossetti was born in London on 5 December 1830, the youngest of four children of the Italian Gabriele Rossetti and his wife Frances, and sister of Dante Gabriel and William Michael Rossetti [^pw1904][^julian][^canterbury-rossetti][^cla1933]. She was a devout Anglican. William Michael, who wrote her memoir, placed her in the High Church party, and called her "an Anglo-Catholic" [^pw1904][^canterbury-rossetti].

**The poem.** Nobody knows when she wrote it. Editing her collected poems in 1904, ten years after her death, William Michael could date it only "Before 1872", the year of its earliest known printing, and recorded no occasion [^pw1904][^canterbury][^julian].

**Printed in New York, 1872.** In its December 1871 number, Scribner's Monthly, an illustrated New York magazine published by Scribner & Co., teased it in its "Old Cabinet" column: the writer had "a little poem" in a breast-pocket, "sweet and clear and musical", about the first Christmas morning "In the bleak mid-winter Long ago", and promised to send a copy "come the blessed Christmas-time" [^scribners1871][^ives2022]. The January 1872 number printed it on page 278 as "A Christmas Carol", by Christina G. Rossetti [^scribners1872][^canterbury]. The page sets the five verses, numbered I to V, under a half-page nativity engraving signed "Leighton" [^scribners1872][^ives2022]. Its banners carry Mary's words from Luke's Gospel, "He hath regarded the low estate of his handmaiden" [^scribners1872][^kjv]. The magazine prints capitals that The English Hymnal later dropped (Him, His Mother, Shepherd, Wise Man) and the American spelling "Worshiped" [^scribners1872][^eh1906].

**Back in Britain.** In 1875 the carol opened the "Devotional Pieces" in her one-volume Goblin Market, The Prince's Progress, and Other Poems (London: Macmillan) [^goblin1875][^pw1904]. In 1885 the editor A. H. Bullen took it into A Christmas Garland, an anthology of carols and poems from the fifteenth century to his own day [^garland1885]. Rossetti died in London on 29 December 1894 [^pw1904][^canterbury-rossetti]. Her brother's Poetical Works of 1904 notes that it was "first published in Scribner's Monthly, January 1872" [^pw1904][^julian].

**The tune, 1906.** The English Hymnal, published by Oxford University Press in 1906, printed the poem as No. 25 among its Christmas hymns, to a new tune headed "CRANHAM" and signed "G. von Holst" [^eh1906][^julian]. Holst was born in Cheltenham, Gloucestershire, in 1874 [^canterbury-holst][^hymnary-cranham]. The preface thanks "Mr. G. von Holst" for working throughout with the music editor, Ralph Vaughan Williams, on choosing, arranging and correcting the music, and lists him among the composers who wrote tunes especially for the book [^eh1906][^massey1984][^crim]. Because Rossetti's lines are of uneven length, the page prints the music for the first verse, a note that singers can fit it to the others, and an extra line showing how verses 2 and 3 go [^eh1906]. The tune takes its name from Cranham, a village in Gloucestershire that Holst knew when young [^crim][^bbcmm].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Holst wrote the tune in a cottage in Cranham, which has since been renamed Midwinter Cottage [^geograph][^flutetunes]. What the record shows: the 1906 hymnal names the tune and its composer and says he wrote it for the book, but gives no place or date [^eh1906]. We have found no letter or diary of Holst's placing it there, so the story is best taken as local tradition.

## What the words mean

The words below follow The English Hymnal of 1906, all five verses. They have barely changed since 1872, beyond the magazine's capitals and the 1875 book's "Throng'd" [^eh1906][^scribners1872][^goblin1875].

**Verse 1** paints a frozen winter: a moaning wind, ground "hard as iron", water frozen "like a stone", and snow falling on snow. "Bleak" means bare and cold, and "mid-winter" its depth. The Gospels give no season or weather for the birth; the winter is Rossetti's, an English one [^hymnary-text][^kjv].

**Verse 2** sets the size of God against the size of the room. "Heaven cannot hold him" echoes Solomon's prayer that "the heaven and heaven of heavens cannot contain thee" (1 Kings 8:27). "Heaven and earth shall flee away when he comes to reign" looks to the end of time, from Revelation 20:11 [^kjv]. Yet a stable "sufficed": it was enough for Almighty God.

**Verse 3** keeps that thought. Cherubim are a high order of angels, and Rossetti has them worship "night and day", like the creatures around God's throne in Revelation 4:8 who "rest not day and night". They are matched with all the child needs: "a breastful of milk", his mother's milk, and "a mangerful of hay", a feeding trough filled for his bed (Luke 2:7) [^kjv]. The ox and ass are not in the Gospels, though Isaiah 1:3 says "The ox knoweth his owner, and the ass his master's crib". Matthew's wise men have no camels either, but Isaiah 60:6 pictures camels bringing gold and incense [^kjv]. "Which adore" means "who adore", the old use of "which" for people, as in "Our Father which art in heaven" [^kjv].

**Verse 4** says the angels "may have" crowded the air, but only his mother, "in her maiden bliss", the joy of the virgin mother, worshipped the child, and with a kiss. "Belovèd" is sung in three syllables, as the accent shows. In the poem the worship goes to the child, not to Mary; her brother wrote that she was "firmly opposed to anything savouring of Mariolatry", the worship of Mary [^pw1904].

**Verse 5** turns to the singer. A shepherd could bring a lamb, and a wise man could "do my part", as the wise men brought gold, frankincense and myrrh (Matthew 2:11) [^kjv]. Someone poor has nothing like that, and gives the one thing left: "Give my heart."

### The full words

Christina Rossetti's poem as printed in The English Hymnal (1906), No. 25, with the book's two dashes given as commas and its misprint "Pcor" corrected to "Poor".

In the bleak mid-winter
Frosty wind made moan,
Earth stood hard as iron,
Water like a stone;
Snow had fallen, snow on snow,
Snow on snow,
In the bleak mid-winter,
Long ago.

Our God, heaven cannot hold him
Nor earth sustain;
Heaven and earth shall flee away
When he comes to reign:
In the bleak mid-winter
A stable-place sufficed
The Lord God Almighty
Jesus Christ.

Enough for him, whom Cherubim
Worship night and day,
A breastful of milk,
And a mangerful of hay;
Enough for him, whom Angels
Fall down before,
The ox and ass and camel
Which adore.

Angels and Archangels
May have gathered there,
Cherubim and Seraphim
Thronged the air,
But only his mother
In her maiden bliss
Worshipped the Belovèd
With a kiss.

What can I give him
Poor as I am?
If I were a shepherd
I would bring a lamb;
If I were a wise man
I would do my part;
Yet what I can I give him,
Give my heart.

## How it is sung

It is a Christmas carol and a Christmas hymn at once. The English Hymnal placed it among the hymns for Christmas, and by 1928 The Oxford Book of Carols took it in too, its editors noting that, sung to its English Hymnal tune, it had become a carol in all but name [^eh1906][^obc1928]. A hymnologist writing in 1984 called CRANHAM its "inevitable" congregational setting [^hymnary-text][^massey1984].

The English Hymnal and Hymnary.org's text page call its meter irregular, since Rossetti's lines change length from verse to verse [^eh1906][^hymnary-text]; Hymnary.org's tune page lists CRANHAM as 6.5.6.5 D [^hymnary-cranham].

Which verses a church sings depends on its book. The Episcopal Church's Hymnal 1982 (No. 112) and The United Methodist Hymnal (No. 221) print four, leaving out the third, with its "breastful of milk" [^eh1982][^umh1989]. Some books print the last verse alone, as "What Can I Give Him?" [^hymnary-text].

Our arrangement is read from the first edition of The English Hymnal (1906), No. 25, headed "CRANHAM. (Irreg.)": the tune Holst composed and harmonised for the book, in F and in 4/4 time as printed [^eh1906]. The soprano sings Holst's tune, and his alto, tenor and bass are adapted for 2 to 4 beginners. We sing three of Rossetti's five verses, her verses 1, 2 and 5, keeping her numbers, so the third is labelled Verse 5. We leave out her verse 3, the one many hymnals drop, and her verse 4, the angels and his mother's kiss [^eh1906][^hymnary-text].

## Versions and other words

In 1911 Stainer and Bell published an anthem on the same words by the English organist Harold Darke (1888 to 1976) [^musicanet][^bbcmm][^crim-darke]. A few hymnals set the words to other tunes, but CRANHAM is by far the most common [^hymnary-text].

## Did you know?

- Holst wrote three tunes especially for The English Hymnal, Nos. 25, 310 and 571; CRANHAM, No. 25, is the one still widely sung [^eh1906][^massey1984].
- The Oxford Book of Carols (1928) printed it as No. 187 under the title "Mid-Winter" [^obc1928].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/in-the-bleak-midwinter/soprano): Holst's tune, most often on G, and every line ends on a rest for a breath. Its top D comes only in each verse's fifth line ("snow on", "win-", "wise"), and its low D only on the fourth note of each second line ("made", "sus-", "I").
- [Alto](/songs/in-the-bleak-midwinter/alto): under the tune all the way, most often on D, up to its top F. It drops to its low A only for the last word of each verse's fourth and last lines ("stone;" and "go." in verse 1), where the tenor sings the same A: match it.
- [Tenor](/songs/in-the-bleak-midwinter/tenor): the middle of the chord, most often on A, then B-flat, never above the alto or below the bass. It starts each verse's third and seventh lines on the alto's C ("Earth", "In"), and its top D comes once, on "A" in "A stable-place".
- [Bass](/songs/in-the-bleak-midwinter/bass): the bass line, most often on F, then D. Its top A comes three times a verse, under the tune's C ("bleak", "hard", "bleak" in verse 1), and its low F only on the song's last word, "heart.".

## Questions

### Who wrote the music for In the Bleak Midwinter?

Gustav Holst (1874 to 1934) wrote the hymn tune CRANHAM for The English Hymnal of 1906, the tune hymnals print and congregations sing [^eh1906][^massey1984][^canterbury-holst]. Composers have also set the poem as choir anthems; the best known was published in 1911 [^musicanet][^bbcmm]. The words are Christina Rossetti's [^scribners1872][^canterbury].

### Why was In the Bleak Midwinter written?

The record doesn't say. Her brother could date it only "Before 1872", the year it was printed, and gave no occasion [^pw1904][^canterbury]. It was published for Christmas: Scribner's Monthly promised it in December 1871 and printed it in its January 1872 number as "A Christmas Carol" [^scribners1871][^scribners1872].

### What does In the Bleak Midwinter mean in the Bible?

The phrase is not from the Bible, which gives no season for the birth; the snow is Rossetti's [^kjv][^hymnary-text]. Much else in the poem is scripture: heaven that "cannot hold him" echoes 1 Kings 8:27, heaven and earth fleeing away comes from Revelation 20:11, the manger from Luke 2:7, the ox and ass recall Isaiah 1:3, and the wise men's gifts from Matthew 2:11 [^kjv].

### Is In the Bleak Midwinter public domain?

Yes, the words and Holst's tune. Rossetti died in 1894 and Holst in 1934, more than 70 years ago, and both were printed before 1931 [^pw1904][^canterbury-holst][^eh1906]. Hymnary.org marks the text and CRANHAM as public domain [^hymnary-text][^hymnary-cranham]. The 1911 anthem is different: its composer died in 1976, so where copyright lasts 70 years after a composer's death, as in Britain and the European Union, it is protected until the end of 2046 [^musicanet][^crim-darke].
