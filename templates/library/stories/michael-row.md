Michael, Row the Boat Ashore is a spiritual, a song made by enslaved African Americans. Its home is the Sea Islands of South Carolina, where Black oarsmen sang it as they rowed. During the Civil War, Charles Pickard Ware wrote it down in the Port Royal islands, probably at Coffin's Point on St. Helena Island, and its earliest known printing is No. 31 in Slave Songs of the United States (1867) [^scan31][^slavesongs][^place2019]. Its first line calls on Michael to row the boat ashore, and every line is answered the same way: "Hallelujah!" [^scan31]. In the 1950s the folk revival took it up, and in 1961 a recording of it topped the charts in the United States and Britain [^place2019][^stereogum][^officialcharts][^number1s].

## Where it comes from

**The islands.** In November 1861 Union naval forces took Port Royal, on the South Carolina coast. The planters of the Sea Islands fled inland, and most of the people they had enslaved stayed behind on the land they had worked [^arroyo][^coastal]. These islands are home to the Gullah Geechee people, whose speech and traditions grew there [^place2019][^coastal]. In 1862 Northern teachers and missionaries began to arrive, to open schools for the freedpeople and to run the cotton plantations with their paid labor. This became known as the Port Royal Experiment [^coastal][^arroyo][^nps]. The sources differ on the dates. Slave Songs dates "the educational mission to the Port Royal islands" to 1861, the year the islands fell; the National Park Service has the first teachers reaching Beaufort in mid-March 1862, and Coastal Heritage has the first missionary group arriving in April [^slavesongs][^nps][^coastal].

**Who wrote it down.** Charles Pickard Ware, a young Northerner, worked at Coffin's Point, a plantation on St. Helena Island, and wrote down the songs he heard there [^slavesongs][^pearson1906]. William Francis Allen's introduction called it "probably" the largest and most accurate single collection of these songs, made "chiefly at Coffin's Point". The editors printed it whole as the basis of their book [^slavesongs].

![[1]]

Ware also left a note on how the rowers used the songs. "As I have written these tunes," he wrote, "two measures are to be sung to each stroke", the first marked by the beginning of the stroke and the second by the rattle of the oars in the rowlocks. He listed "Michael row" among the songs "used when the load was heavy or the tide was against us" [^slavesongs].

**The book.** Slave Songs was published in New York by A. Simpson & Co. in 1867. Its editors were Allen (1830 to 1889), Ware and Lucy McKim Garrison, three white Northerners; Allen and Ware had come south with the mission, so the songs reached print as outsiders heard them [^slavesongs][^uscpress][^pearson1906][^dbcs]. Allen worked on St. Helena from late 1863 to mid-1864 [^uscpress][^slavesongs]. In his introduction he wrote that the islanders used the same songs for rowing as for the shout, the dance of worship held in the praise house after a meeting. He knew "only one pure boat-song", this one, and had "no doubt" it was "a real spiritual", because it is the archangel Michael who is addressed [^slavesongs].

![[2]]

**The page.** No. 31 stands on page 23 of the first edition, headed "MICHAEL ROW THE BOAT ASHORE." with no composer and no collector's name [^scan31]. The book's table of contents credits it "Port Royal Islands. C. P. W.", Ware's initials [^slavesongs][^pearson1906]. Nobody knows who made it, and the words and the tune share this earliest known printing. The tune is in E-flat, in 2/4 time, on two short lines of music. Under the first is "1. Michael row de boat a-shore, Hal-le-lu-jah!"; the second, "2. Mich-ael boat a gos-pel boat, Hal-le-lu-jah!", is printed in two parts, with two notes on most beats [^scan31]. Lines 3 to 22 follow as words only, onto page 24; the only note is the gloss "(there)" after "deh" in line 3. Then come seven "Words from Hilton Head", beginning "Michael haul the boat ashore" [^scan31]. The editors set one set of words to each tune and left singers to fit the rest, as the islanders did [^slavesongs].

![[3]]

The song sits inside Ware's collection, Nos. 1 to 43, and in the middle of a run, Nos. 27 to 33, that the introduction counts among "the most common rowing tunes" [^slavesongs][^scan31].

**The revival.** In the mid-1950s a Boston folk singer found the song in the 1867 book. By his own account he put rhyming couplets from other African American songs in place of the old lines, for the teenagers at the summer work camp where he led the singing, and taught it to Pete Seeger [^kopp][^place2019]. So many verses sung today are not the 1867 ones: lines such as "Michael boat a gospel boat" and "Sister, help for trim dat boat" are the older words [^kopp][^scan31]. Seeger recorded it in 1956 on the Folkways album With Voices Together We Sing [^fa2452][^place2019]. In 1961 a recording by a folk group of Wesleyan University students, shortened to "Michael", reached number one in the United States in September, and in Britain too [^stereogum][^wesleyan][^officialcharts][^number1s].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Michael is the ferryman who carries the souls of the dead across the river. The Smithsonian's notes to Seeger's recording call him the archangel "who ferries the souls of the dead" [^place2019]. What the record shows: no verse of the Bible puts Michael in a boat, and the 1867 book says only that it is the archangel who is addressed [^kjv][^slavesongs]. Catholic tradition gives him the office of bringing souls to judgment, but no boat [^catholicenc]. The ferryman joins that office to the rowers' own boat: it is a reading of the song, not something the song or the Bible says.

## What the words mean

The book spells the words as its editors heard the Sea Island speech. "De" is "the", "deh" is "there" (the book glosses it itself), "gwine" is "going", "mudder" and "fader" are "mother" and "father", "brudder" is "brother", "riber" is "river", "neber" is "never", "t' oder" is "the other", and "maussa" is "master" [^scan31].

**Lines 1 and 2, the ones with music.** The singer calls on Michael to row the boat to shore. In the Bible he is an archangel and "the great prince" who stands for his people [^kjv][^catholicenc]. His boat is "a gospel boat": a boat of the gospel, the good news. Each line ends in "Hallelujah", a Hebrew word that means "praise the Lord"; the King James Bible renders it "Praise ye the LORD" (Psalm 146:1) [^zondervan][^jewishenc][^kjv].

**Lines 3 to 7.** The singer wonders where their mother is, then sees her "on de rock gwine home", going home in Jesus' name. Michael's boat becomes "a music boat", and the angel Gabriel blows the trumpet [^scan31].

**Lines 8 to 11.** A warning: mind your boasting talk, because boasting will sink your soul. Then the crew is called in: a brother to lend a hand and a sister to help trim the boat, setting its load so it rides level [^scan31].

**Lines 12 to 15.** "Jordan stream is wide and deep", and Jesus stands on the other side. The Jordan is the river the Israelites crossed into the promised land [^kjv]. The singer wonders whether the master, the enslaver, will be there too, and says their father has gone to an unknown land [^scan31].

**Lines 16 to 22.** The Lord has planted his garden there and raised fruit to eat, and whoever eats it shall never die. Then a darker turn: the river overflows, darkness is coming, and the last line tells the sinner to row to save their soul [^scan31].

**The Hilton Head words.** Michael hauls the boat ashore, and the trumpet sounds the world around, for rich and poor and for the jubilee, a time of release [^scan31][^kjv].

### The full words

These are all the words of No. 31 in *Slave Songs of the United States*, pages 23 and 24, with the book's spelling and its own gloss "(there)" [^scan31]. Lines 1 and 2 are printed under the music with their "Hallelujah!"; lines 3 to 22 are numbered below it, grouped here as in the notes above, and seven more lines follow under the book's heading "Words from Hilton Head". The book was printed in 1867, so all of it is in the public domain.

**Lines 1 and 2**
Michael row de boat ashore, Hallelujah!
Michael boat a gospel boat, Hallelujah!

**Lines 3 to 7**
I wonder where my mudder deh (there).
See my mudder on de rock gwine home.
On de rock gwine home in Jesus' name.
Michael boat a music boat.
Gabriel blow de trumpet horn.

**Lines 8 to 11**
O you mind your boastin' talk.
Boastin' talk will sink your soul.
Brudder, lend a helpin' hand.
Sister, help for trim dat boat.

**Lines 12 to 15**
Jordan stream is wide and deep.
Jesus stand on t' oder side.
I wonder if my maussa deh.
My fader gone to unknown land.

**Lines 16 to 22**
O de Lord he plant his garden deh.
He raise de fruit for you to eat.
He dat eat shall neber die.
When de riber overflow.
O poor sinner, how you land?
Riber run and darkness comin'.
Sinner row to save your soul.

**Words from Hilton Head**
Michael haul the boat ashore.
Then you'll hear the horn they blow.
Then you'll hear the trumpet sound.
Trumpet sound the world around.
Trumpet sound for rich and poor.
Trumpet sound the jubilee.
Trumpet sound for you and me.

## How it is sung

Most island songs served both for rowing and for the shout. This one, Allen wrote, was the exception: a pure boat-song, sung at the oars [^slavesongs].

It is a call and an answer. The book describes how the islanders sang: a leader starts the words of each verse, often making them up, and the others "base" him, coming in on the refrain. The basers come and go as they please, sometimes an octave above or below or on another note of the chord, "and yet with the most perfect time" [^slavesongs]. In this song the answer is "Hallelujah!" Allen added that there was "no singing in parts as we understand it" [^slavesongs].

This is a folk spiritual, made and sung at the oars and written down by visitors. The concert spiritual came later. From October 1871 the Fisk Jubilee Singers of Fisk University in Nashville toured, and their concert spirituals made the songs known to wide audiences [^amheritage][^tnenc].

Our arrangement is read from Slave Songs of the United States (1867), No. 31, for 2 to 4 singers. It sings the two lines printed with music, "de" sung as "the"; where line 2 is printed in two parts, our tune takes the lower. It is in F, a tone higher than the book's E-flat [^scan31]. The soprano sings the tune, and the alto, tenor and bass sing with it. The harmony is ours: the islanders' way was a leader and a crowd answering, as the book describes [^slavesongs].

## Did you know?

- Ware noted that on the passenger boat at the Beaufort ferry they rowed from sixteen to thirty strokes a minute, twenty-four on average [^slavesongs].
- In 1889 The Scottish Art Review reprinted the first two lines with their music, in B-flat rather than E-flat, in an article on songs of enslaved people [^sar1889].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/michael-row/soprano): you carry the tune, the call to Michael. It is two short lines, each answered by a Hallelujah, so learn the words and the tune follows.
- [Alto](/songs/michael-row/alto): you sing the words close by the tune, mostly on one note, A, and finish just above the soprano. Let the Hallelujahs ring: they are the answer.
- [Tenor](/songs/michael-row/tenor): you fill the middle of the chord, singing the words with the others.
- [Bass](/songs/michael-row/bass): you sing the lowest line on "oo", mostly on F, the floor of the chord. Keep it steady, like a stroke of the oars.

## Questions

### Who wrote Michael, Row the Boat Ashore?

No one knows who made it. It is a spiritual of enslaved African Americans on the South Carolina Sea Islands, sung by oarsmen in the Port Royal islands during the Civil War [^slavesongs][^place2019]. It comes from the collection Charles Pickard Ware made there, chiefly at Coffin's Point, and its earliest known printing is No. 31 in Slave Songs of the United States (1867), which Ware edited with William Francis Allen and Lucy McKim Garrison [^slavesongs][^pearson1906]. They collected the song; they did not write it.

### What does Michael, Row the Boat Ashore mean?

It is a prayer sung at the oars. The singer calls on the archangel Michael to row the boat ashore, and his boat becomes "a gospel boat"; the singer sees a mother "gwine home" and looks over the Jordan, the river into the promised land, to Jesus on the other side [^scan31][^kjv]. Each line answers "Hallelujah", "praise the Lord" [^zondervan]. Michael is in the Bible, "the great prince" in Daniel and "Michael the archangel" in Jude, but never in a boat [^kjv].

### Is it a spiritual or a hymn?

It is a spiritual, a religious folk song of enslaved African Americans, and a work song: Sea Island oarsmen sang it to keep the stroke, not in church from a hymn book. William Francis Allen, who printed it in 1867, called it the one "pure boat-song" he knew and had "no doubt" it was "a real spiritual", because it calls on the archangel [^slavesongs]. Choirs' concert spirituals came later, from the Fisk Jubilee Singers' tours of the 1870s [^amheritage][^tnenc].

### Who made Michael, Row the Boat Ashore a hit?

First the Sea Island oarsmen, who sang it at the oars in the 1860s [^slavesongs]. In the 1950s a Boston folk singer reworked its verses and taught it to Pete Seeger, who recorded it for Folkways in 1956 [^kopp][^place2019][^fa2452]. The hit came in 1961: a recording by a folk group of Wesleyan University students, shortened to "Michael", reached number one in the United States in September and in Britain in October [^stereogum][^wesleyan][^officialcharts][^number1s].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, the call to Michael; the alto, tenor and bass sing with it. On the islands there was no singing in parts as the editor William Francis Allen understood it: a leader sang, and the others answered with the Hallelujah [^slavesongs].
