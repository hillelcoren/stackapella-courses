All Through the Night, in Welsh Ar Hyd y Nos, is a Welsh air sung as a lullaby and as an evening hymn. Its earliest known printing is in Edward Jones's Musical and Poetical Relicks of the Welsh Bards, published in London in 1784, where it is a love song for voice and harp [^jones1784][^dnb-jones][^balladindex]. The English words we sing, "Sleep my love and peace attend thee", are Harold Boulton's; the earliest printing of them we have found is his Songs of the Four Nations of 1892 [^sf4n][^ssu1894]. The poet Ceiriog wrote Welsh words for it [^sow][^ceiriog1902]. A Dublin schoolbook named the air for Reginald Heber's evening hymn by 1838, and the English Hymnal of 1906 printed the two together, as the hymn tune AR HYD Y NOS [^sacred1838][^eh1906][^hymnarchive].

## Where it comes from

**The earliest printing.** Edward Jones (1752 to 1824) was a harper born at Henblas, in Llandderfel parish, Merioneth, north Wales [^dnb-jones][^dwb-jones]. In 1784 he published his Musical and Poetical Relicks of the Welsh Bards, airs "preserved by tradition, and authentic manuscripts, from remote antiquity" [^jones1784][^dnb-jones][^dwb-jones]. On page 56, headed "Ar hŷd y nos", the air is set for voice and harp in B-flat, a minor third above our key, and marked Maestoso, majestic; its opening bars are the ones we sing [^jones1784]. Under the notes run one verse in Welsh and one in English. Five harp variations follow, running on to page 57 and the next air, Ffarwel Ednyfed Fychan [^jones1784][^hymnarchive].

![[1]]

**A pastoral air.** Jones sorts the old Welsh melodies into four kinds: solemn, martial, plaintive, and a pastoral kind, "of all perhaps the most agreeable", with "a pleasing simplicity and soothing tranquillity, suitable to genial love". His footnote lists "Ar hŷd y nos" among them [^jones1784]. His Welsh verse fits: a lover who will not believe that love is lost [^jones1784][^richards1861].

**The Welsh words.** Ceiriog was born in 1832 at Pen-y-bryn, Llanarmon Dyffryn Ceiriog, worked for the railway in Manchester, became station-master at Llanidloes in 1865, and died in 1887 [^dwb-ceiriog][^ceiriog1902]. His aim, the Dictionary of Welsh Biography says, was to write words for the old Welsh melodies [^dwb-ceiriog]. Brinley Richards's Songs of Wales (1873) names Ceiriog Hughes on its title page for the Welsh words [^imslp-sow][^sow]. Its "All through the night" sets his Welsh, "Holl amrantau'r sêr ddywedant", under English words by Walter Maynard, the pen name of the writer Thomas Willert Beale (1828 to 1894) [^sow][^dnb-beale][^imslp-maynard]. A footnote adds that the air is "Known as Poor Mary Ann" [^sow].

**Boulton's English.** Harold Boulton (1859 to 1935) was an English songwriter and philanthropist; he also wrote the words of the Skye Boat Song [^loc-boulton][^henson][^simpson1910]. In 1892 he edited Songs of the Four Nations, fifty old songs of England, Scotland, Ireland and Wales, arranged by Arthur Somervell; where a tune's words were lost or did not fit, its preface says, new words were made "upon older models" [^sf4n]. Song No. 26 is "All Through the Night (Ar hyd y nôs)", with "English Words by Harold Boulton", a "Welsh simile", a Welsh version, by G. M. Probert, and "Old Welsh Air arranged by Arthur Somervell", in A-flat and marked Andante [^sf4n]. Our copy is the second edition, dated 1892. The first, a subscribers' edition, is listed among Cramer's books of December 1892; that September the Athenaeum had reported the book as "shortly to be published" [^englishcat1892][^athenaeum1892]. In 1894 he reprinted the poem in his Songs Sung and Unsung, under "From Songs of the Four Nations" [^ssu1894].

**Myth and record.** Often told: the air is an ancient song of the Welsh bards, as Jones's title page suggests. What the record shows: no copy older than Jones's 1784 book is known, and the air has no known maker [^balladindex][^hymnary-tune][^jones1784].

## What the words mean

Harold Boulton's three verses, as Songs of the Four Nations prints them, are not a translation: he wrote new English words for the old air, and the Welsh beside them is G. M. Probert's version of his English, named here but not given, as we have not found when Probert died. The poem moves from a lullaby to a lament, and ends with a death [^sf4n][^balladindex].

**Verse 1.** Sleep, my love, and may peace stay with you all night. God will lend you guardian angels to watch over you. The sleepy hours creep softly by, and hills and valleys are steeped, soaked through, in sleep [^sf4n][^webster]. Only love stays awake to keep watch, all through the night [^sf4n].

**Verse 2.** The singer is a minstrel, a wandering singer and harper, travelling alone [^webster]. The harp will praise no one else. Young love's dream is over, but the singer's strains, the songs, will still hover near the one the singer loves [^sf4n][^webster].

![[2]]

**Verse 3.** A bell rings, solemn and clear, through the night. The one the singer loves is flying up to heaven, going "home through the night". The dust of earth shaken off, the undying soul will wake, its "last dim journey" made [^sf4n].

### The Welsh words

Two Welsh texts follow, each line with our own plain English under it, made with William Richards's Welsh-English dictionary of 1861 and John Morris-Jones's Welsh Grammar [^richards1861][^morrisjones].

The first is the verse Jones printed in 1784, with the spelling as printed and the syllables joined [^jones1784].

The second is Ceiriog's "Ar hyd y Nos", as printed in O. M. Edwards's selection of his poems, Ceiriog (1902), with its spelling: "ser", stars, which Songs of Wales prints "sêr" [^ceiriog1902][^sow]. It is not a lullaby: the stars tell the way to heaven, and the poem asks us to give our faint light to one another.

**Jones, 1784**
> Er bod rhai yn taeru'n galed,
Though some swear hard
> Ar hŷd y nos.
all through the night
> Ddarfod i mi golli 'nghariad;
that I have lost my love,
> Ar hŷd y nos.
all through the night,
> Minnau fydd heb fedru coelio,
I shall not be able to believe
> I mi golli 'nghariad etto,
that I have lost my love yet,
> I mi golli 'nghariad etto.
that I have lost my love yet.
> Ar hŷd y nos.
All through the night.

**Ceiriog, verse 1**
> Holl amrantau 'r ser ddywedant,
All the eyelids of the stars are saying,
> Ar hyd y nos,
all through the night,
> Dyma 'r ffordd i fro gogoniant,
"This is the way to the land of glory",
> Ar hyd y nos.
all through the night.
> Goleu arall yw tywyllwch,
Darkness is another kind of light,
> I arddangos gwir brydferthwch
to show the true beauty
> Teulu 'r nefoedd mewn tawelwch,
of heaven's family in the stillness,
> Ar hyd y nos.
all through the night.

**Ceiriog, verse 2**
> O mor siriol gwena seren
Oh, how cheerfully a star smiles
> Ar hyd y nos;
all through the night,
> I oleuo 'i chwaer-ddaearen
to light her sister, the earth,
> Ar hyd y nos.
all through the night.
> Nos yw henaint pan ddaw cystudd,
Old age is night when trouble comes,
> Ond i harddu dyn a'i hwyrddydd,
but to make a person's evening beautiful,
> Rhown ein goleu gwan i'n gilydd
let us give our faint light to one another
> Ar hyd y nos.
all through the night.

### The full words

Harold Boulton's three verses as printed on their own page, page 138, of Songs of the Four Nations (second edition, 1892), with spelling and punctuation as printed; the book sets the refrain lines in italics. The dash after "keeping" is left out. We sing the first verse.

**Verse 1**
Sleep my love and peace attend thee,
All through the night;
Guardian angels God will lend thee,
All through the night;
Soft the drowsy hours are creeping,
Hill and dale in slumber steeping,
Love alone his watch is keeping
All through the night.

**Verse 2**
Though I roam a minstrel lonely,
All through the night;
My true harp shall praise thee only,
All through the night;
Love's young dream, alas, is over,
Yet my strains of love shall hover
Near the presence of my lover,
All through the night.

**Verse 3**
Hark! A solemn bell is ringing,
Clear through the night;
Thou my love art heavenward winging,
Home through the night;
Earthly dust from off thee shaken,
Soul immortal thou shalt waken,
With thy last dim journey taken
Home through the night.

## How it is sung

The tune has four phrases: the first, second and fourth share one melody, and the third climbs higher before the tune comes home. In the words, each of the first two long lines is answered by the short refrain; then three long lines run on, two in the third phrase and one in the fourth, and the refrain closes the verse. So hymnals give its meter as 8.4.8.4.8.8.8.4 [^eh1906][^hymnary-tune]. The English Hymnal prints it in G, "In moderate time", among its evening hymns [^eh1906]. A vocal quartet recorded Boulton's words for Columbia in 1915; the Library of Congress has made the disc free to hear [^jukebox1915].

Our arrangement takes the melody and words from Songs of the Four Nations, moved from the book's A-flat down to G. We sing the first verse for 2 to 4 singers, softly, like a hymn. Every part sings the words together, note for note: two singers are soprano and alto, three add the bass, and four the tenor.

## Versions and other words

- **Jones's English, 1784.** The earliest known English words are Jones's own verse, sung by someone who will not believe her "faithful swain" has left her: "But my beating heart will falter, Ere it thinks his heart can alter" [^jones1784].
- **Maynard's English.** Maynard's words in Songs of Wales begin "Love, fear not if sad thy dreaming" [^sow]. Later records and song books kept his credit: a Victor record of 1932 reads "English words by Walter Maynard", and the Ballad Index finds it in the National 4-H Club Song Book [^victor1932][^balladindex].
- **"Sleep, my child".** Later versions turn Boulton's lover into a child: the Ballad Index and Hymnary.org both list the song as "Sleep, my child, and peace attend thee" [^balladindex][^hymnary-text].
- **Heber's evening hymn.** D. R. Thomas, vicar of Meifod, wrote in 1881, from what the host's son told him, that Reginald Heber, a guest of C. W. Williams-Wynn at Llangedwyn with the poet Robert Southey, heard the air on the harp and, with the tune still in his ears, wrote "God, that madest earth and heaven" to it [^thomas1881][^hymnarchive]. The Hymnology Archive notes a reply in Bye-Gones that year that disputed some details [^hymnarchive]. The hymn was printed in 1827 [^eh1906][^hymnarchive]. Its second verse, "Guard us waking, guard us sleeping", is credited to Archbishop Richard Whately. The English Hymnal dates it 1855, but it was already in print, unsigned, in Sacred Poetry, a Dublin schoolbook: its 1838 printing gives both verses as an "Evening Hymn" to the air "Ar hydd y nos", and the Hymnology Archive shows an edition of 1835 [^eh1906][^sacred1838][^hymnarchive]. The English Hymnal of 1906 printed the hymn with the tune itself, as No. 268 [^eh1906][^hymnarchive].

## Did you know?

- Jones's 1784 page writes "Chorus" over the refrain, "Ar hŷd y nos", and the refrain stays in Welsh even under his English verse [^jones1784].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/all-through-the-night/soprano): you carry the tune, moving mostly by step. The opening line's tune comes three times, on "Sleep my love", "Guardian angels" and "Love alone"; the third phrase climbs: "Soft the drowsy" starts high, on C, and your top note, the E, comes on "hours".
- [Alto](/songs/all-through-the-night/alto): you sing the words mostly in thirds and sixths under the tune, resting mostly on C and B, close and easy to hold. The third phrase moves more: you dip to your lowest note, A, on "creep-", then rise to your top G on "and" in "Hill and dale" and on "slum-".
- [Tenor](/songs/all-through-the-night/tenor): you sing in the middle of the chord, mostly on G, D and A, with no big jumps. Your top D comes on "God will", on "and dale" and on "slum-"; you begin on the low D, "Sleep my", and end there, on "through the night".
- [Bass](/songs/all-through-the-night/bass): you sing the bass line, low and steady, mostly on G, A and D, with C now and then. Each "All through the night" ends on your low G, and your top note, E, comes only twice, on "-gels" and on "in" of "in slumber".

## Questions

### What does "Ar Hyd y Nos" mean, and how do you say it?

It means "all through the night", word for word "along the night": ar hyd is "along" and nos is "night" [^morrisjones][^richards1861]. Hyd has a long Welsh y, a dull "ee" made further back in the mouth, sounded as "ee" or nearly so in south Wales; the little word y, "the", is the short "uh" sound, like the o in "ivory"; and nos has a long o [^morrisjones]. Older books print it nôs [^richards1861][^sf4n].

### Who wrote "All Through the Night"?

No one knows who made the tune, a traditional Welsh air whose earliest known printing is Edward Jones's book of 1784 [^jones1784][^balladindex][^hymnary-tune]. The English words we sing are Harold Boulton's, earliest found in his Songs of the Four Nations of 1892 [^sf4n][^ssu1894]. Welsh words by the poet Ceiriog, John Ceiriog Hughes, were printed with the air in Brinley Richards's Songs of Wales [^sow][^ceiriog1902].

### Is "All Through the Night" a lullaby, a hymn or a Christmas song?

It began as none of these: Jones printed it in 1784 as a love song [^jones1784]. Boulton's words open as a lullaby and end with the death of the one the singer loves [^sf4n][^balladindex]. As the hymn tune AR HYD Y NOS it carries Reginald Heber's evening hymn, paired with it by 1838 [^sacred1838][^eh1906][^hymnarchive]. It is not a Christmas song by origin, though the Ballad Index knows one Christmas carol version [^balladindex].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, from the D above middle C up to the E a ninth higher. The other parts sing the same words with it, note for note: the alto close beneath, mostly in thirds and sixths, the tenor in the middle of the chord and the bass below.
