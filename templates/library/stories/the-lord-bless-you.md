The Lord Bless You and Keep You is a short choral blessing by Peter C. Lutkin, an American organist and the first dean of music at Northwestern University, printed in Chicago in 1900 as a "Farewell Anthem with Sevenfold Amen" [^summy][^cdh][^nuhist][^numag]. Its words are the priestly blessing from the Book of Numbers, chapter 6, verses 24 to 26, in the King James Version, which Lutkin rearranged and put into "you" [^summy][^kjv1611][^cdh]. Churches sing it at the close of a service, after the minister's blessing or in its place, and hymnals print it under the tune name BENEDICTION [^price][^phh][^fenner][^hymnary]. We sing Lutkin's own four voice parts, blessing and Amen, for four singers.

## Where it comes from

**The blessing.** In Numbers 6, the Lord tells Moses to have Aaron and his sons, the priests, bless the children of Israel with three short lines [^kjv1611][^je]. In Hebrew they grow as they go, three words, then five, then seven, fifteen words in all [^masorah][^je]. The Mishnah, the early collection of Jewish law, says that outside the Temple the priests gave it as three blessings, the people answering amen after each, while in the Temple they gave it as one, spoke the divine name as it is written, and raised their hands above their heads [^mishnah][^je]. Two tiny rolled silver amulets found in a burial cave at Ketef Hinnom in Jerusalem, dated to around 600 BCE, carry a version of the same words; the Israel Museum, which keeps them, calls them the oldest known copies of biblical text [^imj][^cojs].

**In Christian worship.** Christians came to end services with it too. In his order for the Mass of 1523, Martin Luther let the pastor close with the customary blessing or with the one from Numbers 6, which, he wrote, the Lord himself had arranged [^luther][^phh]. The King James Version of 1611 gave English speakers the wording Lutkin set, in today's spelling "The LORD bless thee, and keep thee" [^kjv1611][^kjv].

![[1]]

**Peter Lutkin.** Lutkin was born in 1858 at Thompsonville, Wisconsin, and grew up in Chicago [^price][^baker][^phh]. A Methodist hymnal guide of 1911 says he sang as a boy in the choir of the Cathedral of Saints Peter and Paul there [^price], and it and a dictionary of 1919 both make him the cathedral's organist while still in his teens [^price][^baker]; a modern hymnal handbook places his boyhood training at St. James instead [^phh]. He went to Europe to study in 1881, at the royal music school in Berlin by the 1919 dictionary's account [^price][^phh][^baker]. Back in Chicago, he was organist at St. James Episcopal Church from 1891, until 1896 by two accounts and 1897 by the church's own list [^baker][^phh][^stjames]. In 1891 he also took charge of Northwestern University's conservatory of music, and when its School of Music was formed in 1895, by the university's own account, he became its first dean; a dictionary of 1919 dates his deanship from 1897, and a hymnal handbook from 1896 [^nuhist][^numag][^baker][^phh]. He was one of the founders of the American Guild of Organists [^baker][^phh], and he loved unaccompanied singing: he founded choirs at Northwestern, among them an a cappella choir that the university's magazine calls the nation's first [^numag][^cdh]. He died in 1931 [^phh][^cdh].

**A farewell.** Carl F. Price, whose guide to the Methodist Hymnal of 1905 thanks Lutkin for helpful letters, told the piece's story in 1911. Lutkin, he wrote, made it as a farewell to William Smedley, choirmaster at St. James. It was sung, everyone kneeling, after the benediction, and Smedley had not known it was coming [^price][^fenner]. A magazine article of 1892 confirms that St. James's choir was then "under the direction of Mr. Smedley" [^whitney]. No source we read dates the farewell itself.

**The printing.** The earliest known printing is Clayton F. Summy's octavo, published in Chicago in 1900 [^summy][^cdh]. We read a later Summy printing from the same plates, scanned at the Internet Archive. Its cover, catalogue number 1089, reads "farewell anthem with sevenfold amen", "for mixed voices", "a cappella ad lib." [^summy]. Inside, page 2 is headed Andante, "Smoothly and deliberately", with an organ part marked "May be omitted, if desired", as the organ only doubles the voices [^summy][^cdh]. The foot of the page reads "Copyright 1900 by Clayton F. Summy Co.", renewed by Lutkin in 1928, plate C. F. S. Co. 524-3 [^summy]. The blessing begins softly; the Amen begins softly too, swells to loud, then fades and slows to a very soft last chord, and a footnote on page 4 says the Amen may be sung on its own [^summy].

![[2]]

**Into the hymnals.** Lutkin was one of the two music editors of the Methodist Hymnal of 1905, made for the Methodist Episcopal Church and the Methodist Episcopal Church, South [^price][^mh1905][^fenner]. It prints his blessing as No. 748, on page 532, among the "Chants and Occasional Pieces", with the same four parts on two staves, "Smoothly and deliberately", but a short two-bar Amen in place of the sevenfold one, and the line "Copyright, C. F. Summy Co. Used by permission" [^mh1905].

## What the words mean

The blessing has three lines, and each asks two things of the Lord. "Bless you and keep you": to keep is to guard and protect [^bdb]. "Make His face to shine upon you, and be gracious unto you": a shining face is a friendly one turned toward you, and to be gracious is to show favour and mercy [^bdb]. "Lift His countenance upon you": to lift up one's face toward someone, said of the Lord, is a sign of favour, of looking on someone kindly [^bdb]. "And give you peace": the Hebrew word is shalom, which means completeness, soundness and welfare as well as peace [^bdb].

Lutkin changed the King James text in four ways. He put "you" for "thee". He moved the third line up, so "lift His countenance upon you, and give you peace" comes second and the blessing ends on "gracious". He wrote "make His face to shine" for "make his face shine" and "lift His countenance" for "lift up his countenance". And he repeated words, "and give you peace" twice and "gracious" again and again near the end [^summy][^kjv1611][^cdh].

### The full words

These are the words as Lutkin set them in Summy's printing, as the soprano sings them (the lower voices sing "and be gracious" twice in place of "and be gracious unto you, be gracious"), with the Amen as the tenor and bass sing it, seven times, then the three verses as the King James Version of 1611 prints them, in today's spelling. The Bible text is of 1611 and Lutkin died in 1931, so both are in the public domain.

**As Lutkin set them**
The Lord bless you and keep you,
the Lord lift His countenance upon you;
and give you peace, and give you peace,
the Lord make His face to shine upon you,
and be gracious unto you, be gracious,
the Lord be gracious, gracious unto you.

**Sevenfold Amen**
Amen, Amen, Amen, Amen, Amen, Amen, Amen.

**Numbers 6:24 to 26, King James Version**
The LORD bless thee, and keep thee:
The LORD make his face shine upon thee, and be gracious unto thee:
The LORD lift up his countenance upon thee, and give thee peace.

## How it is sung

Churches use it as a closing blessing: Price's story has it sung kneeling after the benediction, and a modern hymnal handbook gives its use as the end of worship, when the pastor need not say the blessing as well, and also at baptisms, ordinations and weddings [^price][^phh]. The same handbook calls it more an anthem than a hymn, and notes that without the Amen a congregation can sing it, with a plain Amen at the end [^phh].

Our arrangement is read from Summy's printing, in C as printed, every pitch as Lutkin wrote it, and checked against the Methodist Hymnal of 1905 [^summy][^mh1905]. It is sung without the optional organ, slow, in four beats to the bar, with breaths written in for beginners; to make room for them four short notes are left out, and the last chord is held for three beats. It is for four singers only, who sing the blessing together. In the sevenfold Amen the tenor starts alone, the bass a beat later, then the alto and the soprano, and the tenor and bass each sing "Amen" seven times.

## Versions and other words

Many later hymnals print the blessing without the sevenfold Amen [^phh][^hymnary]. A later setting of the same words by a living composer, now widely sung by church and school choirs, is a different piece of music; it is not the one we sing [^fenner].

## Did you know?

- Pi Kappa Lambda, the music honour society founded at Northwestern and chartered by the State of Illinois in 1918, took its name from Lutkin's initials in Greek letters, and he was its first member [^esm][^numag][^eoe].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/the-lord-bless-you/soprano): you sing the top line of the blessing, smooth and slow, climbing to E on "face". In the Amen you come in last, and your second Amen rises to the highest note of the piece, a G, before you fall back down the scale.
- [Alto](/songs/the-lord-bless-you/alto): you sing under the soprano, mostly on C, E and D. "The Lord bless you and keep you" sits almost entirely on one C, so tune it to the bass. You are the third voice into the Amen.
- [Tenor](/songs/the-lord-bless-you/tenor): you sing in the middle of the chord, mostly on C, A and B, and you start the sevenfold Amen alone, with the bass a beat behind you. Your top E comes in the middle of the Amen, held for three beats, and near the end you step down by half steps from A to F before landing on your low E.
- [Bass](/songs/the-lord-bless-you/bass): you sing the lowest line, mostly on G, C and F, from a low F on "keep" up to middle C on "make" and in the Amen. After "countenance upon you" you sing "and give you peace" first, while the others hold "you", and they answer you. You are the second voice into the Amen.

## Questions

### Where is "The Lord bless you and keep you" in the Bible?

It is Numbers 6:24 to 26, in the Book of Numbers, the fourth book of the Bible. In verses 22 and 23 the Lord tells Moses to have Aaron and his sons, the priests, bless the people of Israel with these words, so it is called the priestly or Aaronic blessing [^kjv1611][^je]. Lutkin's anthem sets those verses in his own order, with "you" for "thee" [^summy][^cdh]. In Spanish, in the Reina-Valera of 1909, they read: "Jehová te bendiga, y te guarde: Haga resplandecer Jehová su rostro sobre ti, y haya de ti misericordia: Jehová alce á ti su rostro, y ponga en ti paz." [^rv1909]

### What does "The Lord bless you and keep you" mean?

It asks the Lord to bless and protect you, to look on you kindly and be gracious to you, and to give you peace [^bdb]. "Keep" means guard; a face that shines or is lifted toward you is a sign of favour; and the Hebrew word for peace, shalom, also means wholeness and welfare [^bdb].

### Who wrote the song "The Lord Bless You and Keep You"?

The setting we sing is by Peter C. Lutkin (1858 to 1931), with its sevenfold Amen; its earliest known printing is Clayton F. Summy's, in Chicago in 1900 [^summy][^cdh][^phh]. The words are older: Numbers 6:24 to 26, in the King James Version [^kjv1611]. A later and widely sung setting of the same words by a living composer is a different piece; it is not ours [^fenner].

### Is "The Lord Bless You and Keep You" a hymn?

Not quite: Lutkin's piece is a choral blessing, which its publisher called a farewell anthem, sung at the close of a service [^summy][^phh]. Hymnals print its blessing without the sevenfold Amen, under the tune name BENEDICTION, and a hymnal handbook calls it more an anthem than a hymn [^mh1905][^phh][^hymnary].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the top line of the blessing. But all four parts sing the blessing together, and in the sevenfold Amen the tenor leads, the bass follows a beat behind, then the alto and last the soprano, who rises to the piece's high G [^summy].
