Row, Row, Row Your Boat is a round: a four-line tune that two, three or four singers start at different times, so that it is sung against itself [^hall1874][^grove]. Its first two lines were in print in 1852, as the chorus of The Old Log Hut, a song from the blackface minstrel stage with a different tune [^sinclair1852][^rice][^ballad]. The round, ending "Merrily, merrily" and "Life is but a dream", appears in The Teachers' Institute Glee Book of 1874, prepared in Lancaster, Pennsylvania, by W. B. Hall and E. O. Lyte; from 1881 it is printed with Lyte's name, but no source we found says who made the tune [^hall1874][^mccaskey1881][^ballad]. It went on into camp singing: the Ballad Index finds it in Boy Scout and 4-H songbooks [^ballad].

## Where it comes from

**The song of 1852.** A copy at the University of Wisconsin's Mills Music Library has a cover that reads "Row, Row Your Boat or the Old Log Hut, as sung by Master Adams of Kunkels Nightingale Opera Troupe, Music by R. Sinclair" [^sinclair1852]. Firth, Pond and Co. of New York entered it for copyright in 1852, and no one is named for the words [^sinclair1852][^ballad]. "Master" was then the title for a boy, so the singer was a boy [^webster-master]. His company was a blackface minstrel troupe: George Kunkel, who led it, had performed in blackface since 1844, and a theatre history of 1870 calls it George Kunkel's Nightingale Minstrels [^rice][^brown]. In blackface minstrelsy, white performers darkened their faces to mock Black people, and the minstrel show became one of the most popular entertainments in the country [^pitt-cam][^arkansas]. Nothing in this song's words is in dialect or caricature: its verses are a plain song of home [^sinclair1852]. A minstrel history of 1911 dates Kunkel's company to 1853, a year after this cover [^rice][^sinclair1852].

Inside, the song is headed The Old Log Hut, marked Moderato Affetuoso, in D and in 4/4, for one voice and piano [^sinclair1852]. Its two verses look back on a childhood home: the log hut by the river where "Father and Mother once dwelt", the church, the mill, the old wharf boat where the schoolboys swam, while the river keeps rolling along [^sinclair1852][^ballad]. Then comes the chorus, marked Animato: "Row, row, row your boat, Gently down the stream, All that's past is gone you know, The future's but a dream" [^sinclair1852]. The last two pages set it again for first and second soprano, tenor and bass, and the library's record notes that the melody is not today's [^sinclair1852].

**The round of 1874.** The Teachers' Institute Glee Book was prepared by W. B. Hall, a teacher of vocal music in the Lancaster public schools, and E. O. Lyte, teacher of vocal music at the State Normal School in Millersville, and published by them as Hall & Lyte in 1874 [^hall1874]. On page 39, headed "Row, Row. Round.", the round is one line of melody in D and in 6/8, with no name beside it, and the numbers 1 to 4 stand over it every two bars, where each voice enters [^hall1874]. The words are the ones sung now, with the second half new: "Merrily, merrily, merrily, merrily; Life is but a dream" [^hall1874].

![[1]]

**Later printings.** J. P. McCaskey (1837 to 1935), principal of the Boys' High School in Lancaster, printed the round as "Row Your Boat", with "E. O. Lyte" at the right, in his Franklin Square Song Collection of 1881 [^annals][^loc-mccaskey][^psj1935][^mccaskey1881]. The same book prints another piece, Even Me, "By per. E. O. Lyte. From 'Institute Glee Book'": by Lyte's permission, from the book of 1874 [^mccaskey1881][^hall1874]. In 1893 Lyte printed the round again as No. 5 of his own The School Bell (Lancaster, Pa.; copyright 1892), signed "E. O. L." and marked "From 'The School Song Book'" [^lyte1893].

![[2]]

**E. O. Lyte.** Eliphalet Oram Lyte (1842 to 1913), a Lancaster County native, served in the Union army in the Civil War, and then studied and taught at the state normal school at Millersville, a teachers' college near Lancaster [^annals][^loc-lyte][^mu-blog]. He was its principal from 1887 to 1912 [^annals][^graver].

![[3]]

**Myth and record.** Often told: Eliphalet Oram Lyte wrote Row, Row, Row Your Boat, and some accounts say he published it in 1852 [^amsongwriter]. What the record shows: Lyte was born in 1842, and the sheet music of 1852 names R. Sinclair for the music and no one for the words [^annals][^loc-lyte][^sinclair1852]. The round is unsigned in the book he prepared with Hall in 1874, credited to him in 1881, and signed with his initials in his own book of 1893 [^hall1874][^mccaskey1881][^lyte1893]. James Fuld, as the Ballad Index reports him, knew of no printing of the tune before 1881 and called the credit not necessarily one of authorship [^ballad]. Whether Lyte composed, arranged or supplied the tune, no source we found says.

## What the words mean

1. **"Row, row, row your boat, Gently down the stream."** Keep pulling at the oars, but go with the current, downstream, where the work is easy.
2. **"Merrily, merrily, merrily, merrily."** Merrily means in a merry way, with mirth and laughter [^webster-merrily]. The word is sung four times, each on a lower note.
3. **"Life is but a dream."** "But" here means "only" [^webster-but]: life is only a dream. In the song of 1852, these lines come after a singer looks back on a childhood home, and the dream is the future: "All that's past is gone you know, The future's but a dream" [^sinclair1852]. In the round, the whole of life is the dream.

Readings of the song are interpretation. Classic FM, a British radio station's website, reports one popular reading: that the song is about keeping going and staying focused [^classicfm]. The thought is old. In a book dated 1872, Lewis Carroll ended Through the Looking-Glass with a poem that begins "A boat, beneath a sunny sky" and closes with the lines "Ever drifting down the stream" and "Life, what is it but a dream?" [^carroll1872]. No source we found links Carroll's poem to the round.

### The full words

These are the words as printed in No. 5 of Lyte's The School Bell (1893), the printing our arrangement is read from, with its punctuation; the printing of 1874 has the same words. They were in print by 1874, and Lyte, the only person named with the round in these printings, died in 1913, so they are in the public domain.

Row, row, row your boat,
Gently down the stream;
Merrily, merrily, merrily, merrily;
Life is but a dream.

## How it is sung

**How a round works.** A round, Grove's Dictionary of Music and Musicians explains, is a canon at the unison: the singers begin the same melody one after another at regular distances, and each returns from the end to the beginning, so that the tune passes "round and round" from one to another [^grove]. One of the oldest known is Sumer is icumen in, in a 13th-century English manuscript, British Library Harley 978, with Latin directions for singing it as a round [^grove][^reading][^diamm]. The title page of Thomas Ravenscroft's Pammelia (London, 1609) shows the idea in a phrase: rounds and catches "of 3. 4. 5. 6. 7. 8. 9. 10. Parts in one", meaning many voices from one tune [^pammelia].

**Row Your Boat as a round.** In the printings of 1874, 1881 and 1893 the numbers over the staff show the plan: when the first singer reaches "Gently", the second starts at the beginning, and so on every two bars, up to four voices [^hall1874][^mccaskey1881][^lyte1893]. None gives a count of times round [^hall1874][^mccaskey1881][^lyte1893]. When all four are singing, the four lines of the verse sound at once: one voice sings "Row, row, row your boat" climbing up from the home note, one "gently down the stream" climbing higher, one the "Merrily"s dropping down the chord from the top, and one "Life is but a dream" walking back down to the home note.

**What our arrangement sings.** Our round is read from No. 5 of The School Bell (1893), checked against the printings of 1874 and 1881, in 6/8, a tone lower, in C instead of D [^lyte1893]. All three print "-ly" of "Gently" on E in our key, the note of "Gent-" and "down"; we sing it on D, as the round is commonly sung today [^hall1874][^mccaskey1881][^lyte1893]. The soprano starts alone, the alto comes in two bars later at the same pitch, then the tenor and the bass, each two bars after the last, an octave lower. Each part sings the tune three times through, our choice. The parts finish one by one, and the bass ends alone.

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/row-row-row/soprano): you start alone and carry the tune three times through, so everyone hears it first. Your top note is the C of the first "Merrily", and each "merrily" is three quick notes on one pitch, dropping C, G, E, C. Breathe in the shortened "stream" and "dream"; hold the last "dream" a full bar.
- [Alto](/songs/row-row-row/alto): the same tune at the same pitch, two bars after the soprano: nothing new to learn. The hard part is holding your place: as you sing "Row, row", the soprano is two bars ahead, on "gently down the stream".
- [Tenor](/songs/row-row-row/tenor): the same tune an octave lower, from the C below middle C up to middle C, two bars after the alto. As you start, the soprano is on "Merrily" and the alto on "gently down the stream".
- [Bass](/songs/row-row-row/bass): the tenor's low tune, coming in last, two bars after it; from your entry all four lines sound at once. You finish alone: after the tenor's last "dream", you sing "Life is but a dream" by yourself and hold the last note a full bar.

## Questions

### Does Row, Row, Row Your Boat have a dark meaning?

Not in the record. The 1852 song its first lines come from looks back on a childhood log hut, and its chorus says the past is gone and the future a dream [^sinclair1852]. The round's earliest known printing, in 1874, is in a songbook for schools [^hall1874]. The dark readings we found are modern: a 2009 student newspaper column, written as a mock weather forecast, read the stream as a one-way road to death, as a joke [^nexus].

### What does "life is but a dream" mean?

It means "life is only a dream", and what more it says is a matter of reading [^webster-but]. In the 1852 song the line was "The future's but a dream", after "All that's past is gone you know" [^sinclair1852]. Classic FM reports a popular reading of the round: keep going and stay focused [^classicfm].

### How do you sing Row, Row, Row Your Boat as a round?

Everyone sings the same tune, but starts at a different time. The 1874 printing marks the entries: when the first singer reaches "Gently", the second starts from the beginning, and every two bars another voice joins, up to four [^hall1874]. Each singer goes back to the start at the end, which is what makes it a round [^grove][^webster-round]. In our arrangement each part goes round three times and the parts finish one by one, the bass last and alone.

### Is Row, Row, Row Your Boat a nursery rhyme?

It is sung as one today, and Classic FM calls it one, but it began elsewhere [^classicfm]. Its first lines were the chorus of an 1852 song sung on the minstrel stage, and the round's earliest known printing is in an 1874 songbook for schools, not a book of nursery rhymes [^sinclair1852][^rice][^hall1874]. The Ballad Index files it as a camp song and a round [^ballad].

### What are the original words and melody of Row, Row, Row Your Boat?

The first words were the chorus of an 1852 song, The Old Log Hut: "Row, row, row your boat, Gently down the stream, All that's past is gone you know, The future's but a dream", sung to R. Sinclair's tune in 4/4, which is not today's [^sinclair1852]. Today's words and tune, in 6/8 with "Merrily, merrily" and "Life is but a dream", have their earliest known printing in 1874 [^hall1874]. The printings of 1874, 1881 and 1893 sing the "-ly" of "Gently" one note higher than most singers do now [^hall1874][^mccaskey1881][^lyte1893].
