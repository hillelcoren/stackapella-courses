Echad Mi Yodea is a Hebrew counting song for the Passover seder, the home service and meal on the first nights of the festival.[^idel29][^je] Ashkenazi families sing it near the end of the evening, just before Chad Gadya.[^ej][^jmrc][^sefaria] One voice asks, "Who knows one?", and the table answers: one is Hashem, in heaven and on earth.[^sefaria][^uh1923] Each new number brings a new answer, two tablets of the covenant, three fathers, four mothers, and the whole list is counted back down to one, all the way to thirteen.[^sefaria][^je] Its job was to keep everyone, children above all, awake to the end of a long night.[^ej][^chabad] The Union Haggadah of 1923 turned it into a race: "each reading as fast as possible, in the effort to finish the answer first."[^uh1923]

## Where it comes from

No one knows who made it.[^chabad][^uh1908] The Jewish Encyclopedia of 1903 called it a Hebrew nursery rhyme, probably first recited as a dialogue.[^je] The scholar Zunz placed it in German Haggadot from the fifteenth century on, and many scholars followed him in dating it to fifteenth-century Germany; the Union Haggadah of 1923 agreed, adding "largely under German influence".[^je][^ej][^uh1923] The Encyclopaedia Judaica says it is first found in Ashkenazi Haggadot of the sixteenth century.[^ej] A. Z. Idelsohn, the historian of Jewish music, listed it among seder songs composed "from the tenth century on".[^idel29]

The earliest known copy is handwritten: a Hebrew text with a Yiddish one, added at an unknown date to a copy of the Haggadah printed in Prague in 1526/27, now at the National Library of Israel.[^jmrc][^jl] The earliest known printing is the Prague Haggadah of 1590, which set a Yiddish translation beside it.[^jmrc][^jrb] From then on it was a fixed part of the Ashkenazi seder.[^jmrc] It was not only for Passover: Zunz later found it in the rite of Avignon, in southern France, as a table song for all the festivals.[^je][^ej][^jrb]

The song has a German twin. Karl Simrock's collection of German folk songs, printed in 1851, has "Guter Freund, ich frage dich", "Good friend, I ask you". It counts the same way, and its first answer ends like the Hebrew: one is God the Lord, "der da lebt und der da schwebt im Himmel und auf Erden", who lives and hovers in heaven and on earth.[^simrock][^ej] Its two are the tablets of Moses and its three the patriarchs, but from four on its answers are almost all Christian: the evangelists, the wounds of Christ, the sacraments, the apostles. It stops at twelve.[^simrock][^je] Simrock printed it among his drinking songs, and the Jewish Encyclopedia took it for a peasants' drinking song that monks had reworked.[^simrock][^je]

Which came first is not settled. Zunz thought the Hebrew was taken from a German folk song, and the scholar Perles worked the idea out in detail.[^je][^ej][^jrb] The Encyclopaedia Judaica still says a Christian original was given a Jewish content.[^ej] The Jewish Encyclopedia disagreed: once Zunz had found the song in Avignon, it argued, the theory of a German original had to be revised.[^je] Hebrew University's Jewish Music Research Centre notes that the German songs were first published after 1590, and that researchers have asked whether they rework the Hebrew; a 2025 essay finds no written non-Jewish version clearly older than a Jewish one, while granting that this does not settle the question.[^jmrc][^jrb] Writers have also compared it to Greek and English church songs and a Scottish nursery rhyme.[^je][^ej]

**Myth and record.** Often told: the song is far older, because a manuscript of 1406 is said to record that it was found written on a parchment at Worms, in the Rhineland, in the house of study of Rabbi Eleazar of Worms, who died in 1238.[^chabad][^iemj][^jmrc] What the record shows: the 1406 document is now lost and known only from later report, and another report, of a fourteenth-century copy, is hearsay. The oldest copies that survive are the undated handwritten one and the printing of 1590.[^jmrc][^jrb]

The tune we sing has no known composer. The Union Haggadah of the Central Conference of American Rabbis, the Reform rabbis of America, printed it in its first edition of 1908, in D minor for voice and piano, among the "familiar Passover music, as edited by the Society of American Cantors".[^uh1908][^uh1923] The revised edition of 1923 kept the same tune in the same key.[^uh1908][^uh1923]

## What the words mean

The title is the first line: echad, one; mi, who; yodea, knows. "Who knows one?"[^sefaria][^uh1923] Each verse asks who knows a number, "Shenayim mi yodea?", who knows two?, and answers "shenayim ani yodea", I know two, then names what it stands for and counts back down through every answer before it.[^sefaria][^uh1923] Every verse ends on the same line: "echad Elokeinu shebashamayim uvaaretz", one is Hashem, who is in heaven and on earth.[^sefaria][^uh1923]

Where the Haggadah has Eloheinu, we sing Elokeinu, with a k, the substituted name customary when singing outside prayer. Many Jews alter Hashem's names when they say them outside prayer, out of reverence and to avoid taking the name in vain, and a practice song is outside prayer.[^ohr]

**Verse 1.** One is Hashem, in heaven and on earth.[^sefaria]

**Verse 2.** Sh'nei luchot hab'rit: the two tablets of the covenant.[^sefaria][^uh1923]

**Verse 3.** Sh'losha avot: three fathers, the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. The Talmud keeps the title "fathers" for these three alone.[^berakhot][^jepatriarchs]

**Verse 4.** Arba imahot: four mothers, the matriarchs Sarah, Rebecca, Rachel and Leah, again the four the Talmud names.[^berakhot][^jepatriarchs]

**The verses we don't sing.** Five is the five books of the Torah; six, the six orders of the Mishnah; seven, the days of the week; eight, the eight days to a baby boy's circumcision; nine, the nine months to birth; ten, the Ten Commandments; eleven, the lights in the sky that bowed to Joseph in his dream; twelve, the tribes of Israel; and thirteen, the thirteen attributes of Hashem's mercy, from Exodus 34.[^sefaria][^je][^uh1923] Kaufmann Kohler, writing in the Jewish Encyclopedia, saw meaning in the song's stopping at thirteen, the unlucky number: it made thirteen a holy and so a lucky number for Jews.[^je][^uh1908]

## How it is sung

It comes in the seder's closing section, Nirtzah, after Ki Lo Naeh and Adir Hu.[^sefaria][^jmrc][^ej] The Union Haggadah of 1923 calls it "A Madrigal of Numbers".[^uh1923]

Our arrangement is read from page 93 of that book. There it is "Ehod Mi Yodea", in D minor and 4/4.[^uh1923] We sing it a minor third higher, in F minor, with four parts of our own; the piano part was used only to check the chords. The book prints two verses under the notes and marks the third bar "(Omit for first stanza)". As we read it, that bar is the counting bar, sung once for each number on the way back down to two, so each verse grows by a bar.[^uh1923] We sing verses one to four, their Hebrew from page 87, respelled from the book's Ashkenazi "Ehod" and "luhos hab'ris" in modern Israeli pronunciation. The tune never rests until a verse ends, so short breaths are written in for beginners, and each verse's last note is held, as the book's pause sign asks.

## Versions and other words

The Union Haggadah changed some numbers. Its first edition of 1908 made eight the eight lights of Hanukkah and nine the nine Jewish festivals in its English, while its Hebrew kept the traditional eight days to circumcision and nine months to birth.[^uh1908] The 1923 revision changed the Hebrew too, to the eight days of Hanukkah and nine seasons of joy. Both English texts also make six the six days of creation, where the Hebrew keeps the six orders of the Mishnah.[^uh1923][^uh1908]

Ladino-speaking Sephardi Jews of Salonica, Rhodes and Turkey sing their own version, "Ken supiense".[^uw][^jl][^jmrc] In most of those versions two is Moses and Aaron, the leaders of the Passover story, not the tablets, and in the Rhodes and Turkish versions thirteen is the thirteen principles of faith of Maimonides.[^uw][^jl] The Union Haggadah of 1923 wrote that the Sephardim had refused to admit it into their ritual.[^uh1923] That was not the whole picture: it began to appear in non-Ashkenazi Haggadot in the nineteenth century, and Ladino-speaking families sang it, though few printed Sephardi Haggadot include it.[^jmrc][^uw]

## Did you know?

- The letters of echad, "one", add up to thirteen in Hebrew numbering, so the song starts and ends on Hashem's oneness. Kaufmann Kohler made the point in the Jewish Encyclopedia, the Union Haggadah of 1908 repeated it, and later writers still do.[^je][^uh1908][^chabad]
- In Cochin, in India, it was a wedding song: the Jews there sang it on the Sabbath for a bride and groom.[^ej][^jrb]

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/echad-mi-yodea/soprano): the traditional tune, most often on F, then C. Every verse opens with two Fs and leaps a fifth up to C. Its top D-flat comes in a quick turn of sixteenths on the first "yodea," and on "shebashamayim" in every verse, and its low E only on "uvaaretz.".
- [Alto](/songs/echad-mi-yodea/alto): under the tune, mostly a sixth below, most often on A-flat, then E-flat. Watch the "yodea," after "ani" in every verse: it ends on E natural, though the alto sang E-flat on "ani" a moment before. Its top F comes on "ani" in verses 1, 3 and 4, on the "shenayim" after "mi yodea," in verse 2 and on every "shebashamayim", and its low A-flat on the number that opens each verse, the "echad" before every "elokeinu" and every "uvaaretz.".
- [Tenor](/songs/echad-mi-yodea/tenor): the middle of the chord, most often on A-flat, then F, up to its top B-flat on the first "yodea," in verses 1 and 4 and on every "avot,", "imahot," and "hab'rit,", and down to its low C on every "uvaaretz.". In the last verse it rises to F and G on "uva-" before dropping to that low C.
- [Bass](/songs/echad-mi-yodea/bass): the bass line, most often on F, then C, with its low F on the number that opens each verse, on every "elokeinu" and every "uvaaretz.". Under "echad elokeinu shebashamayim" it moves on almost every eighth note.

## Questions

### Who wrote Echad Mi Yodea?

No one knows. It is an anonymous folk song.[^chabad][^uh1908] The earliest known copy is an undated handwritten addition, in Hebrew and Yiddish, to a copy of a Prague Haggadah printed in 1526/27, and the earliest known printing is the Prague Haggadah of 1590.[^jmrc][^jl][^jrb] Many scholars placed its making in Germany in the fifteenth century, and a lost document of 1406 from Worms is said to show it was older still, but it can't be checked today.[^ej][^jmrc][^jrb]

### Is there an English version of Echad Mi Yodea?

Yes. It is often sung or read in English as "Who Knows One?". The Union Haggadah printed an English version in 1908 and again in 1923, when it was read aloud as a race, and modern Haggadot translate it line by line.[^uh1908][^uh1923][^sefaria]

### Is there a Yiddish Echad Mi Yodea?

Yes, from its earliest records. The earliest known copy, added by hand to a copy of a Prague Haggadah printed in 1526/27, has a Yiddish text with the Hebrew, and the Prague Haggadah of 1590, its earliest known printing, set a Yiddish translation beside it. Ladino-speaking Sephardi Jews of Salonica, Rhodes and Turkey sing their own version, "Ken supiense", where in most versions two is Moses and Aaron, not the tablets.[^jmrc][^jl][^jrb][^uw]

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune throughout, as the Union Haggadah prints it. The alto sings under it, mostly a sixth below. With two singers it is soprano and alto; with three the bass joins; with four, the tenor.[^uh1923]
