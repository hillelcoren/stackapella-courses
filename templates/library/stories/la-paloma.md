La Paloma, "the dove", is a habanera, a song in the rhythm of the Cuban dance of that name, by the Basque composer Sebastián Iradier, also printed as Yradier [^century1906][^henriquezurena1947][^bnf-iradier]. A man leaves Havana, and the refrain asks his love: if a dove comes to your window, treat it kindly, for it is me [^century1906]. Iradier called it a "canción americana", an American song, and published it himself in Madrid by 1859 [^boletin1861][^bnf-work]. It travelled fast. By 1878 it was printed in Boston with English words, a Portuguese novel, in its 1889 edition, has a young woman sing it at the piano, and by about 1896 it was on a gramophone disc [^ava1887][^eca1889][^bnf-1896].

## Where it comes from

**A singing teacher from Álava.** Iradier was born in 1809 in Álava, in the Basque Country, and died in 1865 in Vitoria [^bnf-iradier][^loc-yradier]. In Madrid he taught solfège, sight-singing for singers, at the Conservatory: Baltasar Saldoni, a colleague there, records a pupil entering his class in March 1848 [^saldoni2]. In his dictionary of Spanish musicians, Saldoni calls Iradier an author "muy conocido", well known, for his popular songs, and adds a complaint: he asked Iradier many times for his life story, Iradier never refused, and he died without giving it [^saldoni4]. Saldoni also passes on what Iradier told him, that he had been the teacher of the future Empress Eugénie of France, and that she had a fine contralto voice [^saldoni2].

**Cuba.** Later historians say Iradier lived or travelled for years in Cuba [^henriquezurena1947][^livermore1972][^lexdk], where, a Danish encyclopedia says, he came to know the habanera [^lexdk]. A Cuban dictionary of 1862 describes the island's favourite dance, the danza, in 2/4, and says it was formerly called the contradanza [^pichardo1862]. When, and for how long, the sources we have read do not agree, so we leave the dates out. The song itself points to Havana: its first line is "Cuando salí de la Habana", when I left Havana [^century1906].

![[1]]

**Registered in Madrid.** The earliest record of the song we have found is a Madrid list of works registered as literary property, printed in 1861 in the Boletín bibliográfico español. Entry 917 reads: "LA PALOMA, cancion americana con acompañamiento de piano, música de D. Sebastian Iradier, editor. Madrid, 1859. En folio." It sits in the list for December 1860, the month the edition was presented at the Ministry of Development. The list names Iradier himself as "editor", the publisher, as it does for each of his songs there, and the song was for voice and piano, in an edition dated 1859 [^boletin1861]. Four entries earlier, his "Juanita ó la perla de Aragon, cancion española" is dated 1857 [^boletin1861]. The Bibliothèque nationale de France, citing the Grove dictionary, records the same order: published first in Spanish in Madrid in 1859, then in French in Paris in 1864 [^bnf-work]. That September the Paris weekly Le Ménestrel offered it to subscribers as "La Paloma (Colombe)", No. 14 of 25 Iradier songs with French words [^menestrel1864]. A Danish encyclopedia dates the song about 1863, after Cuba [^lexdk]; the Madrid list shows it was in print earlier.

![[2]]

**The pages we read.** Our arrangement comes from a New York edition, copyright 1906, Century Music's "Vocal Blossoms" series, No. 84, "La Paloma, Low, C, Yradier" [^century1906]. Page 2 is headed "LA PALOMA. CANCIÓN AMERICANA.", with English words by "M. H. R." over the Spanish. Under the Spanish a previous owner pencilled a word-for-word English gloss, starting on page 2 with "When I came up from the havana protect me God" [^century1906]. We checked it against the Artists' Vocal Album of 1887 (Boston, White, Smith & Co.), where the song is "La Paloma (Fly, Dove, o'er the Billow)", "Translated by L. C. Elson", its page marked "Copyright, 1878" [^ava1887]. Both prints have the same first verse and the same patter coda.

![[3]]

**Myth and record.** Often told: La Paloma was a favourite at the Mexican court of the Emperor Maximilian and the Empress Carlota. A Danish encyclopedia says it spread in Mexico under Maximilian, both at court and among his opponents, who sang mocking words to it [^lexdk], and a 1972 history of Spanish music says the song outlived the two empires it served, Eugénie's in France and Carlota's in Mexico [^livermore1972]. What the record shows: we have found no document from the court itself that names the song. What is in print is that Mexican liberals sang parodies of it [^siller][^lexdk]. A study of the songs of the French Intervention counts "La nueva Paloma" among the popular songs against the invader; it follows Iradier's verse line by line, from "Cuando salí del Congreso", when I left Congress, and rewrites his coda about the Austrians [^siller].

## What the words mean

No print we have read names anyone but Iradier for the Spanish words, though the 1861 register credits him only with "música", the music, where for another of his songs it says "letra y música", words and music [^boletin1861][^century1906][^ava1887]. The song has four verses, each followed by the refrain, and a quick patter coda [^century1906].

1. **Leaving Havana.** When I left Havana, God help me ("¡Válgame Dios!", an everyday cry), no one saw me go but me. And a pretty "guachinanga" came following after me. A Cuban dictionary of 1862 says "guachinango, ga" was the name for people from Mexico [^pichardo1862], so she is a Mexican girl. "Allá voy yo", off I go, and "que sí, señor", oh, yes, sir, are lively fillers.
2. **The refrain.** If a dove comes to your window, treat it kindly, for it is my very self. Tell it of your loves, treasure of my life ("bien de mi vida"), and crown it with flowers, for it is mine. Then he pleads: oh, "chinita", darling, say yes, give me your love, come with me to where I live.
3. **The wedding verses.** Verses 2 to 4 tease about the marriage: the day we marry, we'll go straight from the church to sleep, side by side; the little priest ("curita") will bless us in the cathedral, I'll give you my hand, and the priest will give us two sprinkles of holy water ("hisopazos"); and in time we'll have at least seven, or fifteen, little "guachinanguitos" [^century1906].
4. **The coda.** A comic patter of short notes. Its "-au" endings are a clipped "-ado": "enseñau" for "enseñado", shown. Haven't I shown you the much-praised "cuadrilátero", quadrilateral, that the Austrians have given to my love, all finely drawn, and the little certified paper saying the war is over, stuck on with three sealing wafers ("obleas")? [^century1906][^ava1887] It sounds like a joke on the news of the day; we have found no source that explains it.

## How it is sung

It is a solo song with piano, in 2/4, marked Allegretto [^century1906][^ava1887]. Under the voice the piano keeps the habanera's rocking pattern all the way through, the coda included [^century1906]. The print runs verse 1, the refrain and the coda, then goes back to the start ("D.C.") for the later verses [^century1906].

Our arrangement is verse 1 and the refrain in Spanish, in C as printed, for 2 to 4 singers, the coda left out. The soprano has Iradier's tune; the alto and tenor sing the words with it on his chords; and the bass takes the piano's rhythm on "dm", so the habanera never stops.

## Did you know?

- In the 1889 edition of Eça de Queirós's novel O crime do padre Amaro, set in Leiria, Portugal, a young woman sings it at the piano after tea; the novel calls it an old Mexican song, "a Chiquita", and quotes "Quando sali de la Habana" and the dove at the window [^eca1889].
- It is not the habanera in Bizet's Carmen, though that one came from Iradier too: in 1887 his Paris publishers' weekly, Le Ménestrel, said Bizet had used Iradier's El Arreglito without knowing whose it was, and that Carmen's score marks the habanera "imitée d'une chanson espagnole", imitated from a Spanish song [^menestrel1887][^henriquezurena1947]. A library copy of El Arreglito, printed in Paris as a "chanson havanaise", a Havana song, carries a handwritten note: "Imité par Bizet dans Carmen" [^mcgill1862].
- In 1902 the Victor company recorded it sung in Spanish; the Library of Congress National Jukebox has the disc [^jukebox1902].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/la-paloma/soprano): you sing the tune. The first line climbs, partly in quick triplets, E, F, G, A, B, C on "sa-lí de la Ha-ba-na", and "Na-die" jumps straight up to D. In the refrain's last "¡ay!" you touch your top F, then "vi-vo yo" falls to the low C at the end.
- [Alto](/songs/la-paloma/alto): you sing the words mostly a third or a sixth under the tune, between low B and G. Hold steady on F through "que ven-te con mi-go" the first time and on G the second.
- [Tenor](/songs/la-paloma/tenor): you sing mostly C and G. The whole first line, up to "Vál-ga-me", is on one C, so keep it bright and in tune. In the refrain you rise to D for "mor, ¡Ay! que ven-te con mi-go, Chi-", your highest note.
- [Bass](/songs/la-paloma/bass): you are the habanera. On "dm" you sing its pattern, long, short, then two even notes, on C, G and D, from low G up to G, with a breath before each line. Keep it light and exact; everyone else leans on it.

## Questions

### What does La Paloma mean in English?

"La paloma" means "the dove". In the song a man leaving Havana asks the woman he loves to welcome any dove that comes to her window, treat it kindly and crown it with flowers, because the dove is him; then he begs her to come with him to where he lives [^century1906].

### Is La Paloma a Mexican or a Cuban song?

Neither, strictly: it is a Spanish composer's song in the Cuban habanera style. Sebastián Iradier, a Basque, published it in Madrid by 1859 as a "canción americana" [^boletin1861][^bnf-iradier]. Its words begin in Havana [^century1906], and historians say he spent years in Cuba [^henriquezurena1947][^livermore1972]. Mexico took it up: liberals fighting the French Intervention sang parodies of it [^siller][^lexdk]. The story that it was a favourite at Maximilian and Carlota's court is told [^lexdk][^livermore1972], but we have found no court document naming it.

### Is it the same song as Paloma negra or Cucurrucucú paloma?

No. Those are different songs, with their own words and tunes. Iradier's La Paloma is the one that begins "Cuando salí de la Habana" and has the refrain "Si a tu ventana llega una paloma" [^century1906]. Rafael Alberti's poem "La paloma", from his book Entre el clavel y la espada, is another work again, later set to music by Carlos Guastavino and others [^uned-alberti][^liedernet-alberti].

### Who first sang La Paloma, and is there an English version?

No record says who first sang it: Iradier published it in Madrid by 1859 for voice and piano, and the register names no singer [^boletin1861]. The earliest recording we know is a tenor's Berliner disc of about 1896, by F. Giannini, and Victor recorded it in Spanish in 1902 [^bnf-1896][^jukebox1902]. English words came early: L. C. Elson's "Fly, Dove, o'er the Billow" (1878) and M. H. R.'s (1906) [^ava1887][^century1906]. In the 1961 film Blue Hawaii, Elvis Presley sang "No More", new words by Don Robertson and Hal Blair to Iradier's tune [^afi1961][^cce1961][^poparchives][^originals].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings Iradier's tune. The alto and tenor sing the same words with it, the alto mostly a third or a sixth below, and the bass sings the habanera rhythm on "dm" underneath. With two singers you have the tune and the alto; add the bass for three, so the rhythm is there.
