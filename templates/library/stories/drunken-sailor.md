Drunken Sailor, also sung as "What Shall We Do with the Drunken Sailor?" or "What Shall We Do with a Drunken Sailor?", is a sea shanty: a work song that sailors sang to pull together on a rope. The shantyman asks what to do with the drunken sailor, and everyone answers, "Hooray and up she rises, early in the morning" [^terryscan][^bullen]. The master mariner W. B. Whall, who printed it as "Early in the Morning", remembered the whole crew singing it at a quick march as they walked away with the rope [^whall]. Its chorus is in a whaler's journal of 1840, printed in 1841 [^olmsted][^balladindex]. Richard Terry, who printed this tune in 1921, called it "always a great favourite" [^terry].

## Where it comes from

**A whaler out of New London.** On 11 October 1839 the whaling ship North America sailed from New London, Connecticut, for the Pacific, with a young passenger, Francis Allyn Olmsted, who kept a journal [^olmsted]. Her crew was mixed, he wrote: Americans, three men he called Indians (one of them John Uncas, a Mohegan), an Englishman, six Portuguese and several Black sailors. The cook, a Black man often called "Mr. Freeman", was in Olmsted's words "the fountain of all the fun and good humor aboard the ship" [^olmsted].

![[1]]

On 11 February 1840 Olmsted wrote about the songs the men sang as they hauled on the rigging. They went well "when there is a long line of men hauling together". Mr. Freeman, he wrote, "usually officiates as chorister", striking up the song "while all the rest join in the chorus". Sometimes, he added, "they all sing together as I have endeavored to represent", and that is how he wrote down the first song he printed [^olmsted]: its tune, with the words "Ho! Ho! and up she rises" three times and "Early in the morning". His *Incidents of a Whaling Voyage* came out in New York in 1841, the earliest known printing of the chorus, though without the drunken sailor verse [^olmsted][^balladindex].

![[2]]

**The sailors who remembered it.** The verse is in print by 1908 at the latest, in the poet John Masefield's *A Sailor's Garland*, whose first edition came out in 1906 [^masefield][^openlibrary]. W. B. Whall went to sea in 1861 and spent eleven years in the old passenger ships, the East Indiamen, writing down songs as sailors sang them, by his own account [^whall]. He wrote that, though shanties were generally thought never to be allowed in the Royal Navy, this song and one other were sometimes allowed, in revenue cutters especially, or sung under the breath in bigger ships [^whall]. In 1914 Cecil Sharp printed a version sung by James Tucker at Bristol [^sharp], and Frank Bullen, who had been a shantyman at sea, printed another [^bullen].

![[3]]

**Terry's tune.** Richard Runciman Terry was a church musician, from 1901 the organist and director of music at Westminster Cathedral [^oxford][^julian]. His mother's family, he wrote, had followed the sea as far back as it could be traced, and sailor uncles sang him shanties from childhood; in the 1890s he lived in Antigua, where he heard shanties sung ashore [^terry][^oxford]. In *The Shanty Book* of 1921 he printed Drunken Sailor as No. 14, with six verses, and called it "in the first Mode", the mode now called Dorian [^terry][^terryscan]. It is the tune we sing.

![[4]]

**Where shanties came from.** Terry wrote that sailors picked up songs wherever they went, and that a number of shanties without doubt came from African American singers [^terry]. A Library of Congress folklorist writes that the most valued singer on board was often a Black sailor, and that shanties can be traced to African American work songs [^winick]. Steam ended the shanty's age, when the steam winch took over the windlass and capstan; Whall had not heard a shanty worth the name since 1872 [^terry][^whall].

**Myth and record.** Often told: the song was first written down on a whaling voyage of 1839. What the record shows: the voyage began in October 1839, the journal entry is dated 11 February 1840, and the book was printed in 1841 [^olmsted].

## What the words mean

The song is a joke: each verse offers a rough cure to sober the drunken sailor up [^terryscan].

**The chorus.** "Hooray and up she rises" is the crew's answer. The older printings sing it differently: "Ho! Ho!" in 1841, "Way, hay, there she rises" in Masefield, "Way, ay" in Sharp, "Hoo-rah" in Whall, and "Hooray" in Bullen and Terry [^olmsted][^masefield][^sharp][^whall][^bullen][^terryscan]. None of them says what "she" is.

**Verse 2.** Put him in the long-boat until he's sober: usually the largest boat a ship carried [^smyth]. In Whall and Sharp he is also made to bail it out [^whall][^sharp].

**Verse 3.** Pull out the plug and wet him all over. A boat's plug was a cone of wood fitted into a hole in its bottom [^smyth].

**Verse 4.** Put him in the scuppers with a hose-pipe on him. The scuppers are the openings along the deck's edge that let water run off into the sea [^smyth].

**Verse 5.** Heave him by the leg in a running bowlin'. A running bowline is a bowline tied round its own rope, a noose [^smyth]: haul him up by the leg.

**Verse 6.** Tie him to the taffrail when she's yard-arm under. The taffrail is the rail round the top of the stern, and a yard-arm is the end of a spar that carries a sail [^smyth]. A ship "yard-arm under" leans so far that the end of a yard dips into the sea: a soaking.

### The full words

These are the words of No. 14 in Richard Terry's *The Shanty Book, Part I* (1921), pages 30 and 31 [^terryscan]. Terry prints verses 2 to 6 as one line each, marked "thrice"; here they are written out as sung, with "Early in the morning" to close each, as in verse 1, and the chorus after each. Terry has "with the drunken sailor"; we sing "with a", as Whall, Masefield and Sharp print it [^whall][^masefield][^sharp]. The words are traditional, printed in 1921, and Terry died in 1938, so they are in the public domain [^oxford].

Early in the morning?

**Chorus, after every verse**

## How it is sung

It did more than one job.

- **Stamp and go.** Whall wrote that it was the only song used for a "stamp and go": the whole crew sang as they marched away along the deck with the rope, with no pause between verses. When crews grew too small to walk away with anything, it dropped out of use [^whall]. The Sailor's Word-Book of 1867 gives "Stamp and go!" as the order to step out at the capstan or with a rope, often to a fife or fiddle [^smyth].
- **A runaway chorus.** Masefield describes all hands racing along the deck at the braces to swing the great yards round, stamping on each "Way, hay" [^masefield]. Sharp was told the chorus was sung as sailors tramped along the deck raising a yard [^sharp].
- **The capstan.** Terry put it among the windlass and capstan shanties, for weighing the anchor, and was told by Sir Walter Runciman, who wrote the book's foreword, that he often sang it for hauling hand over hand [^terry].

So the books disagree about its main job: a stamp and go for Whall, mostly the windlass or capstan for Terry [^whall][^terry].

Terry, Masefield and Whall agree it was quick [^terry][^masefield][^whall]. They differ on who sang what. Whall says the whole crew sang every line, Masefield calls it a chorus with no solo part, and Olmsted wrote his down as sung by all together [^whall][^masefield][^olmsted]. Sharp and Bullen give the verse to the shantyman and the chorus to the crew [^sharp][^bullen].

Terry printed it in 2/4, with no key signature, in D Dorian, a minor-sounding scale with a raised sixth [^terryscan][^terry]. Our arrangement is read from his page, a whole tone higher in E and slower than the ships sang it: verse 1 and the chorus for 2 to 4 singers, the soprano singing the verse alone as Terry wrote it, and the others joining on the chorus with the notes of his piano part. The harmony is his, not the ships'.

## Versions and other words

- **Other tunes.** Sharp heard a different tune from James Tucker, "obviously a bagpipe air" [^sharp]. The two books of 1914 disagree about Bullen's tune: Sharp thought it matched another sailor's and was probably "the normal form of the tune", while Bullen's co-editor, W. F. Arnold, called it unique [^sharp][^bullen]. Terry blamed sailors of the last days of sail for singing it in a major key, and called that a perversion of the old tune [^terry].
- **A drunken soldier.** Whall and Masefield both print a verse for the army: what to do with a drunken soldier? Put him in the guardroom till he gets sober [^whall][^masefield].

## Did you know?

- Frank Bullen called it "an exceedingly fine spirited Chanty, apart altogether from its trivial words", and remembered it best from the Savage Club in London, where the members sang its chorus with all their might [^bullen].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/drunken-sailor/soprano): you are the shantyman. Sing "What shall we do with a drunken sailor" alone, then lead the chorus. The third line of each climbs to your top E, on "sailor" and on "rises"; the C-sharp on the way up, in the third "drunken" and the third "she", is the note that makes the tune Dorian.
- [Alto](/songs/drunken-sailor/alto): you rest through the verse and come in on "Hooray and up she rises", mostly on G and F-sharp, dropping to a low G on "morning".
- [Tenor](/songs/drunken-sailor/tenor): you come in on the chorus in strong, simple chords, mostly on B and A, rising to D on the third "up" and finishing on E on "morning".
- [Bass](/songs/drunken-sailor/bass): you sing the chorus mostly on the roots of the chords, E and D above all, and step down to a low G on the third "rises". Keep it steady, like feet on a deck.

## Questions

### Who originally wrote Drunken Sailor?

No one knows: it is traditional, and no early source names a maker. Its chorus is in print by 1841, from a journal kept in 1840 on the New London whaler North America, where the crew sang it together as they hauled [^olmsted][^balladindex]. The verse, "What shall we do with a drunken sailor", is in print by 1908 at the latest, in John Masefield's *A Sailor's Garland* [^masefield]. The tune we sing is the one Richard Terry printed in 1921 [^terryscan].

### What are the original words of Drunken Sailor?

There is no single original text. The earliest known printing, in 1841, from a whaler's journal of 1840, has only the chorus: "Ho! Ho! and up she rises" three times, then "Early in the morning" [^olmsted]. The verse "What shall we do with a drunken sailor" is in print by 1908, in Masefield's *A Sailor's Garland*, with the chorus "Way, hay, there she rises" [^masefield]. The six verses most people sing follow Richard Terry's *The Shanty Book* of 1921 [^terryscan].

### Is Drunken Sailor Irish?

No old source we have read says so. The earliest record of its chorus is from an American whaling ship out of Connecticut in 1840 [^olmsted]. The collectors who printed it heard it at sea or in English ports: Whall in East Indiamen from 1861, Sharp from James Tucker at Bristol, and Terry from Sir Walter Runciman among others [^whall][^sharp][^terry]. A link to an Irish tune is sometimes suggested, but we found no printing before 1921 that makes it.

### What does the captain's daughter mean in Drunken Sailor?

We found no printing before 1921 that has that verse: it is in none of the versions printed from 1841 to 1921 that we read [^olmsted][^masefield][^whall][^sharp][^terryscan]. Some say it means a flogging, but we found no printing before 1921 that makes that reading. Sailors did have a name of that shape: The Sailor's Word-Book of 1867 says "the gunner's daughter" was the gun to which boys were "married", or lashed, to be punished [^smyth].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune. In our arrangement the soprano is the shantyman, singing the verse alone, and keeps the tune when everyone comes in on the chorus; the alto, tenor and bass sing only the chorus, on the harmony of Richard Terry's 1921 piano part [^terryscan].
