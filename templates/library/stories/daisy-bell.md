Daisy Bell, the song many people know as A Bicycle Built for Two or simply "Daisy, Daisy", was written and composed by Harry Dacre and published in 1892 by T. B. Harms in New York and Francis, Day and Hunter in London [^harms1892][^powell][^loc-nrr]. It is a waltz in which a young man proposes to Daisy: he can't afford a carriage, but she will look sweet on the seat of a tandem bicycle [^harms1892]. Its chorus was sung in the music halls of the 1890s [^powell][^loc-nrr], and in 1961 researchers at Bell Labs made a computer sing it in a synthesized voice [^loc-nrr][^ccrma][^mathews2011]. Seven years later the dying computer HAL sang it in the film 2001: A Space Odyssey [^loc-nrr][^mathews2011].

## Where it comes from

**The songwriter.** Harry Dacre was the pen name of Frank Dean, born in Douglas, on the Isle of Man, in 1857, the year of his baptism; the Victoria and Albert Museum's catalogue gives 1860 [^powell][^lucerna][^vam]. Some American sources call him Henry Decker, perhaps a name he used there; the census and probate records know him as Frank Dean [^loc-nrr][^powell][^lucerna]. The census of 1881 found him living in Manchester, listed as a "dramatic author" [^powell][^lucerna]. He later ran a music publishing firm in London, Frank Dean and Co. [^powell][^lucerna][^loc-auth].

**The sheet music.** The copy we read, scanned for the Internet Archive, has a red and green cover: "Daisy Bell, Song, Written and Composed by Harry Dacre", published by T. B. Harms and Co. of New York and Francis, Day and Hunter of London, "Copyright MDCCCXCII" (1892), and entered at Stationers' Hall, London [^harms1892]. A music dealer in St. Paul, Minnesota stamped the cover, and an owner signed the top and added "94, Jan." [^harms1892]. Its back page advertises two other Harms songs copyrighted in 1893, one of them Dacre's Rainbow in de Sky!, so this copy was printed in 1893 or later, from the plates of 1892 [^harms1892]. Inside, the song is marked Tempo di Valse, waltz time, in G, and all three verses are printed under the one tune, with the chorus on page 5, marked "A little faster" [^harms1892]. Quotation marks flag the puns: "tandem", "Ped'ling", "lamps", "bright lights", "wheel", "lead" and "trip", and the bell is printed "bell(e)" [^harms1892].

![[2]]

**The music halls.** The singer most closely tied to the song in its first years was Katie Lawrence, a British music hall performer [^powell][^loc-nrr]. Dacre wrote a sequel, Fare You Well, Daisy Bell, published in London in 1894, in which Daisy turns her suitor down and he resolves to ride alone [^vam][^powell]. Dacre died in London on 16 July 1922 [^lucerna][^powell][^loc-auth].

**Myth and record.** Often told: when Dacre brought his bicycle to America he was charged import duty on it, and a friend joked that he was lucky it was not a bicycle built for two, or he would have paid double; the phrase became the song's last line. The Library of Congress essay calls this a legend, BBC Music Magazine tells it as a story that goes around, and a Manx music history, following a 2014 history of the music hall, sees no reason to doubt it [^loc-nrr][^bbcmusic][^powell]. What the record shows: every telling we found was written long afterwards, and none quotes Dacre or anything printed in 1892. Often told, too: Daisy was Daisy Greville, Countess of Warwick, a society hostess; the Manx history calls this "generally acknowledged" and BBC Music Magazine repeats it [^powell][^bbcmusic]. What the record shows: the sheet music ties Daisy to no real person, and we found no word from Dacre linking his Daisy to her [^harms1892].

**The computer that sang.** In 1961, at Bell Labs, the physicist John L. Kelly Jr. and a programmer in Max Mathews's department made a computer sing Daisy Bell, and Mathews (1926 to 2011), often called the father of computer music, programmed the accompaniment [^loc-nrr][^loc-pr][^ccrma][^mathews2011]. Their program modelled the human vocal tract as a tube whose shape the computer could change [^mathews2011][^ccrma]. The song was issued, about 1962, on a Decca LP, Music from Mathematics [^mfm], whose cover says its music was "Played by I.B.M. 7090 Computer"; the Library of Congress and a Stanford account say the voice was computed on an IBM 704, and we found no paper from the team itself to settle it [^mathews2011][^loc-pr][^loc-nrr][^ccrma].

**HAL's song.** In 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968) the computer HAL sings the song as it is switched off, and two accounts tell how it got there [^loc-nrr][^ccrma]. The Library of Congress and a Stanford account say that Arthur C. Clarke heard the singing computer while visiting his friend John Pierce at Bell Labs, and put it into the story [^loc-pr][^loc-nrr][^ccrma]. Mathews told it another way: Stanley Kubrick sent someone to Bell Labs to ask what a telephone booth in space should be like, Pierce sent the visitor to hear Mathews's computer music, and the visitor, and apparently Kubrick, liked the singing [^mathews2011]. Mathews added that the film did not use the tape he sent; the song was made again for it [^mathews2011]. In June 2010 the Library of Congress named the 1961 recording to the National Recording Registry, among its selections for 2009 [^loc-pr][^loc-nrr][^nmusa].

## What the words mean

1. **Verse 1.** Love for Daisy grows in the singer's heart like a flower, planted by a single glance, as if by Cupid's arrow. He can't tell whether she loves him or not, but he longs to "share the lot", to share her life's fortune, as her husband.
2. **Chorus.** He begs her to answer; he is half crazy with love. The wedding won't be stylish, and he can't afford a carriage, a wheeled vehicle made for elegance and comfort [^webster-carriage]. But she will look sweet on the seat of a bicycle built for two.
3. **Verse 2.** They will go "tandem" as man and wife, one seated behind the other like the riders of a tandem bicycle [^webster-tandem], pedalling down the road of life together. When the road is dark they need not care about policemen or lamps, because her dazzling eyes give all the light they need.
4. **Verse 3.** He will stand by her in "wheel" or woe: "wheel" was a word for a bicycle, and "weal or woe" means in good times or bad [^webster-wheel][^webster-weal]. She will be the "bell(e)" he rings: a bicycle bell, and a belle, a beautiful young woman [^webster-belle]. She will take the "lead" in every "trip", and if he misbehaves, she may use the brake.

### The full words

These are the three verses and the chorus as printed in Dacre's sheet music (copyright 1892), pages 3 to 5, quotation marks and all. The chorus is printed once, on page 5, after the verses. Dacre died in 1922 and the song was published in 1892, so all of it is in the public domain.

![[3]]

**Verse 1**
There is a flower within my heart,
Daisy, Daisy!
Planted one day by a glancing dart,
Planted by Daisy Bell!
Whether she loves me or loves me not,
Sometimes it's hard to tell;
Yet I am longing to share the lot
Of beautiful Daisy Bell!

**Chorus**
Daisy, Daisy,
Give me your answer, do!
I'm half crazy,
All for the love of you!
It won't be a stylish marriage,
I can't afford a carriage,
But you'll look sweet
On the seat
Of a bicycle built for two!

**Verse 2**
We will go "tandem" as man and wife,
Daisy, Daisy!
"Ped'ling" away down the road of life,
I and my Daisy Bell!
When the road's dark we can both despise
P'licemen and "lamps" as well;
There are "bright lights" in the dazzling eyes
Of beautiful Daisy Bell!

**Verse 3**
I will stand by you in "wheel" or woe,
Daisy, Daisy!
You'll be the bell(e) which I'll ring, you know!
Sweet little Daisy Bell!
You'll take the "lead" in each "trip" we take,
Then, if I don't do well,
I will permit you to use the brake,
My beautiful Daisy Bell!

## How it is sung

Daisy Bell is a popular song of the 1890s in waltz time, sold as sheet music for voice and piano and sung on the music hall stage [^harms1892][^powell][^loc-nrr]. In 1926 the song historian Sigmund Spaeth wrote that it was still on the song sheets of Kiwanis and Rotary clubs, and that singing crowds still took it up [^spaeth].

Our arrangement is read from Dacre's sheet music (copyright 1892), in its own key of G and in 3/4 [^harms1892]. We sing the first verse and the chorus, for 2 to 4 singers, all on the words: soprano and alto, then the bass, then the tenor. The harmony is our own close harmony, built on the chords of Dacre's piano part, and on "Of a", just before the last line, the tune sings alone.

## Did you know?

- In 1895 Daisy Bell went to court: in Fuller v. Blackpool Winter Gardens, over its use, in costume, in a burlesque, the court held that the song was not a "dramatic piece" under the copyright law [^copinger][^powell].
- The Bell Labs computer sang with Russian vowels: its vocal tract shapes came from X-ray studies of Russian speakers by Gunnar Fant in Stockholm, close enough to English to be understood [^mathews2011][^ccrma].
- An early recording, an Edison brown-wax cylinder sung by Edward M. Favor and dated by the UC Santa Barbara library to 1894, can be heard free in its Cylinder Audio Archive [^ucsb].
- The Library of Congress calls the 1961 recording "the earliest known recording of a computer-synthesized voice singing a song" [^loc-pr].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/daisy-bell/soprano): you carry the tune, from the D above middle C to the E a ninth higher. In the verse, "Daisy, Daisy!" leaps an octave from low D to high D. Your only top E comes on "ti-" of "beau-ti-ful", and "Bell!" then holds a high D. Keep the triplet on "mar-" light, sing "Of a" on your own, and hold the last "two!" for two bars.
- [Alto](/songs/daisy-bell/alto): you sing close under the tune, mostly a third or a sixth below it. At the low ends of the verse's lines, "heart", "-sy" of the first "Daisy", "dart", "not" and "lot", the tune drops below you and you keep the note above it; on the first "Bell!" you rise to your top B over the tune's G.
- [Tenor](/songs/daisy-bell/tenor): you sing in the middle of the chord, mostly on G, C, D and B. On "Some-" and "hard" in "Sometimes it's hard to tell" your C is the seventh of the D7 chord, so tune it with care. At the end you walk down "bi-cy-cle built for two!" to the low D, your lowest note, held for two bars.
- [Bass](/songs/daisy-bell/bass): you sing the words on the bass line, low and steady, mostly on G and D, with E. "Plant-ed by Dai-sy Bell!" starts on your low G and climbs through D to the G an octave above. You end on the low G under "two!", held for two bars.

## Questions

### Why did a computer sing Daisy Bell?

No source we read says why the Bell Labs team chose it. In 1961 John L. Kelly Jr. and a colleague programmed a computer to sing it, Max Mathews wrote the accompaniment, and for years it was part of the formal tour of Bell Labs [^loc-pr][^loc-nrr][^ccrma][^mathews2011]. Mathews explained that a song was easier than speech for a computer, since the tune supplies the pitch of every syllable [^mathews2011].

### Why is Daisy Bell creepy?

No source we read calls the song eerie: its 1892 words are a cheerful proposal full of cycling puns [^harms1892]. In 1961 a Bell Labs computer sang it in a voice the Library of Congress compares to a dial tone, and in 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968) the computer HAL sings it as it is switched off, slowing as its power ebbs, a scene the Library counts among science fiction film's most famous [^loc-nrr][^ccrma]. Since then software, a video game and a television series have had machines sing it [^loc-nrr].

### Who was Daisy in Daisy Bell?

No one we can name. Dacre's sheet music calls her only Daisy Bell [^harms1892]. A Manx music history and BBC Music Magazine say she was Daisy Greville, Countess of Warwick, a society hostess [^powell][^bbcmusic], but we found no word from Dacre linking his Daisy to her [^harms1892]. In the song, Daisy is simply the girl the singer hopes to marry.

### Is Daisy Bell a nursery rhyme?

No. Daisy Bell is a popular song of 1892, sold as sheet music for voice and piano and sung on the music hall stage [^harms1892][^powell][^loc-nrr]. An English court in 1895 treated it as an ordinary song, not a stage drama [^copinger][^powell]. In 1926 a song historian found crowds still joining in its chorus [^spaeth].
