Three Blind Mice is an English nursery rhyme and a round: one short tune that three or four singers start at different times, so that it is sung against itself [^grove-round][^sefton1869][^homesongs1906]. Its earliest known printing is in Deuteromelia, a book of rounds and catches printed in London in 1609, where "Three blinde Mice" is a round for three voices that names Dame Julian, a miller and his merry old wife [^deuteromelia1609][^ballad]. The farmer's wife and her carving knife came later: they are in print by 1842 [^halliwell1842]. The music bibliographer James Fuld, as the Ballad Index reports him, called it the oldest printed non-religious song that is still very widely known, and the Index finds it in Boy Scout and 4-H songbooks [^ballad].

## Where it comes from

**The book of 1609.** Its title page reads "Deuteromelia: or The Second part of Musicks melodie ... Of Pleasant Roundelaies; K. H. mirth, or Freemens Songs. And such delightfull Catches", printed in London "for Thomas Adams, dwelling in Paules Church-yard at the signe of the white Lion. 1609" [^deuteromelia1609]. It followed Pammelia, a book of rounds and catches printed earlier that year, which William Chappell, the Victorian music historian, says did so well that a second was wanted [^pammelia1609][^chappell]. He read "K. H." as King Henry's [^chappell].

![[1]]

**Who made it.** The preface is signed only "Thine T. R." [^deuteromelia1609]. Grove's dictionary of 1900 and Chappell name the compiler as Thomas Ravenscroft, a bachelor of music [^grove-ravenscroft][^chappell]. His dates are uncertain, and the sources disagree. Grove's dictionary (1900 printing) puts his birth about 1582 and says his death is given by some as about 1630, by others about 1635 [^grove-ravenscroft]. New Grove, as the Library of Congress records it, has born about 1592 and died about 1635 [^loc-ravenscroft]. A scholarly edition of 2012 says he was probably born in 1589, and that the guesses at his death, 1630 to 1635 or even 1640, have no evidence behind them [^loc-ravenscroft]. The book names no composer for any piece, and Chappell took that to mean Ravenscroft did not know them [^chappell]. His preface says songs many had "priuately ioyed in", that is, enjoyed in private, could now "publikely be inioyed", "made truely Musicall with Art by my correction" [^deuteromelia1609]. So he collected and corrected the round; who first made it, no one knows, and the Ballad Index names him only with a question mark [^ballad].

**The page.** Three blinde Mice is number 13, under the heading "Rounds or Catches of 3. Voices", after a round about a black sow called to her dinner [^deuteromelia1609]. It is one line of diamond-shaped notes on three staves, and these are all its words: "Three blinde Mice, three blinde Mice, Dame Iulian, Dame Iulian, the Miller and his merry olde Wife, shee scrapte her tripe licke thou the knife." [^deuteromelia1609] There is no farmer's wife, no cutting of tails, and nobody runs [^deuteromelia1609][^ballad].

![[2]]

**The farmer's wife.** The earliest printing of the farmer's wife we have found is in James Orchard Halliwell's The Nursery Rhymes of England, printed for the Percy Society with 1842 on its title page; Halliwell later wrote that it was printed at the close of 1841 [^halliwell1842][^halliwell1843]. There, as number LXX, the rhyme is three lines: the mice run after the farmer's wife, "Who cut off their tails with the carving-knife" [^halliwell1842]. His second edition (London, 1843), as number XC, opens "see how they run!" and asks, "Did you ever see such fools in your life?" [^halliwell1843]. In a note he printed the 1609 round, in modern spelling, with one "Dame Julian" and "take thou the knife" where the 1609 page has "licke thou the knife" [^halliwell1843][^deuteromelia1609].

![[3]]

**The tune sung today.** In Toronto in 1869, Henry Francis Sefton, music teacher at the Normal and Model Schools of Ontario, printed "Three Blind Mice (Round for three voices.)" in his Three-Part Songs for Canada's public schools, marked "The Original" [^sefton1869]. It is in 6/8, on three staves numbered 1 to 3, and opens with the three falling notes sung today [^sefton1869]. In 1886 a reader of Notes and Queries, Thos. Ratcliffe of Worksop, wrote that he had learned it at school as "And after them the farmer's wife", though in all the copies of the round he had met the line was "They all ran after the farmer's wife". He asked who had composed the words and the music [^nq1886].

**The printing we sing from.** Home Songs, a Boston book of 1906 from the Oliver Ditson Company, gathers favourite songs, hymns and rounds "for the fireside", with no editor named [^homesongs1906]. On page 95 is "Three Blind Mice (Round in Four Parts)": one line of melody in D and 6/8, numbered 1 to 4 every four bars where each voice comes in [^homesongs1906].

![[4]]

**Myth and record.** Often told: the farmer's wife is Queen Mary I [^thomas1930][^mentalfloss], known as "Bloody Mary" [^londonmuseum][^christiantoday], and the mice are three Protestant bishops she had put to death [^thomas1930][^countryfile], sometimes said to have been blinded first [^countryfile][^mentalfloss]. What the record shows: the Ballad Index says the idea probably goes back to Katherine Elwes Thomas's The Real Personages of Mother Goose (1930) [^ballad]. She reads the farmer's wife as Mary, holder of the old abbey farmlands, and the mice as Ridley, Latimer and Cranmer, the carving knife standing for their burning [^thomas1930]. Mary reigned from 1553 to 1558 [^royal-mary][^londonmuseum]. Ridley and Latimer were burned at Oxford on 16 October 1555, and Cranmer on 21 March 1556 [^hammer1999][^oxfordhistory]. They were burned, not blinded [^hammer1999][^oxfordhistory][^mentalfloss]. The earliest known words, printed half a century after her death, have a miller's wife and her tripe, and we found the farmer's wife in print no earlier than the 1840s [^deuteromelia1609][^ballad][^halliwell1842].

### Timeline

- 1609: Deuteromelia is printed in London for Thomas Adams, with Three blinde Mice as number 13 [^deuteromelia1609][^chappell]
- 1842: Halliwell's Nursery Rhymes of England, printed for the Percy Society, has the farmer's wife and the carving knife [^halliwell1842]
- 1843: Halliwell's second edition adds "see how they run" and, in a note, the round of 1609 [^halliwell1843]
- 1869: Sefton's Three-Part Songs, Toronto, prints it as a round for three voices in 6/8 [^sefton1869]
- 1886: a reader of Notes and Queries asks who wrote "the school round" [^nq1886]
- 1906: Home Songs, Boston, prints it as a round in four parts [^homesongs1906]
- 1930: Katherine Elwes Thomas reads it as a rhyme about Mary I and three bishops [^thomas1930][^ballad]

## What the words mean

1. **"Three blind mice, Three blind mice, See how they run!"** The singer points at three mice that cannot see, scurrying along.
2. **"They all ran after the farmer's wife; She cut off their tails with a carving knife."** The mice follow her, and she turns on them with the big knife for carving meat. In the school version of 1886 she runs after them instead [^nq1886].
3. **"Did ever you see such a sight in your life, As three blind mice."** "Did ever you see" is an older word order for "Did you ever see". The printings differ here: "such fools" in 1843, "hear such a thing" in 1869, "such a thing" in 1886, and "such a sight" in 1906 [^halliwell1843][^sefton1869][^nq1886][^homesongs1906].

**The words of 1609.** On the 1609 page I stands for J and u for v, as in "serue" and "haue", so "Iulian" is Julian, as Halliwell read it [^deuteromelia1609][^halliwell1843]. "Dame" was the title of the mistress of a household, or a woman in general [^webster-dame]. Tripe is the large stomach of a cud-chewing animal, prepared as food [^webster-tripe]. So the miller's merry old wife scraped her tripe, and someone is told, "licke thou the knife" [^deuteromelia1609].

### The full words

These are the words as printed on page 95 of Home Songs (Boston: Oliver Ditson Company, 1906), with its punctuation. They are traditional and were printed in 1906 with no author or editor named, so they are in the public domain.

Three blind mice, Three blind mice,
See how they run! See how they run!
They all ran after the farmer's wife;
She cut off their tails with a carving knife:
Did ever you see such a sight in your life,
As three blind mice.

## How it is sung

**A round and a catch.** In a round, Grove's dictionary explains, singers begin the same melody one after another, and each goes back from the end to the beginning, so the tune passes round and round [^grove-round]. A catch, says Webster's of 1913, is a humorous round in which the singers catch up each other's words [^webster-catch]. The books of 1609 and 1869 give Three Blind Mice to three voices; Home Songs makes it four [^deuteromelia1609][^sefton1869][^homesongs1906].

**What our arrangement sings.** Our round is read from the 1906 page, in 6/8, written a whole tone lower, in C instead of D. The soprano starts alone, the alto comes in four bars later at the same pitch, then the tenor and the bass, each four bars after the last, an octave lower. Each part sings the tune twice through, our choice, and the parts finish one by one, so the bass ends alone.
## Versions and other words

- **Sefton's new words, 1869.** Beside "The Original", Sefton printed two adaptations for the same tune: "Three young flies", who fly into a grocer's shop and drop into the honey jar, and "Three silly boys", who slip into the pastry-cook's when they should be at their books, until their teacher looks in at the window [^sefton1869].

## Did you know?

- Deuteromelia also prints "Hold thy peace", the catch that Sir Toby Belch, Sir Andrew Aguecheek and the Clown sing late at night in Shakespeare's Twelfth Night [^deuteromelia1609][^grove-ravenscroft][^twelfthnight].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/three-blind-mice/soprano): you start the round alone and carry the tune twice through, so everyone hears it first. You go down to the low C on every "mice" and up to the top C on "all ran", "farmer's", "cut off their", "carving", "ever you" and "sight". "Three blind mice" is slow, a beat or more to each note; the "farmer's wife" and "carving knife" lines are quick, mostly three to a beat.
- [Alto](/songs/three-blind-mice/alto): the same tune at the same pitch, four bars after the soprano: nothing new to learn. The hard part is holding your place: as you sing "Three blind mice", the soprano is already on "See how they run".
- [Tenor](/songs/three-blind-mice/tenor): the same tune an octave lower, from the C below middle C up to middle C, four bars after the alto. As you start, the soprano is on "They all ran after the farmer's wife" and the alto on "See how they run".
- [Bass](/songs/three-blind-mice/bass): the tenor's low tune, coming in last, four bars after it; from your entry all four lines sound at once. You finish alone, from "ever you see such a sight in your life" to the last "As three blind mice".

## Questions

### Is Three Blind Mice about Bloody Mary?

Not as far as the record shows. The Ballad Index says the reading of the farmer's wife as Mary I, and the mice as three Protestant bishops, probably goes back to a book of 1930 [^ballad][^thomas1930]. The earliest known words, of 1609, have a miller's wife who scraped her tripe, with no farmer's wife and no tails cut off [^deuteromelia1609]. The bishops usually named, Ridley, Latimer and Cranmer, were burned at Oxford in 1555 and 1556, not blinded [^hammer1999][^oxfordhistory][^mentalfloss].

### Who wrote Three Blind Mice?

No one knows. Its earliest known printing is in Deuteromelia (1609), a collection compiled by Thomas Ravenscroft, which names no composer for any of its pieces [^deuteromelia1609][^chappell]. The Ballad Index lists Ravenscroft only with a question mark [^ballad]. The words sung today, with the farmer's wife, are anonymous and in print by 1842 [^halliwell1842].

### What are the original lyrics of Three Blind Mice?

The earliest known words, printed in 1609, are: "Three blinde Mice, three blinde Mice, Dame Iulian, Dame Iulian, the Miller and his merry olde Wife, shee scrapte her tripe licke thou the knife." [^deuteromelia1609] The farmer's wife, the carving knife and the cut tails are in print by 1842 [^halliwell1842].

### How do you sing Three Blind Mice as a round?

Everyone sings the same tune, starting at different times. The 1906 printing numbers the entries 1 to 4, four bars apart: when the first singer reaches "See how they run", the second starts from the beginning, and so on, up to four voices [^homesongs1906]. Each singer goes back to the start at the end [^grove-round].
