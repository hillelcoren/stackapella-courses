Down by the Old Mill Stream, sometimes printed as Down by the Old Millstream, is a song by Tell Taylor, a songwriter and music publisher from Hancock County, Ohio, published in Chicago and dated 12 August 1910 in the copyright records [^cce1910][^scan1910][^loc-auth]. Its singer is an old man remembering the girl he met by a mill stream, who has been his sweetheart for forty years [^scan1910]. By 1911 a male quartet had recorded it for Columbia, and Victor and Edison had issued it too [^dahr][^ucsb], and in 1940 the song historian Sigmund Spaeth used its opening line to teach barbershop harmony, calling it a famous Taylor classic [^spaeth1940]. Findlay, Ohio, holds that the stream is its Blanchard River [^marker][^touring].

## Where it comes from

**The songwriter.** Tell Taylor was born in 1876 on a farm in Hancock County, Ohio, near Findlay [^loc-auth][^touring][^earlyjas][^ballad]. By 1910 he ran his own music publishing firm in Chicago: that year's copyright records list him as the owner of songs by other writers as well as his own, and the back page of our copy gives his address as the Grand Opera House, Chicago [^cce1910][^cce1910a][^scan1910].

![[1]]

**The copy we read.** Our arrangement is read from a copy at Connecticut College's Greer Music Library, scanned for the Internet Archive [^scan1910][^conncoll]. Its green cover is a drawing of a water mill, its wheel spilling water, with a couple walking on the bank [^scan1910]. In one corner is a photograph of three performers, captioned "Fox, Peck & Franks", in which the middle figure appears to be made up in blackface: on stages of the time, white performers darkened their faces to mock Black people [^scan1910]. Inside, printed in G, the verse is marked Andante espressivo, in 4/4; the chorus turns to a slow waltz, Valse lento, in 3/4, marked "(Not fast.)" [^scan1910]. Page 2 reads "Copyright, 1910, by Tell Taylor", and page 5 adds the chorus for male quartet, "Melody in 2d Tenor", arranged by Chas. Miller [^scan1910]. Its back page advertises Taylor's "new ballad hit", I'm Going to Write You a Letter, whose cover calls him the "writer of Down by the Old Mill Stream". That song was entered for copyright on 20 June 1912, so our copy was printed in 1912 or later, after the song had made his name [^scan1910][^cce1912].

![[2]]

**A second name.** The pianist Earl K. Smith, born in Greenfield, Indiana, worked in music publishing houses in Chicago and New York [^ascap1948]. He went to work for Taylor in 1909 as a demonstrator, playing the firm's songs, and the two wrote songs together, such as Flowers of Love (1909); a court later heard that Taylor had credited him as co-writer on thirteen songs [^court1945][^grainger]. In 1937, the last year of the song's first 28-year copyright term, two renewals were filed: one in Smith's name, for words and music by Taylor and Smith, claiming as "one of the authors", dated 14 August, and one by Taylor himself, from Findlay, dated 1 September [^cce1937b][^cce1937a][^court1945]. The court dates Smith's renewal, which it says Vogel obtained on his assignment, 13 August [^court1945]. Taylor died in Chicago in November 1937; Variety reported that he died suddenly on Monday 22 November, and the court's opinion gives the 23rd [^variety1937][^court1945]. He is buried in Van Horn Cemetery, near Findlay [^touring][^earlyjas].

**The court case.** Taylor had assigned the song's copyright to Forster Music Publisher of Chicago, and his heirs signed his renewal over to Forster; the Jerry Vogel Music Company held Smith's, and by 1940 the two firms were in dispute [^court1945][^spaeth1940]. Vogel's case rested on the copies Taylor deposited in 1910: on their insert page of music Smith's name was printed above Taylor's, in smaller letters [^court1945]. None of the nine later editions carried it, the 1910 registration read "Words and music by Tell Taylor", and New York's "Dead Man Statute" barred Smith from testifying about his dealings with Taylor [^court1945]. On 20 February 1945 the Circuit Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit upheld the ruling that Vogel had not proved Smith a co-author, and that Vogel's renewal was void [^court1945]. Smith's hometown tells it differently. A history of Greenfield, Indiana, published by the city in 2012, says Smith wrote the tune and a local man, Charlie Gant, the words, and that Taylor paid them for their rights. It gives no source, and it says Taylor left Smith the copyright in his will, while the court records that Taylor died without one [^greenfield][^court1945]. Our copy names Taylor alone: it is one of the later editions, printed in 1912 or later [^scan1910][^court1945][^cce1912].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Taylor wrote the song in 1908 on the bank of the Blanchard River near Findlay, thinking of a mill he had known as a boy, and an Ohio historical marker in Findlay's Riverside Park gives the year as 1908 [^marker][^touring]. Local accounts, one by a Misamore family historian, name the mill as the Misamore mill on the Blanchard, now gone [^touring][^earlyjas]. What the record shows: the earliest dated trace of the song is its copyright entry of August 1910, made by Taylor's Chicago firm [^cce1910]. Its words name no river, mill or town [^scan1910], and we found no word from Taylor himself about where he wrote it. One local telling even gives both 1908 and about 1910 for the writing [^touring].

## What the words mean

1. **Verse 1.** An old man speaks to his wife. He is dreaming of the days when they were courting under the summer sky. Her golden hair has turned to silver, but he still remembers the place where they first met.
2. **Chorus.** That place was by the old mill stream, the water that turned a mill's wheel [^webster-mill]. She had blue eyes and wore gingham, a cotton or linen cloth, usually striped or checked, woven from yarn dyed first [^webster-gingham]. There he knew she loved him. She was sixteen, his "village queen": the girl the whole village admired.
3. **Verse 2.** Now the mill wheel, the water wheel that drove the mill's machinery, is silent and has fallen down [^webster-mill]. The old oak has withered and lies on the ground. But the two of them are sweethearts still, as in the days of yore, that is, long ago [^webster-yore], after forty years and more together.

### The full words

These are the two verses and the chorus as printed in Taylor's sheet music (copyright 1910), pages 2 to 4, from the Connecticut College copy. The chorus is printed once, after the verses, and is sung after each. Taylor died in 1937, more than 70 years ago, and the song was printed in 1910, so all of it is in the public domain.

**Verse 1**
My darling I am dreaming, of the days gone by,
When you and I were sweethearts, beneath the summer sky;
Your hair has turned to silver, the gold has faded too;
But still I will remember, where I first met you.

**Chorus**
Down by the old mill stream, where I first met you,
With your eyes of blue, dressed in gingham too,
It was there I knew, that you loved me true,
You were sixteen, my village queen, by the old mill stream.

**Verse 2**
The old mill wheel is silent, and has fallen down,
The old oak tree has withered, and lies there on the ground;
While you and I are sweethearts, the same as days of yore;
Although we've been together, forty years and more.

## How it is sung

The song was sold for voice and piano, and its waltz chorus asks for freedom: its first "Down by the" is held under pauses, "You were six-" is marked "very slow", with the piano following the voice, and "teen" and "queen" are held again [^scan1910]. It is printed with a repeat, soft the first time and loud the second (pp and ff), and a D.C. sends the singer back for the second verse [^scan1910].

![[4]]

It was a quartet song early on. Our copy gives male quartets their own page, with the tune in the second tenor, and there the other voices answer with "(sweet sixteen)" and "(village queen)" [^scan1910]. IMSLP lists the same quartet page in a printing with the song's first, 1910 imprint [^imslp]. A male quartet recorded it for Columbia in June 1911; Victor's quartet takes that September were never issued, and its October record was a solo with a quartet chorus [^dahr]. In his 1940 book on barbershop singing, Spaeth used its opening as his example of the tenor climbing while the melody falls, to stay above the lead, and noted that quartets sang the echoes on "my village queen" in various ways [^spaeth1940].

Our arrangement, read from the same copy, is in 3/4, written in A, a step above the printed G, for men's voices [^scan1910]. We sing the chorus once, for 2 to 4 singers: the lead and baritone, then the bass, then the tenor. The harmony is a barbershop voicing over our copy's own chords. The lead sings "You were sixteen" and "my village queen" alone, and the others answer "sweet sixteen" and "village queen", as the quartet page has them.

## Versions and other words

The old mill stream was a common setting for songs of the day: the same 1910 copyright catalogue lists Waiting by the Old Mill Stream, by B. R. Hardy and Alfred Olaff, entered on 23 June 1910 [^cce1910b]. Taylor came back to the scene himself: Down Where the Old Mill Stream Flows, with music by Earl K. Smith, came out under his firm's name in 1914 [^wisc], and On the Banks of the Old Mill Stream, words and music by Taylor, was entered for copyright by Forster on 27 December 1937, a month after his death [^cce1938].

## Singing it

- [Lead](/songs/down-by-the-old-mill-stream/lead): you carry Taylor's tune, from a low C-sharp on "With" up to E on "old" and "there". Your pickup "where I" creeps up a half step before you leap to "first", so tune it with care. Sing "You were sixteen" and "my village queen" alone, taking your time, and let the others answer you.
- [Tenor](/songs/down-by-the-old-mill-stream/tenor): you ring above the tune, mostly on E. On "old" and "mill" you climb to A and hold it while the tune slides down beneath you, and again on "there" and "I". You answer the lead with "sweet sixteen" on one repeated note, then "village queen".
- [Baritone](/songs/down-by-the-old-mill-stream/baritone): you fill the chord between the lead and the bass, mostly on A and C-sharp. You start on the high C-sharp for "Down by the old", above the tune on "Down by", and later sink to the low C-sharp on "With your eyes". Expect to cross the lead, as a barbershop baritone does.
- [Bass](/songs/down-by-the-old-mill-stream/bass): you hold the roots, mostly on a low A. Under the opening "old mill stream" you climb from A through C to E, and on "ging-" you drop to your lowest E. Your "sweet sixteen" answer sits on a low F.

## Questions

### When was Down by the Old Mill Stream written?

Its copyright is dated 12 August 1910, when it was published in Chicago as words and music by Tell Taylor, and that is the earliest dated record of it [^cce1910]. A historical marker in Findlay, Ohio, and other local accounts say he wrote it in 1908 by the Blanchard River, but we found no word from Taylor himself to confirm the year or the place [^marker][^touring].

### What does Down by the Old Mill Stream mean?

It is an old man's song to his wife of forty years. The verses say her golden hair has turned silver, the mill wheel is still and the old oak has fallen, but they are sweethearts still [^scan1910]. The chorus is his memory of meeting her by the mill stream, when she was sixteen, in a gingham dress, the "village queen" [^scan1910][^webster-gingham].

### Is Down by the Old Mill Stream a barbershop song?

Yes, though it was written for voice and piano. Its early sheet music already had a page for male quartet, with echoes on "sweet sixteen" and "village queen", and a male quartet recorded it for Columbia in 1911 [^scan1910][^imslp][^dahr]. In 1940 Sigmund Spaeth's book on barbershop singing used its opening line as a model of the style [^spaeth1940].

### Which part sings the tune?

The lead. In our barbershop arrangement the lead carries Taylor's melody, with the tenor above it, the baritone filling the chord and the bass on the roots, as on the sheet music's quartet page, where the tune is in the second tenor [^scan1910]. The lead also sings "You were sixteen" alone before the others answer.
