Deep River is an African American spiritual, made by enslaved people, about crossing the river Jordan to a home on the other side: "Deep river, my home is over Jordan" [^johnson1925]. No maker's name is known. Its earliest known printing is in the second edition of The Story of the Jubilee Singers (London, 1875), the book of Fisk University's touring student choir, as a bare tune [^marsh1875][^pitt]. In the 1910s the Black baritone and composer Harry T. Burleigh set it slow, for chorus and then for solo voice, and in that form it became a standard song for concert singers [^burleigh1914][^cce1916][^carnegie][^johnson1925].

## Where it comes from

**The singers who carried it.** Fisk University in Nashville was deep in debt by 1871. On 6 October that year its treasurer, George L. White, left Nashville with a small company of student singers to raise money for it [^marsh1876][^fisk][^jubilee1872]. They sang spirituals, the religious songs of enslaved people, across the North and in Britain, where they sang before Queen Victoria [^marsh1876][^fisk]. J. B. T. Marsh's history of the group counts twenty-four singers who had belonged to the company by then: twenty, it says, had been enslaved, and three of the other four were children of enslaved parents [^marsh1876].

![[1]]

**The earliest known printing.** Deep River is not in the singers' first songbook, Jubilee Songs (1872), whose 24 songs include Go Down, Moses and Steal Away, nor in the two books about their tours of 1873 and 1875 [^jubilee1872][^pike1873][^pike1875]. It is No. 77, "Deep River", on pages 196 and 197 of Marsh's book in its second edition, London, 1875, whose note, dated 6 November 1875, says "a large number of new songs have been added" [^marsh1875]. The University of Pittsburgh's teaching guide also dates its first appearance to London, 1875 [^pitt]. The sixth edition of 1876 prints it on the same pages [^marsh1876].

**What the 1875 page shows.** The song is printed as a tune alone, on one staff, in E major and common time, with no tempo mark [^marsh1875]. Its refrain is the one sung today, but its last line, "Lord, I want to cross over into camp ground", comes four times, in short dotted notes, marked by turns very soft and loud. Three verses follow, each a couplet answered by that line, then back to the start ("D.C.") [^marsh1875].

![[2]]

**Coleridge-Taylor's slow setting.** In 1905 the London composer Samuel Coleridge-Taylor published Twenty-Four Negro Melodies for piano [^coleridgetaylor1905][^englishheritage]. His foreword thanks Frederick J. Loudin, manager of the Jubilee Singers, through whom he "first learned to appreciate the beautiful folk-music of my race". His No. 10, "Deep River", is marked "From 'Jubilee Songs'" and Lento, slow, with "camp ground" sung once, and a footnote calls it "the most beautiful and touching melody of the whole series" [^coleridgetaylor1905].

**Harry T. Burleigh.** Burleigh was born in Erie, Pennsylvania, in 1866 [^hymnarchive-burleigh][^hymnary-burleigh]. At the National Conservatory of Music in New York the composer Antonín Dvořák befriended him and, Carnegie Hall's history says, encouraged him to preserve the spiritual [^hymnary-burleigh][^carnegie]. On 20 December 1913 G. Schirmer registered his settings of Dig My Grave and Deep River for four-part mixed chorus [^cce1913]. The octavo we sing from reads "Copyright, 1914, by G. Schirmer" and is marked Lento, with a piano part "only for rehearsal" [^burleigh1914]. Burleigh gives the tune to the altos and sets one verse, the first of the three in Marsh's book [^burleigh1914][^marsh1875].

In 1916 G. Ricordi began to issue Burleigh's spirituals as songs for a trained voice with piano, written like art songs, registering his Deep River on 3 June, and it became the best known of them [^cce1916][^carnegie]. In 1925 James Weldon Johnson called Burleigh "the pioneer in making arrangements for the Spirituals that widened their appeal" to singers and the musical public [^johnson1925]. Burleigh died in Stamford, Connecticut, in 1949; his best-known arrangement, his obituary said, was Deep River [^hymnarchive-burleigh][^hymnary-burleigh].

## What the words mean

The river is the Jordan. In the Bible, Moses sees the promised land from across it but may not go over; after his death Joshua leads the Israelites across it on dry ground [^kjv]. Christian song made that crossing an image of death, with heaven on the far bank: the Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology reads the deep river as Jordan, "the traditional river of death", and the campground beyond it as a place of rest [^canterbury][^pitt].

![[4]]

- **The refrain.** "My home is over Jordan": home is not here but across the river, in heaven. The singer longs to "cross over into camp ground".
- **"Camp ground".** A camp meeting was an outdoor revival lasting several days, with people camping on the grounds [^tnencyc]. Theodore F. Seward, who wrote down the Fisk singers' music, said the spirituals were born "during some protracted meeting in church or camp" [^jubilee1872][^marsh1876]. The song's campground is that gathering place, made an image of heaven [^canterbury].
- **Verse 1.** The "gospel feast" is the feast of the gospel, like the great supper of Luke 14, to which all are invited [^kjv]. The "promis'd land" is Canaan, across the Jordan, and here heaven [^kjv].
- **Verse 2 (1875).** In heaven the singer will "take my seat" and "cast my crown at Jesus' feet", as the elders cast their crowns before the throne in Revelation 4:10 [^kjv].
- **Verse 3 (1875).** "There's nobody there for to turn me out": in heaven no one can send the singer away.

For the people who made these songs, the words could carry more. Frederick Douglass wrote in 1855 that when he and his friends sang of reaching Canaan while planning to escape, they meant more than heaven: "the north was our Canaan" [^douglass1855]. One teaching guide notes that a home "over Jordan" can also mean a lost home across the ocean, in Africa [^pitt]. No account of this song from the 1800s that we have read says how its first singers meant it; its printed verses are about heaven [^marsh1876].

### The full words

The refrain and verse 1 are as Burleigh's octavo prints them, the alto's words, with its spelling: it gives one verse [^burleigh1914]. Verses 2 and 3 are as printed in The Story of the Jubilee Singers (1875), where each verse is followed by "Lord, I want to cross over into camp ground", sung four times, and then the refrain [^marsh1875].

**Refrain**
Deep river, my home is over Jordan,
Deep river, Lord, I want to cross over into camp-ground.

**Verse 1**
Oh, don't you want to go to that gospel feast,
That promis'd land where all is peace?
Oh, deep river.

**Verse 2**
I'll go into heaven, and take my seat,
Cast my crown at Jesus' feet.

**Verse 3**
Oh, when I get to heav'n, I'll walk all about,
There's nobody there for to turn me out.

## How it is sung

A folk spiritual was sung by ear, in worship, by the people who made it, often a leader's line and everyone's answer [^johnson1925]. James Weldon Johnson remembered one such leader, "Singing" Johnson, who could "improvise lines on the moment" and was, "at camp meetings, revivals, and on special occasions", nearly as important as the preachers [^johnson1925]. A concert spiritual is arranged for trained singers on a stage, with set harmony or a piano part [^carnegie]. Deep River is best known now as a concert piece: slow, sustained and quiet, as Coleridge-Taylor and Burleigh marked it [^coleridgetaylor1905][^burleigh1914].

Our arrangement is Burleigh's own four parts of 1914, in E-flat as he wrote them, very slow and smooth, for 4 singers, without the rehearsal piano [^burleigh1914]. The alto sings the refrain twice, the soprano joining the second time; then everyone sings the verse, ending softly on "Oh, deep river". Where Burleigh's tenors or basses divide we sing one of his lines, and two low bass notes go up an octave.

## Versions and other words

- **For solo voice, 1916.** Burleigh's setting for voice and piano; its 1917 copies, in D-flat and in F, are dedicated to Miss Mary Jordan [^cce1916][^burleigh1917][^imslp-burleigh].
- **On record, 1923.** The contralto Marian Anderson recorded Burleigh's setting for Victor on 10 December 1923 [^anderson1923][^imslp-burleigh].

## Did you know?

- Coleridge-Taylor marked his piece 'From "Jubilee Songs"', and the copyright entry for Burleigh's chorus setting says its words are "from Jubilee songs": the heading under which Marsh's book printed the Fisk singers' songs, Deep River among them, not their 1872 songbook of that name, which lacks it [^coleridgetaylor1905][^cce1913][^marsh1875][^jubilee1872]. Burleigh later gave his own series of spirituals the name Jubilee Songs of the United States of America [^burleigh1914][^carnegie].
- In 1925 James Weldon Johnson named Deep River as one of the spirituals whose form grows "almost purely choral" [^johnson1925].

## Singing it

- [Alto](/songs/deep-river/alto): you carry the tune, twice through the refrain. Its big moment is the octave leap from "is" up to your top E-flat on "over" in "my home is over Jordan"; take it lightly. In the verse you share the tune with the soprano up to "gospel feast".
- [Soprano](/songs/deep-river/soprano): you rest through the first refrain, then float the refrain above the alto, mostly on long notes. In the verse you sing the tune with the alto, in unison, through "gospel feast", then take the top line. Your top G comes on "over" in your refrain and on "promised".
- [Tenor](/songs/deep-river/tenor): you sing the words in the middle of Burleigh's chords. Under "Jordan, Deep, deep river" in the first refrain you step down D, D-flat, C: tune those half steps carefully. In the verse you sing "Want to go" while the soprano and alto sing "Oh, don't you want to go", and your one high G comes on "all" in "where all is peace?".
- [Bass](/songs/deep-river/bass): you sing Burleigh's bass line, on E-flat nearly a third of the time, down to a low F on "over" in "cross over" in the first refrain. At the close you move B-flat, B natural, C, B-flat, A-flat, B-flat, E-flat under "is peace? Oh, deep river".

## Questions

### What does the song Deep River mean?

It is about crossing the river Jordan to a home in heaven. The Jordan is the river the Israelites crossed into the promised land, and Christian song made it an image of death [^kjv][^canterbury]. The verses picture heaven as a feast, a seat and a crown [^marsh1876].

### What does campground mean in Deep River?

A campground was where a camp meeting was held: an outdoor revival lasting several days, with people camping on the grounds; the Tennessee Encyclopedia traces such meetings on the southern frontier to 1800 [^tnencyc]. Spirituals were sung and made at such meetings [^johnson1925][^marsh1876]. In the song, crossing "into camp ground" means reaching heaven, a place of rest on the far side of the Jordan [^canterbury].

### Who wrote Deep River?

No one knows: it is a spiritual made by enslaved African Americans, and no maker's name survives. Its earliest known printing is in The Story of the Jubilee Singers (London, 1875), the book of Fisk University's student singers [^marsh1875][^pitt]. Harry T. Burleigh's setting for chorus (registered in 1913, printed in 1914) and his solo setting (1916) made it a concert song [^cce1913][^burleigh1914][^cce1916].

### Why is the river Jordan important?

In the Bible the Israelites cross it into the promised land; the book of Joshua says it overflowed its banks at harvest, yet they crossed on dry ground [^kjv]. It is not a wide river: an American naval party that rowed down it from the Sea of Galilee in April 1848 logged it at 25 to 90 yards wide and 2 to 11 feet deep, and a 19th-century Bible encyclopedia, drawing on that survey, gives 80 to 150 feet wide and 5 to 12 feet deep [^lynch1852][^mcclintock]. In hymns and spirituals it stands for death, with heaven on the other side, and the promised land beyond it could also mean freedom: Frederick Douglass wrote that for him and his friends "the north was our Canaan" [^canterbury][^pitt][^douglass1855].

### Which part sings the tune?

The alto, twice through the refrain, as Burleigh wrote it; the soprano waits eight bars, then floats a line above on long notes. In the verse soprano and alto share the tune until the soprano climbs alone to the high note on "promised" [^burleigh1914].
