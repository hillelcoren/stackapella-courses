Shenandoah, also sung as Oh Shenandoah or The Wide Missouri, is a traditional song of longing. The singer calls to Shenandoah, a river or a person, the chorus answers "Away, you rolling river", and the song sets off "'cross the wide Missouri" [^terry][^colcord]. No one knows who made it [^locsong][^whall]. Its earliest known printing, in a magazine for young people of April 1868, has a sailor called Dave lead it at the capstan as "Shannydore" [^riverside][^schreffler]. Sailors sang it walking the capstan round to raise the anchor, and it became a favourite there [^terry][^colcord][^winick].

## Where it comes from

**At the capstan, 1868.** In April 1868 The Riverside Magazine for Young People printed an unsigned article called "Sailors' Songs" [^riverside][^schreffler]. As the ship sets out, the order comes to man the capstan bars and give a song, and "old Dave", the chanty-man, starts up "Shannydore" [^riverside]. A footnote explains the word: Shenandoah. Another says the songs were "taken from the lips of a veritable 'old Dave' during the writer's recent voyage across the Atlantic" [^riverside]. This oldest text has no chief, no daughter and no Missouri: where later singers cross the wide Missouri, Dave's crew sings of the Atlantic [^riverside][^schreffler]. Much of today's song is already there, though: "O Shannydore, I long to hear you!", "Away, you rollin' river!" and "Ah ha! I'm bound away", and one verse has the singer courting Sally for seven years [^riverside].

![[1]]

**In the Arctic, 1876.** Januarius MacGahan, a correspondent for the New York Herald, wrote of his Arctic voyage in Under the Northern Lights (London, 1876) [^macgahan][^schreffler]. In his chapter "A Capstan Song", the crew weighs anchor to leave Beechey Island, and a petty officer and old whaler named Randerson sets up a song with a refrain at the end of every line, which all hands join. MacGahan calls its tune full of "a wild, sweet melody", and the song is "Shanadoa" [^macgahan].

**In Montreal, 1876.** In April the same year The New Dominion Monthly of Montreal printed "Sailors' Songs" by a sea captain, R. C. Adams, and on its page 262 "Shanadore" appears with its tune, a single line in 6/8 with the chorus marked [^ndm]. Here the Missouri arrives: the chorus answers "Hurrah, you rolling river" and "Ah hah, I'm bounding away o'er the wild Missouri", and one verse is already "Shanadore I long to hear you" [^ndm]. Adams thought the name "probably means Shenandoah, a river in Virginia", and said the song went on "according to the ingenuity of the impromptu composer" [^ndm].

**What the writers made of it.** In July 1882 William L. Alden printed a "Shanandore" in his article "Sailor Songs" in Harper's New Monthly Magazine [^alden][^winick][^locsong]. Like Adams, he took the name to be the Shenandoah River, and he believed the song came from Black singers [^alden][^schreffler]. The Library of Congress, on its Song of America site and its folklife blog, calls Alden's article the song's first appearance in print, but the printings of 1868 and 1876 are older [^locsong][^winick][^riverside][^macgahan][^ndm]. W. B. Whall, a master mariner, gave a different account in Ships, Sea Songs and Shanties. He wrote that it began as a song, not a shanty, with nothing to do with the sea, and that it probably came from the voyageurs, the American and Canadian boatmen of the fur trade, whom he called great singers [^whall][^colcord]. His verses tell of a white trader who loves the daughter of Shenandoah, a Native American chief. The chief turns down the trader's money, and in the end a Yankee skipper sells the chief liquor and steals the daughter across the river [^whall]. They are told in words about Native Americans that read as crude and offensive today, and we don't print them. Joanna Colcord, who collected the songs of American sailors, called Whall's the earlier version and the one sung on American ships after the Civil War a later, worn-down form [^colcord]. Colcord, retelling it, has the trader carry her off [^colcord].

**The version we sing.** In 1921 the English church musician and collector Richard Runciman Terry put "Shenandoah" ninth in The Shanty Book, Part I, among the windlass and capstan shanties [^terry][^hymnology]. He had it from a Captain Robertson, and said it was almost, but not quite, the version he had learned as a boy [^terry]. Our tune is his, note for note.

![[2]]

**Myth and record.** Often told: Shenandoah was a famous chief, the song is about his daughter, and the name Shenandoah means "Daughter of the Stars". What the record shows: the 1868 text names no chief at all [^riverside][^schreffler]. Whall and Terry each call Shenandoah a celebrated chief, in the words of their day, but don't say which one [^whall][^terry]. A real chief of a similar name, Skenandoah of the Oneida Nation, a friend of the missionary Samuel Kirkland, who brought the Oneidas to the American side in the Revolution, died in 1816 and lies buried beside Kirkland at Hamilton College in New York State, far from the Missouri [^npsskenandoah][^hamilton]. No early source ties him to the song. As for the name, its meaning is disputed: "Daughter of the Stars" is only the most popular reading, and the National Park Service says there is no more evidence for its "Beautiful Daughter of the Stars" than for any other theory [^nps][^ewv].

### Timeline

- 1868: "Shannydore", led by "old Dave" at the capstan, is printed in The Riverside Magazine for Young People, in April [^riverside][^schreffler]
- 1876: MacGahan's Under the Northern Lights has the crew singing "Shanadoa" as they weigh anchor at Beechey Island [^macgahan][^schreffler]
- 1876: Capt. R. C. Adams's "Sailors' Songs" in The New Dominion Monthly of Montreal, in April, prints "Shanadore" with its tune and "the wild Missouri" [^ndm]

- 1921: Terry prints it as No. 9 of The Shanty Book, Part I, a windlass and capstan shanty [^terry]

## What the words mean

We sing the first verse of Terry's 1921 text, with one change: his last line is "Away, I'm bound to go 'Cross the wide Missouri", and we sing "I'm bound away", as Whall printed it [^terry][^whall].

**"Oh Shenandoah, I long to hear you."** The singer is far away and misses Shenandoah, a river or a person; the old sources never agree which [^riverside][^alden][^whall][^terry]. To "hear" a river is to be near enough to hear its water.

**"Away, you rolling river."** This is the crew's chorus, answering the leader's line [^locsong][^terry]. A rolling river is a big one in full flow.

**"Away, I'm bound away, 'cross the wide Missouri."** "Bound" means headed for, or obliged to go: the singer is leaving, across the Missouri River.

![[3]]

The later verses in Terry turn to love and parting. "I love your daughter" is a trace of the trader's story; Whall wrote that the seamen of his day knew nothing of the old song but its tune and that one line [^terry][^whall]. "'Tis seven long years since last I see thee" means it has been seven years since I last saw you; "see" for "saw" is old, everyday speech. "I took a notion" means I made up my mind, here to go to sea. "I'm bound to leave you" means I have to go, and "I'll not deceive you" is a promise to be true.

### The full words

These are the six verses of Terry's The Shanty Book, Part I (1921) [^terry]. Terry prints the chorus only under the first verse and marks the one-line verses "(twice)"; here the chorus is written out in each verse, as it is sung.

**Verse 1**
Oh Shenandoah, I long to hear you.
Away you rolling river.
Oh Shenandoah, I long to hear you.
Away, I'm bound to go
'Cross the wide Missouri.

**Verse 2**
Oh Shenandoah, I love your daughter.
Away you rolling river.
Oh Shenandoah, I love your daughter.
Away, I'm bound to go
'Cross the wide Missouri.

**Verse 3**
'Tis seven long years since last I see thee.
Away you rolling river.
'Tis seven long years since last I see thee.
Away, I'm bound to go
'Cross the wide Missouri.

**Verse 4**
Oh Shenandoah, I took a notion
Away you rolling river.
To sail across the stormy ocean.
Away, I'm bound to go
'Cross the wide Missouri.

**Verse 5**
Oh Shenandoah, I'm bound to leave you.
Away you rolling river.
Oh Shenandoah, I'll not deceive you.
Away, I'm bound to go
'Cross the wide Missouri.

**Verse 6**
Oh Shenandoah, I long to hear you.
Away you rolling river.
Oh Shenandoah, I long to hear you.
Away, I'm bound to go
'Cross the wide Missouri.

## How it is sung

At sea it was a capstan shanty. The capstan was a big upright drum on deck: the men pushed on bars set into it and walked round in a circle, winding in the cable to lift the anchor or to warp the ship in to a wharf [^winick][^terry]. A shantyman sang each line alone and the crew answered with the chorus [^locsong][^riverside]. Black sailors and singers were at the heart of the shanty tradition, and crews often prized a Black shantyman above any other singer [^winick][^colcord]. Terry wrote that the tune was always sung with great feeling and in a very free rhythm [^terry]. The songs faded from the sea as steam replaced sail [^terry][^colcord].

Our arrangement sings Terry's first verse, slow and spacious, for 2 to 4 singers. Terry printed it in E-flat in a free mix of bars; we sing it in F, with his notes and syllables kept and his rhythm evened into four beats a bar, the held notes on "you", "wide" and "sou-" kept long [^terry]. The soprano sings the tune, the alto and tenor sing the words beneath it, and the bass holds the low notes on "oo". The harmony is ours.

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/shenandoah/soprano): you carry the long, rolling tune. You start low, on middle C for "Oh", and leap up to F for "Shenandoah". Your highest note comes early, the top F on the first syllable of "Away, you rolling river", falling through E to D. Save breath for the last line: "wide" is held for three beats.
- [Alto](/songs/shenandoah/alto): you come in on "Shen-", just after the tune's first note, on a low A. On "I long to hear you" you climb a whole octave, from A up to A, then sing mostly a third under the tune. In the third line you walk back down, and you end on that low A under the tune's last F.
- [Tenor](/songs/shenandoah/tenor): you sing the words on just four notes, F, A, B-flat and C, and mostly on C. You begin and end on a low F, and on "Away, I'm bound" you sing C four times running, a calm middle line.
- [Bass](/songs/shenandoah/bass): you hold the bass line on "oo", long low notes on F and B-flat for most of the verse. In the last line you move: D under "wide", then G and C under "Missouri", and down to your lowest note, a low F, on the very last syllable.

## Questions

### What does Shenandoah mean?

In the song, Shenandoah is someone or something the singer longs for, either the river or a man named Shenandoah. The 1868 text glosses it simply as Shenandoah, R. C. Adams in 1876 and Alden in 1882 took it for the river, and Whall and Terry called Shenandoah a chief [^riverside][^ndm][^alden][^whall][^terry]. The name itself is of unknown origin, though generally believed to be Native American, and its meaning is disputed; the popular "Daughter of the Stars" has no more evidence behind it than any other theory [^nps][^ewv].

### Is Shenandoah a sea shanty?

Yes, in use: sailors sang it at the capstan while raising the anchor, and Terry and Colcord both file it among the windlass and capstan shanties [^terry][^colcord]. Two of its oldest printings, from 1868 and 1876, show it sung at the capstan [^riverside][^macgahan]. But Whall thought it began on land as a song, perhaps among the voyageurs, the fur trade's river men, before sailors took it up [^whall][^colcord].

### What is the Shenandoah song about?

It is about leaving, and longing for what is left behind. The verse we sing calls to Shenandoah and sets off across the wide Missouri [^terry]. In Whall's version there is a story: a trader falls in love with the daughter of Shenandoah, a chief, who turns him away, and a Yankee skipper gets the chief drunk and steals her across the river [^whall].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune all the way through. With two singers, the soprano and alto sing; three add the bass, and four the tenor. The alto and tenor sing the words beneath the tune, and the bass sings "oo". Terry's book gives the tune; the harmony is ours [^terry].
