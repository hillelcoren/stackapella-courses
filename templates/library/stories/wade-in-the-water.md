Wade in the Water is an African American spiritual, a religious folk song of the kind made by enslaved and freed Black worshippers [^carnegiefolk][^work1907]. Its chorus calls, "Wade in the water, children", because "God's a-going to trouble the water", a line read from John's Gospel, where an angel troubles the healing pool of Bethesda [^work1904][^kjv][^hawn]. Its earliest printing we have read is in the 1904 second edition of New Jubilee Songs, the songbook of Fisk University in Nashville, where a note calls it a favorite melody "while a baptism is in progress" [^work1904]. It is still sung in church: the Methodist songbook The Faith We Sing prints it as No. 2107, filed under baptism [^hymnaryfws][^hawn].

## Where it comes from

**Fisk's new book.** Fisk University's Jubilee Singers had made spirituals famous on the concert stage since 1871 [^work1907][^carnegie]. The preface of their new book, signed in Nashville in June 1902 by a Committee on Publication, says that in the more than eighteen years since the last edition of their old songbook, by Theodore F. Seward and George L. White, "many new and beautiful songs from various localities and sources" had been collected, most of them harmonized, and many collected, by Frederick J. Work, a Fisk student "of the class of 1903" [^work1904]. Fisk University registered the first edition for copyright on 19 June 1902, as "New jubilee songs; collected and harmonized by Frederick J. Work" [^cce1902]. Some accounts date it 1901 [^maymachunda][^songofamerica], and one says Wade in the Water was already in it [^songofamerica]; the copyright record, the book's title page and two hymnology references all give 1902, and the copyright catalogues of 1901 have no entry for it [^cce1902][^work1904][^canterburyfjw][^hymnologyarchive]. We have not seen a copy of the first edition, so the 1904 page is the earliest we can describe.

![[1]]

**What the 1904 page shows.** "WADE IN THE WATER" starts at the foot of page 8, under a note in parentheses: "(A favorite melody among Southern Baptists while a baptism is in progress.)" [^work1904]. It is in E minor and 2/4 time, and though the preface says most of the book's songs were harmonized, this one is printed as a bare tune on a single staff [^work1904]. The chorus takes the foot of page 8; page 9, headed "WADE IN THE WATER, Concluded", stacks four verses under the two lines of the verse tune [^work1904]. The title page names only Frederick J. Work, as "Collected and Harmonized by" [^work1904][^canterburyjww].

![[2]]

**The Work brothers.** In 1907 the same pages came out again, line for line, in Folk Songs of the American Negro, Number Two, edited by Frederick J. Work [^work1907][^hymnologyarchive]. His brother John Wesley Work II, then leading the Fisk Jubilee Singers, wrote its introduction, dated 15 May 1907 [^work1907][^canterburyjww][^worknum1rev]. "These songs are still being hunted and found", he wrote, and "They are sources of great joy as well as sources of history" [^work1907]. John W. Work II died in 1925 and Frederick J. Work in 1942 [^canterburyjww][^canterburyfjw][^dlg][^admp].

**Earlier books.** We did not find the song in Slave Songs of the United States (1867), made by three white collectors, the Fisk singers' books of the 1870s or the 1903 edition of their story [^slavesongs1867][^pike1875][^marsh1903].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Wade in the Water was a code song of the Underground Railroad, telling people escaping slavery to take to the water so that the dogs following them would lose the scent, and Harriet Tubman sang it as a signal [^songofamerica][^bordewich][^nps]. What the record shows: no source from slavery times connects this song with escape or with Tubman, and its earliest known printing came decades after the Civil War [^work1904][^nps]. A historian of the Underground Railroad names it among the supposed "map" songs and finds no evidence that any song was used that way, and a Tubman fact sheet from Maryland's Harriet Tubman Underground Railroad Byway, posted on the National Park Service's site, lists it as a myth [^bordewich][^nps]. Tubman did sing signals. Her biographer Sarah Bradford printed in 1869 the words of a hymn, "Hail, oh hail ye happy spirits", that Tubman sang as she came back to fugitives hiding in the woods: the first time she passed they stayed hidden, and when she sang it again they came out; lines of a Moses song, "Moses go down in Egypt", meant danger [^bradford1869]. Bradford's 1886 book tells the signal a little differently, with "Oh go down, Moses" sung so that they are certain "it is their leader who is coming" [^bradford1886]. Neither book mentions this song. The fact sheet names Tubman's two songs as "Go Down Moses" and "Bound For the Promised Land" and says she changed their tempo; Bradford's books do print both, but the promised-land song is the farewell she sang when she left the plantation to escape, not a rescue signal, and the signal they describe is which song she sang, not how fast [^nps][^bradford1869][^bradford1886].

A double meaning in such songs is documented. Of that farewell, Bradford wrote that "the uninitiated knew not the hidden meaning of the words", which "meant something more than a journey to the Heavenly Canaan" [^bradford1869]. Frederick Douglass wrote in 1855 that when he and his friends sang of Canaan, "the north was our Canaan" [^douglass1855]. A singer could hear freedom in Jordan's stream too; that this song was a code has no evidence behind it.

### Timeline

- 1869: Sarah Bradford prints Harriet Tubman's own account of the hymns she sang as signals; Wade in the Water is not among them [^bradford1869]
- 1902: Fisk University registers New Jubilee Songs, collected and harmonized by Frederick J. Work, for copyright on 19 June; some accounts date it 1901 [^cce1902][^work1904][^songofamerica]
- 1904: The second edition prints "Wade in the Water" on pages 8 and 9 as a bare tune for baptisms, the earliest printing we have read [^work1904]
- 1907: Folk Songs of the American Negro, Number Two, reprints the same pages, with John W. Work II's introduction [^work1907]
- 1925: Harry T. Burleigh's arrangement for voice and piano, Wade in de Water, is copyrighted by G. Ricordi [^burleigh1925][^cce1925]
- 1926: The Second Book of Negro Spirituals prints it as "God's A-Gwineter Trouble De Water" [^johnson1926]

## What the words mean

- **The chorus.** "God's a-going to trouble the water" means God is going to stir it. In John's Gospel, sick people lay by the pool of Bethesda, because "an angel went down at a certain season into the pool, and troubled the water", and the first to step in after was healed [^kjv]. A Methodist hymnologist reads the chorus from that story, and The Faith We Sing lists John 5:2-9 as its scripture [^hawn][^hymnaryfws].
- **Verse 1.** "See that band all dressed in white": a band is a company of people. In the Book of Revelation a great multitude stands before the throne "clothed with white robes" [^kjv]. "The leader looks like the Israelite": one of the people of Israel.
- **Verse 2.** A band "dressed in red" looks "like the band that Moses led", the Israelites Moses led out of Egypt through the sea on dry ground [^kjv]. The hymnologist reads the first two verses as the Red Sea crossing [^hawn].
- **Verse 3.** "Look over yonder what do I see? The Holy Ghost a-coming on me": the Spirit of God comes down on the singer. The verse names no scripture; Isaiah 61, "The Spirit of the Lord God is upon me", is one suggestion [^hawn][^kjv].
- **Verse 4.** "You don't believe I've been redeemed?": to be redeemed is to be saved, bought back. "Just follow me down to Jordan's stream": the Jordan is the river the Israelites crossed into the promised land, and where Jesus came to John to be baptized [^kjv][^hawn].

### The full words

These are the words as New Jubilee Songs (1904) prints them on pages 8 and 9, with its spelling and punctuation; the page prints "a going" without a hyphen once, in verse 2 [^work1904].

![[4]]

**Chorus**
Wade in the water, Wade in the water, children,
Wade in the water, God's a-going to trouble the water.

**Verse 1**
See that band all dressed in white, God's a-going to trouble the water,
The leader looks like the Israelite, God's a-going to trouble the water.

**Verse 2**
See that band all dressed in red, God's a going to trouble the water,
Looks like the band that Moses led, God's a-going to trouble the water.

**Verse 3**
Look over yonder what do I see? God's a-going to trouble the water;
The Holy Ghost a-coming on me, God's a-going to trouble the water.

**Verse 4**
You don't believe I've been redeemed? God's a-going to trouble the water,
Just follow me down to Jordan's stream, God's a-going to trouble the water.

## How it is sung

A folk spiritual was made up by Black worshippers in their own services, with a leader's line, the group's answer and repeated choruses; a concert spiritual is arranged for the stage [^carnegiefolk][^carnegie]. Wade in the Water has the folk shape: each verse line is a call, and the answer is always the same, "God's a-going to trouble the water" [^work1904].

Our arrangement takes its melody and words from the 1904 page, moved up a half step from E minor to F minor, in the book's 2/4 time; the harmony is our own [^work1904]. We sing the chorus, verse 1, the chorus, verse 2 and the chorus again. In the verses the soprano sings each call alone while the alto and tenor hum on "oo", and everyone joins on the answer. Under it all the bass keeps a steady "doom, ba-doom". Two singers are soprano and alto; three add the bass, four the tenor.

## Versions and other words

- **Burleigh, 1925.** The Black composer Harry T. Burleigh set it for voice and piano as Wade in de Water, published by G. Ricordi [^burleigh1925][^cce1925][^carnegie].
- **The Johnsons, 1926.** The Second Book of Negro Spirituals, edited by James Weldon Johnson with arrangements by J. Rosamond Johnson, prints it as "God's A-Gwineter Trouble De Water". It has two verses, the first beginning "See dat host all dressed in white" [^johnson1926].

## Did you know?

- Tubman did lead people through water. Thomas Garrett, the Wilmington Quaker, wrote in 1868 that one March, with two men, she came to a stream of tide water with no boat or bridge, said God told her to go through, and went in up to her armpits; the men followed only when they saw her safe on the far side [^bradford1869][^bradford1886].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/wade-in-the-water/soprano): you carry the tune and sing the calls of each verse alone; everyone joins you on the answers. The chorus's second phrase, "Wade in the water, children", climbs to your high F on "wa-" and falls straight back down; take the top gently. "God's a-going to trouble the water" dips to your low C on "the".
- [Alto](/songs/wade-in-the-water/alto): you sing the chorus, then hum on "oo" under each verse call, then join every answer, often a sixth below the tune, mostly on a low A-flat.
- [Tenor](/songs/wade-in-the-water/tenor): you sing mostly on one note, F, humming under the verse calls and joining every answer. Your highest note is C, on "in the wa-" in "Wade in the water, children", and on the very last "water" you drop to a low C.
- [Bass](/songs/wade-in-the-water/bass): you keep a steady "doom, ba-doom" under everything, a quick low line mostly on F, C and A-flat, and end on a held low F.

## Questions

### What does "God's gonna trouble the water" mean?

It means God is going to stir the water, as an angel "troubled the water" of the healing pool of Bethesda in John's Gospel [^kjv][^hawn]. The Faith We Sing lists John 5:2-9 as its scripture and files it under baptism, and Fisk's 1904 book calls it a baptism melody, so the troubled water is also the water of baptism [^hymnaryfws][^work1904].

### Did Harriet Tubman write or sing Wade in the Water?

There is no evidence that she did. The song's earliest known printing is from 1904, and no account from Tubman's lifetime connects her with it [^work1904][^nps]. The signal songs in her biographer Sarah Bradford's books of 1869 and 1886 are a hymn of "happy spirits" and a song of Moses [^bradford1869][^bradford1886]. A Tubman fact sheet posted by the National Park Service calls the Wade in the Water story a myth [^nps].

### Who wrote Wade in the Water?

No one knows. It is a traditional spiritual, and the earliest printing we have read, Fisk University's New Jubilee Songs of 1904, names no maker; Frederick J. Work is credited with collecting and harmonizing the book's songs, not writing them [^work1904]. One account says it was printed in 1901, in that book's first edition, but the book and its copyright record date that edition to June 1902 [^songofamerica][^work1904][^cce1902].

### Is Wade in the Water public domain?

Yes, the traditional song is. No maker is known, and its words and tune were printed by Fisk University in New Jubilee Songs, whose first edition was registered for copyright in June 1902; we read them in the edition of 1904 [^work1904][^cce1902]. Later arrangements are separate works with their own rights, Harry T. Burleigh's, copyrighted in 1925, among them, and so are recordings [^cce1925]. Our arrangement is our own.

### Is Wade in the Water gospel, blues or jazz?

It is a spiritual: a religious folk song in call and response, which Fisk's book of 1904 calls a melody for baptisms [^work1904][^carnegiefolk]. Spirituals grew into the concert spiritual and fed gospel, and Burleigh set this one for the concert stage in 1925 [^songofamerica][^burleigh1925]. It did not begin as blues or jazz, but jazz took it up: a recording by the jazz pianist Ramsey Lewis (1935 to 2022) was on Billboard's Hot 100 in September 1966 [^billboard1966][^relix2022].
