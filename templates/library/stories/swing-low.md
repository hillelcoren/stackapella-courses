Swing Low, Sweet Chariot is a spiritual, one of the religious songs made by enslaved African Americans. Its chorus asks for a chariot to come down low and carry the singer home, a picture taken from the Bible's story of the prophet Elijah [^kjv][^psh]. Students from Fisk University in Nashville sang it on the concert tours they began in October 1871, and it was printed in their book, Jubilee Songs, in 1872 [^seward1872][^balladindex][^marsh][^okhist]. A tradition credits it to Wallace Willis, an enslaved man in the Choctaw Nation, in what is now Oklahoma [^okhist][^oksenate]. Hymnals still print it, and English rugby crowds have sung it since the 1980s [^hymnary-text][^psh][^rte][^espn].

## Where it comes from

**The Fisk singers.** On 6 October 1871 a small group of Fisk students left Nashville under George L. White, the university's treasurer, to give concerts and raise money for their school [^marsh][^pbs][^tnenc]. In 1872 Biglow & Main of New York published Jubilee Songs: twenty-four songs, the words taken down from the singers by White and the music by Theodore F. Seward [^seward1872][^adsl]. Seward's preface says the music came from the singers' own singing, over repeated sessions, and nothing went in without their approval [^seward1872]. "Swing low, sweet chariot" is on page 6, with four verses, the earliest known printing of the song with its music [^seward1872][^balladindex]. The page sets it in F major and 2/4 time, in four parts on two staves. Under "Swing low, sweet chariot" and each verse's opening line the lower staff is silent, and every part comes in on "Coming for to carry me home": call and response, printed on the page [^seward1872]. His preface (p. 3) singles the song out. The turn on the last syllable of "chariot" in its first line, he writes, recurs so often in the book that it gives a false sense of sameness. More than half the melodies, he adds, use a scale without the fourth and seventh notes, as this one does [^seward1872].

**An earlier mention.** The song's name was in print the year before the book. In October 1871 the New York magazine The Galaxy printed an essay by Edward A. Pollard that names "the hymn of 'Swing low, Chariot'" in passing, by title only, with no words or music [^galaxy][^adsl].

**The Willis tradition.** Wallace and Minerva Willis were enslaved by Britt Willis, a Choctaw slaveholder [^okhist][^oksenate]. Before the Civil War they were hired out to Spencer Academy, a Choctaw boarding school for boys, whose superintendent, the Reverend Alexander Reid, came to love their singing [^okhist][^oksenate]. In 1871, the story goes, Reid heard the Fisk singers, told White of the songs he remembered from Spencer, wrote down their words and taught them to the singers, Swing Low among them [^okhist][^oksenate]. The tellers differ: the Oklahoma Historical Society's encyclopedia puts that concert in Newark, New Jersey, and the Oklahoma Senate's account in Nashville. The Senate calls Minerva Wallace's wife; the encyclopedia reports newer family research finding that she was his daughter [^okhist][^oksenate].

**The record beside it.** The 1872 book names no maker for this or any song [^seward1872]. The Willis credit is in print later. The encyclopedia cites Reid's own notice of Wallace's death in The Fisk Herald in 1884, and two folklorists, citing an 1890 article in The Presbyterian, say the Willises taught the singers themselves [^okhist][^balladindex]. When the song was made is not known. The encyclopedia says Wallace made his songs in the mid-1800s, while working in the fields [^okhist]. The Senate's account puts this one before 1862 [^oksenate]. A Tubman biographer writes that it came after the Civil War [^larson]. That date clashes with Reid's years at Spencer, 1849 to about 1861 [^okhist].

**Another origin story.** John Wesley Work, a Fisk professor, told a different one in Folk Song of the American Negro (1915) [^work1915][^pdr]. In his telling, Sarah Hannah Sheppard went in despair to the river meaning to drown herself and her baby daughter, and an old woman stopped her, telling her to wait for the Lord's chariot to swing low. That baby, Work writes, was Ella Sheppard, later one of the first Jubilee Singers [^work1915][^pdr].

**Myth and record.** Often told: the song was a signal on the Underground Railroad, the chariot a guide, the Jordan the Ohio River, and it was Harriet Tubman's song. Hymnary.org's note on the text reads the river as the free states beyond the Ohio [^hymnary-text]. What the record shows: a historian of the Underground Railroad, writing on its myths for Dickinson College's online handbook, lists Swing Low among songs alleged to have told people escaping that a guide was near, and finds no evidence that this or the other so-called code songs were used that way [^bordewich]. A Tubman biographer, writing about Tubman herself, calls the Tubman claim a myth: the songs in Sarah Bradford's life of Tubman are "Go Down Moses" and "Bound for the Promised Land". There Tubman sings those two to people in hiding as a signal, slower or faster as it was safe or not [^larson]. That is a singer choosing how to sing, not a code in the words, so the two historians do not clash. A double meaning in spirituals is documented, though. Frederick Douglass, who escaped slavery, wrote in 1855 that when he and his friends sang of Canaan they meant more than heaven: "the north was our Canaan" [^douglass1855]. That a singer could hear freedom in this song too is possible; that it was a coded message has no evidence behind it.

## What the words mean

**The chorus.** "Swing low, sweet chariot": come down low, come close enough to reach me. The chariot is Elijah's. In the Second Book of Kings (2 Kings 2:11), as Elijah and his follower Elisha walk on together, "there appeared a chariot of fire, and horses of fire", and Elijah goes up "by a whirlwind into heaven" [^kjv][^psh]. The chariot and horses of fire part the two men, and a whirlwind takes Elijah up [^kjv]. In "Coming for to carry me home", "for to" is an old way of saying "to". Home is heaven. The song welcomes death as the moment of being carried home [^psh].

**Verse 1, the one we sing.** "I looked over Jordan, and what did I see": I looked across the river. The Jordan is the river the Israelites crossed on dry ground into the promised land, and the river Elijah and Elisha crossed, again on dry ground, just before the chariot appeared [^kjv][^psh]. Over the river the singer sees "a band of angels coming after me": a company of angels coming to fetch me.

**The other verses.** Jubilee Songs prints three more verses: "If you get there before I do", a message to friends to say the singer is coming too; "the brightest day", the day the singer's sins were washed away; and being sometimes up and sometimes down but still bound for heaven [^seward1872]. The 1872 book spells the refrain's word "Coming", as we sing it [^seward1872].

### The full words

Jubilee Songs prints the refrain and four verses on page 6: verses 1 and 2 under the music, verses 3 and 4 in full below it, and the refrain is sung again after each verse [^seward1872]. The spelling is the book's.

**Refrain**
Swing low, sweet chariot,
Coming for to carry me home,
Swing low, sweet chariot,
Coming for to carry me home.

**Verse 1**
I looked over Jordan, and what did I see,
Coming for to carry me home?
A band of angels coming after me,
Coming for to carry me home.

**Verse 2**
If you get there before I do,
Coming for to carry me home,
Tell all my friends I'm coming too,
Coming for to carry me home.

**Verse 3**
The brightest day that ever I saw,
Coming for to carry me home,
When Jesus washed my sins away,
Coming for to carry me home.

**Verse 4**
I'm sometimes up and sometimes down,
Coming for to carry me home,
But still my soul feels heavenly bound,
Coming for to carry me home.

## How it is sung

It began as a folk spiritual, sung by enslaved people among themselves [^carnegie]. The Fisk singers took such songs to the concert stage in arranged harmony, the start of the concert spiritual, and singers from Hampton Institute and other Black colleges followed [^carnegie][^psh]. The Psalter Hymnal Handbook describes its tune, SWING LOW, as pentatonic and built as call and response: a soloist or a few voices sing the calls and everyone answers in harmony. It suggests singing it unaccompanied and slowly [^psh].

Our arrangement is the chorus, the first verse and the chorus again, in G, a tone above the 1872 page's F, warm and unhurried [^seward1872]. Its melody was checked against a modern songbook's transcription, not read from the 1872 page. It is for 2 to 4 singers. The soprano sings the tune; with two singers the alto joins on the words beneath it; with three the bass adds the low notes on "oo"; with four the tenor fills in the middle on the words.

## Versions and other words

The Hampton Institute singers had their own version, printed in Hampton and Its Students (1874) on page 179 among fifty "cabin and plantation songs" arranged by Thomas P. Fenner [^hampton1874].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/swing-low/soprano): you carry the tune, built on just five notes and centred on G. The second "Swing low" of each chorus starts on your top note, a high E, and so does "band" in the verse; then the line falls back to the low D. Hold the first "home" for its full length on the high D, and let the second one settle onto G.
- [Alto](/songs/swing-low/alto): you sing the words beneath the tune, often a sixth below it. "I looked over Jordan, and" stays on B the whole way, so keep it steady and light. "Coming for to carry me home" brings your two notes outside the tune's five: C on "to", then F sharp near the end of the line. Solo that line in the player until both are sure.
- [Tenor](/songs/swing-low/tenor): you sing the words mostly on G and B, swapping between them. The first "Swing low, sweet chariot" is all on G. You step up to C on the second "Swing low" of each chorus and in the verse on "what did I" and "coming after". Your very last "home" rises to D, your highest note, to end the song.
- [Bass](/songs/swing-low/bass): you hold long low notes on "oo" that keep coming back to G. The one place you walk is under "A band of angels": a new note on every beat, G, C, G, B, E, then C. In the last line of the verse and of the song you climb to your top G under "Coming" and step back down to the low G.

## Questions

### Was Swing Low, Sweet Chariot an Underground Railroad song?

There is no evidence that it was. The ideas that it signalled a guide was near, or was Harriet Tubman's song, rest on no record from the time; historians of the Underground Railroad and of Tubman list them as myths [^bordewich][^larson]. Spirituals could carry a second meaning, though, as Frederick Douglass recorded of songs about Canaan [^douglass1855].

### Why do England rugby fans sing Swing Low, Sweet Chariot?

England supporters took it up at matches in the 1980s [^rte][^espn]. In June 2020 the Rugby Football Union reviewed it over its roots in American slavery, and in October decided not to ban it but to teach fans where it came from [^the42][^skysports].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, all the way through, and the other parts sing in harmony beneath it. The tune itself uses only five notes, G, A, B, D and E, the five-note scale Seward's 1872 preface found in more than half the Fisk songs [^seward1872].
