Go Down, Moses is an African American spiritual, made by enslaved people who sang the Bible's story of the Exodus as their own. God sends Moses to Pharaoh, the king of Egypt, with one demand, "Let my people go", and the song answers every line of its verses with it. Its earliest known printing with music is sheet music of 1861, The Song of the Contrabands, taken down at Fort Monroe, Virginia, from people who had escaped slavery in the first months of the Civil War [^contrabands1861][^canterbury]. In 1872 it was printed with 24 verses as the Fisk Jubilee Singers sang it [^jubilee1872]. Harry T. Burleigh set it for voice and piano in 1917 [^burleigh1917][^imslp-burleigh], and Louis Armstrong recorded it in 1958 [^jazzjournal][^freshsound].

## Where it comes from

**Fort Monroe, 1861.** Fort Monroe, then often called Fortress Monroe, was a Union fort in Virginia. In May 1861 three enslaved men, Frank Baker, James Townsend and Shepard Mallory, escaped to it, and its commander, General Benjamin F. Butler, refused to send them back [^leonard][^nps-q1]. Many more followed [^nps-fm]. They were called "contrabands", from "contraband of war", a name that left them, in the National Park Service's words, "neither free nor enslaved" [^nps-dw][^canterbury].

![[1]]

**Lewis Lockwood.** The American Missionary Association sent the Rev. Lewis C. Lockwood to Fort Monroe as its first missionary to the refugees. He wrote that he began the mission there "about the first of September, 1861" [^lockwood1862]; a 1963 study of his work dates his arrival to 4 September [^fen1963]. By the end of the year he had opened schools there [^fen1963][^lockwood1862]. The refugees, he said, had "a deep impression they were the second children of Israel" [^zoellner]. He copied down the words of one song and sent them to New York, and in 1861 they were printed in the papers, without the music, as "The Contrabands' Freedom Hymn" [^zoellner][^pitt].

**The sheet music.** By December 1861 the song was on sale in New York as sheet music, The Song of the "Contrabands": "O Let My People Go", arranged by Thomas Baker and published by Horace Waters [^contrabands1861][^uva][^canterbury][^zoellner]. Its copyright line is dated 1861 [^contrabands1861]; one teaching guide, the University of Pittsburgh's, puts the sheet music a year after the newspaper printing, in 1862 [^pitt]. The cover says its words and music were "obtained through the Rev. L. C. Lockwood, Chaplain of the 'Contrabands' at Fortress Munroe" [^contrabands1861]. A note above the music says the song began among the refugees and was first heard from them when they reached the fort. A note at the end, signed L. C. L., says enslaved people in Virginia had sung it for about nine years [^contrabands1861]. The newspaper printing's headnote, by a YMCA official, said it had been sung "for at least fifteen or twenty years, in Virginia and Maryland"; that headnote is reported only in a history of 2025 [^zoellner]. Baker set it for one voice and piano, with eleven verses and a chorus in four parts: "O! go down, Moses, Away down to Egypt's land, And tell King Pharaoh, To let my people go!" [^contrabands1861]. Some accounts call it the first spiritual to be written down or printed [^pitt][^balladofamerica].

**The Fisk Jubilee Singers.** Fisk University in Nashville, a young school for Black students backed by the American Missionary Association, was deep in debt by 1871 [^fisk][^tsu]. That year its treasurer and music teacher, George L. White, set out with nine student singers to raise money for it [^tsu][^amheritage]. Their songs were printed in 1872 as Jubilee Songs, published in New York by Biglow & Main [^jubilee1872]. The book's introduction says White took the words down from the singers and Theodore F. Seward wrote down the music, testing every melody at the piano and keeping nothing the singers did not approve [^jubilee1872].

**The 1872 printing.** "Go down, Moses" is on pages 22 and 23, in F minor [^jubilee1872]. Its 24 verses run from Israel in Egypt through the Red Sea and the wilderness, across the Jordan to the walls of Jericho, and on to "the golden street" [^jubilee1872]. Its chorus is the one our arrangement sings [^jubilee1872]. Several of the 1861 verses come back in 1872, some almost word for word, among them "No more shall they in bondage toil" and "Jordan shall stand up like a wall" [^contrabands1861][^jubilee1872].

![[2]]

**Myth and record.** Often told: Harriet Tubman, who led enslaved people out of Maryland, was the song's "Moses", and she sang it as a signal to people ready to escape. What the record shows: Tubman was called Moses in her lifetime, for leading her people out of bondage [^bradford1869][^pbs]. Sarah H. Bradford's book of 1869 gives Tubman's own account. To people hiding in the woods, one hymn, sung twice, meant it was safe to come out; a song beginning "Moses go down in Egypt, Till ole Pharo' let me go" meant danger [^bradford1869]. A Tubman historian counts Go Down, Moses as one of two songs Tubman used, and writes that Tubman said she changed the tempo to signal whether it was safe to come out [^larson]. Nothing in these records says the song was made about her [^bradford1869]. Lockwood's note places it in Virginia, and the newspaper headnote in Virginia and Maryland [^contrabands1861][^zoellner].

## What the words mean

The story is in the Book of Exodus. The Israelites are enslaved in Egypt, made to build cities for Pharaoh and to work "with rigour" [^kjv]. In chapter 5 Moses and Aaron bring Pharaoh God's words, "Let my people go". At last, at midnight, the firstborn of Egypt die, and Pharaoh lets the people go (chapter 12) [^kjv].

- **Verse 1.** "When Israel was in Egypt's land" means when the Israelites were enslaved there. "Oppressed so hard they could not stand" means treated so cruelly they could bear no more.
- **Verse 2.** "Thus saith the Lord" means "this is what the Lord says". Moses carries God's warning from Exodus 4: let the people go, or Pharaoh's firstborn son will die. It comes true in Exodus 12 [^kjv].
- **The refrain.** God tells Moses to go down into Egypt and speak to the king. "Ole" is old, and "Pharoh" is the 1872 book's spelling of Pharaoh, which we keep as printed [^jubilee1872].
- **Verses 22 and 23.** "Yer" is your, "de" is the, and "I likes" is I like; the full words below keep the 1872 spelling. "Take your shoes from off your feet" is God's word to Moses at the burning bush (Exodus 3:5), here said at heaven's door [^kjv].

For the people who made it, the story was not only about the past. Frederick Douglass wrote in 1855 that when he and his friends sang of Canaan while planning to escape, they meant more than heaven: "the north was our Canaan" [^douglass1855]. The printed verses say so too: the 1861 sheet music has "O let us all from bondage flee", and the 1872 book has "We need not always weep and moan, And wear these slavery chains forlorn" [^contrabands1861][^jubilee1872].

### The full words

These are all 24 verses as *Jubilee Songs* (1872) prints them on pages 22 and 23, with spelling as printed and the refrain set out in full after verse 1 [^jubilee1872]. The book writes "Go down, Moses, etc." after verses 2 to 4 only, and spells the king "Pharoh" in the refrain but "Pharaoh" in verse 11.

When Israel was in Egypt's land:
Let my people go,
Oppressed so hard they could not stand,
Let my people go.

**Refrain**
Go down, Moses, way down in Egypt land,
Tell ole Pharoh,
Let my people go.

**Verse 2**

Let my people go;
If not I'll smite your first-born dead,
Let my people go.
Go down, Moses, etc.

**Verse 3**

Let my people go;
Let them come out with Egypt's spoil,
Let my people go.
Go down, Moses, etc.

**Verse 4**

Let my people go;
And left the proud oppressive land,
Let my people go.
Go down, Moses, etc.

**Verse 5**

Let my people go;
When Moses led the Israelites,
Let my people go.

**Verse 6**
'Twas good old Moses and Aaron, too,
Let my people go;
'Twas they that led the armies through,
Let my people go.

**Verse 7**
The Lord told Moses what to do,
Let my people go:
To lead the children of Israel through,
Let my people go.

**Verse 8**

Let my people go;

Let my people go.

**Verse 9**

At the command of God it did divide,
Let my people go.

**Verse 10**
When they had reached the other shore,
Let my people go;
They sang a song of triumph o'er,
Let my people go.

**Verse 11**
Pharaoh said he would go across.
Let my people go;
But Pharaoh and his host were lost,
Let my people go.

**Verse 12**
O Moses the cloud shall cleave the way,
Let my people go;
A fire by night, a shade by day,
Let my people go.

**Verse 13**
You'll not get lost in the wilderness,
Let my people go:
With a lighted candle in your breast,
Let my people go.

**Verse 14**
Jordan shall stand up like a wall,
Let my people go;
And the walls of Jericho shall fall
Let my people go.

**Verse 15**
Your foes shall not before you stand,
Let my people go;
And you'll possess fair Canaan's land,
Let my people go.

**Verse 16**
'Twas just about in harvest time,
Let my people go;
When Joshua led his host divine,
Let my people go.

**Verse 17**
O let us all from bondage flee,
Let my people go;
And let us all in Christ be free,
Let my people go

**Verse 18**
We need not always weep and moan,
Let my people go:
And wear these slavery chains forlorn,
Let my people go.

**Verse 19**
This world's a wilderness of woe,
Let my people go;
O, let us on to Canaan go,
Let my people go.

**Verse 20**
What a beautiful morning that will be.
Let my people go:
When time breaks up in eternity,
Let my people go.

**Verse 21**

Let my people go;

**Verse 22**

Let my people go;

## How it is sung

A folk spiritual was sung by ear, in worship, by the people who made it; a concert spiritual is arranged for trained singers on a stage. Go Down, Moses has been both. The 1872 printing keeps its folk shape, a leader's line and everyone's answer: each verse's call is the tune for one voice, the refrain's "Go down, Moses" is the tune for everyone in octaves, and only "Let my people go" is in four-part harmony [^jubilee1872]. The 1861 sheet music instead gives the verses to one voice with piano and sets the whole chorus in four parts [^contrabands1861]. The Fisk singers carried it into concert halls, and their book's introduction says plainly that their schooling had changed how they sang [^jubilee1872]. Burleigh's 1917 setting made it an art song for one voice and piano [^burleigh1917][^imslp-burleigh].

Our arrangement is read from page 22 of the 1872 book, a tone higher, in G minor, and its answer is built on Seward's [^jubilee1872]. Where his answer opens on one bare note, ours fills in the chord. Our alto takes Seward's tenor line from "people" on, the tenor's line is new, and the bass ends an octave below his, on a low G. We sing verses 1 and 2, each with the refrain, slowly and steadily: the soprano sings every call alone, the refrain's too, where the book has everyone sing it, and everyone answers. Two singers sing the soprano and alto, three add the bass, and four the tenor.

## Did you know?

- The 1872 book's introduction says that, as far as the singers knew, none of its songs had been printed before. Go Down, Moses had been sold as sheet music in New York eleven years earlier [^jubilee1872][^contrabands1861].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/go-down-moses/soprano): you sing every call alone. Your top D comes on "way down" and "Egypt" in "way down in Egypt land"; take a full breath before it. Every "people" has a sharpened F: lean up into it, then settle on G for "go".
- [Alto](/songs/go-down-moses/alto): you sing only the answer, "Let my people go", on just two notes, B-flat and C. Listen for the soprano's call, then come in together.
- [Tenor](/songs/go-down-moses/tenor): you sing only the answer, on just two notes, G and A, a good first line to try.
- [Bass](/songs/go-down-moses/bass): you sing only the answer: D for "Let my people", then down to a low G on "go". Land that low G firmly.

## Questions

### Who wrote Go Down Moses?

No one person wrote it: it is a spiritual made by enslaved African Americans, and no maker's name is known. Its earliest known printing with music, the sheet music of 1861 arranged by Thomas Baker, says its words and music came through the Rev. Lewis C. Lockwood, from refugees at Fort Monroe, Virginia [^contrabands1861][^canterbury]. In 1872 George L. White and Theodore F. Seward wrote it down as the Fisk Jubilee Singers sang it [^jubilee1872].

### Was Go Down Moses banned?

No order or law banning it has turned up in the records we have read, though the claim is often repeated; a history of 2025 says the song had been banned in slavery for its pointed words [^zoellner]. The 1861 newspaper printing's headnote, as that history reports it, said it had long been sung "stealthily, for fear of the lash", and was "now sung openly" by those who had escaped [^zoellner]. Its words could mean freedom as well as faith: for Frederick Douglass, singing of Canaan meant the North [^douglass1855].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, and sings every call alone; the 1872 book gives the verse calls to one voice and has everyone sing the refrain's "Go down, Moses" in octaves. Everyone joins for the answer, "Let my people go", in harmony: the alto and tenor on two notes each, and the bass on D and a low G. With two singers you have the soprano and the alto, three add the bass, and four add the tenor.

### Did Harriet Tubman sing Go Down Moses?

By her own account she sang a song close to it, as a warning. In Sarah H. Bradford's book of 1869, a song beginning "Moses go down in Egypt" told people hiding in the woods to stay hidden [^bradford1869]. A Tubman historian writes that Tubman also said she changed a song's tempo to signal whether it was safe to come out [^larson]. Nothing in the record says the song was made about her: Lockwood's note of 1861 places it in Virginia [^contrabands1861].
