Riu, riu, chiu, also written Ríu, ríu, chíu or Riu chiu, is a Spanish Christmas villancico printed in Venice in 1556 in *Villancicos de diversos autores* [^uppsala-title][^uppsala-riu]. The only known copy of that book is in Uppsala University Library in Sweden, hence its name, the Cancionero de Upsala, the Uppsala songbook; it is also called the Cancionero del Duque de Calabria [^mitjana1909][^enccat-canconer]. The refrain opens with a call of pure sound, then sings that God kept the wolf away from "our ewe-lamb", and the first verse makes the lamb the Virgin Mary, kept from every sin [^uppsala-riu][^lopez2026]. No poet or composer is named [^uppsala-riu][^mitjana1909]. A Spanish diplomat, Rafael Mitjana, printed its words in 1909, and the music came out in modern notation in 1944 [^mitjana1909][^residencia][^griffiths].

## Where it comes from

**The book.** Its title page offers villancicos "de diversos autores", by various authors, for two to five voices, "agora nuevamente corregidos", newly corrected, and eight tones of plainchant and eight of part-music for "los que a cantar comencaren", those beginning to sing. Girolamo Scotto (about 1505 to 1572) printed it in Venice in 1556 [^uppsala-title][^omi][^ricercar]. Of its 54 songs, only one names a composer: no. 49, by the Flemish master Nicolas Gombert [^mitjana1909][^enccat-canconer].

**The page.** Riu, riu, chiu fills one opening, folios 42v and 43r, the recto numbered XLIII; the book is numbered on one side of each leaf only [^uppsala-riu][^mitjana1909]. It is in choirbook layout, tiple (the top part) and alto on the left page, tenor and bass on the right, in white mensural notation [^uppsala-riu]. The bass sings the refrain first, alone; then all four sing it to a pause. Then the bass's staff says "SOLO LAS COBLAS", the verses alone, with "El lobo rabioso" printed under its notes, and six more verses stand in italic blocks between the staves [^uppsala-riu]. In the tiple, one "guardo" is set as "gnardo", a turned letter [^uppsala-riu]. Its neighbours show through the paper in mirror image: the Catalan carol "E la don don", Mitjana's no. 45, and "Señores, el qu'es nascido", his no. 47 [^uppsala-riu][^mitjana1909].

![[1]]

**The finder.** Rafael Mitjana (1869 to 1921) was a Spanish diplomat and musicologist who served in Sweden [^bakers][^pardo2013]. At Uppsala's university library, he wrote, "tuve la singular fortuna de tropezar con un rarísimo volumen": he had the rare luck to stumble on a very rare volume. He named it the Cancionero de Uppsala and thought it probably the only copy [^mitjana1909]. His book of 1909 printed the words alone, Riu, riu, chiu as no. 46 [^mitjana1909][^imslp]. In Lavignac's *Encyclopédie de la musique* he gave its shelfmark, 611, and said he had transcribed the music [^lavignac1920][^uppsala-shelfmark].

**Who made it.** Words and tune are both anonymous and first known from this print, the earliest known printing of each and of their pairing [^uppsala-riu][^mitjana1909]. Mitjana thought the book's songs were by Spanish musicians in Italy, and wrote that he recognised some as Flecha's, without a first name or naming which [^lavignac1920]. The Gran Enciclopèdia Catalana says the collection comes from the Valencia court of Ferdinand of Aragon, Duke of Calabria, viceroy there from 1526 to 1550, and that the scholar Josep Romeu i Figueras named it for him, the Cancionero del Duque de Calabria [^enccat-canconer]. Flecha, said to have been born at Prades in Catalonia about 1481, was choirmaster of Valencia Cathedral from 1526 for a little over four years, beside the viceroys' chapel, and died about 1553, probably at the monastery of Poblet [^gomez][^enccat-fletxa].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Mateo Flecha the Elder wrote Riu, riu, chiu. ChoralWiki lists the song under his name, and Wikimedia Commons labels the Uppsala photograph "Attributed to" him [^cpdl][^commons-riu]. What the record shows: the print names no composer for it [^uppsala-riu][^mitjana1909]. The Gran Enciclopèdia Catalana says some of the book's anonymous songs have been attributed to Flecha, but, from concordances, it gives this one, its no. 46, to Bartomeu Càrceres [^enccat-fletxa][^enccat-canconer]. Neither name is in the print, so we credit it as anonymous.

**Mitjana's text.** Mitjana wrote that he had altered nothing, "No me he permitido alterar para nada el texto original" [^mitjana1909]. Yet every refrain in his book reads "Dios guarde el lobo", may God keep the wolf away, a wish. The print, which has no accents, has "guardo" in every line but one, which we read as "guardó", God kept it away, and "guarda", God keeps it, once in the bass's solo [^mitjana1909][^uppsala-riu][^lopez2026]. In the last verse he prints "con nosotros uiniera (sic)", he came with us, where the print has "Conel hombre uiniera", he came with man [^mitjana1909][^uppsala-riu].

### Timeline

- 1556: Girolamo Scotto prints *Villancicos de diversos autores* in Venice, with Riu, riu, chiu on fols. 42v to 43r [^uppsala-title][^uppsala-riu]
- 1909: Rafael Mitjana prints the book's words at Uppsala and names it the Cancionero de Uppsala [^mitjana1909][^enccat-canconer]
- 1920: His history of Spanish music in Lavignac's *Encyclopédie* calls it the only known copy [^lavignac1920]
- 1944: Jesús Bal y Gay's modern transcription of the music is published by El Colegio de México [^residencia][^griffiths]

## What the words mean

The print has a refrain and seven verses; we sing the refrain, the first verse and "Yo vi mil garçones" [^uppsala-riu]. We keep its spelling (hazer for today's hacer, bolando for volando, nasçiera for naciera), read "duziendo" as "diziendo", and add accents to guardó, aquí and Jesús. The print has no accents; we read its "guardo" as "guardó", God kept the wolf away. The soprano's opening call keeps the print's one "guarda", God keeps the wolf away, in its third line, where the bass's solo has it; every other line sings "guardó" [^uppsala-riu].

**The refrain.** "Riu, riu, chiu" is a call of sounds, not words, and neither the print nor Mitjana explains it [^uppsala-riu][^mitjana1909]. "La guarda ribera" is "the guard on the riverbank". "Dios guardó el lobo de nuestra cordera" is, word for word, "God guarded the wolf from our ewe-lamb": he kept the wolf away from her.

**Verse 1** names the lamb. The "lobo rabioso", the raging wolf, tried to bite her, but God defended her: he willed ("quisole hazer") that she could not sin, "ni aun original", not even original sin [^uppsala-riu]. That is the Immaculate Conception: Mary kept free of original sin from the start. In the song's century it was still argued: the Council of Trent in 1546 left her out of its decree on original sin, and the Church defined the doctrine only in 1854 [^trent][^cathen][^ineffabilis]. The lamb is the Virgin [^uppsala-riu][^lopez2026]. The wolf is what would have brought her to sin; one modern commentary reads it as the serpent of Genesis in a new shape [^lopez2026].

**The other verses** turn to Christmas: the child is the great king, clothed in flesh, small though he was infinite. In the verse we sing second, a thousand "garçones", young lads (the angels), go flying about making a thousand "sones", sounds, and singing glory in heaven and peace on earth, the angels' song of Luke 2:14 [^uppsala-riu][^kjv-luke]. Their "a gascones", word for word "to Gascons", is unexplained. The child is also the lamb of John the Baptist's "Behold the Lamb of God" [^uppsala-riu][^kjv-john].

### The full words

These are the refrain and the seven verses as printed on fols. 42v to 43r of the 1556 print at Uppsala, with the spelling as printed and modern letter forms (u and v, i and j by sound, long s as s), words divided as today, and "S." written out as "San". The print adds almost no punctuation and we add none, apart from apostrophes where words run together (qu'es, aunqu'era, porqu'el, qu'era); capitals are regularised. Accents are only the three we sing. The six verses printed as text are not numbered; they are given here in the order they stand on the page, left to right and top to bottom, and Mitjana prints them in another order: El lobo rabioso, Este qu'es nacido, Este viene a dar, Muchas profecías, Mira bien, Yo vi mil garçones, Pues que ya tenemos. Where the type seems to slip we keep it and give the sense, as Mitjana also read it [^mitjana1909]: "ses hiziera" (se hiziera), "alconçado" (alcançado, reached), "estes al" (este es el, this is the), "su hyo so dixera" (su hijo se dixera, called himself her son); the type's "Ioan" we read as "lo an" (lo han, have).

## How it is sung

A villancico is a short song in the vernacular, a refrain with verses (coplas, here "coblas") [^gomez][^uppsala-riu]. Such songs belonged first of all to Christmas Eve; Mitjana pictured the book's Christmas ones sung before the crib in churches and great houses [^gomez][^lavignac1920].

Our arrangement is the print's four parts for 2 to 4 singers, a semitone above its pitch, in E-flat minor: the call and two verses, each followed by the refrain. We raise the leading note at cadences (musica ficta), in the alto and the soprano, and change the alto's and tenor's last notes so the final chords keep their third.

## Did you know?

- The Uppsala copy's binding bears a Stockholm court binder's stamp and a pencil note in Swedish: "Bound at the expense of embassy counsellor R. Mitjana, St. Petersburg. 1913." [^uppsala-flyleaf][^bakers]
- The same book's no. 33 is a shepherd's song with a refrain of sounds, "Falalalanlera", ending "de la guarda riera": a shepherd coming home "de guardar ganado", from minding the flock [^mitjana1909]

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/riu-riu-chiu/soprano): you carry the tune and sing the call and both verses alone. Every line starts on your top B-flat and falls to E-flat ("Ri-u ri-u chi-u la"). Each verse's third line climbs back from E-flat to B-flat ("pu-dies-se pe-car", "glo-ria sea en el çie-lo"). In the second verse, the "mil" of "haziendo mil sones", the "-nes" of "gascones" and the "-lo" of "suelo" take two notes each. In the refrain's second and third lines you come in last, two beats after the tenor and bass, and each refrain's last "nues-tra cor-de-ra" dips to D natural on "tra cor-" before the final E-flat.
- [Alto](/songs/riu-riu-chiu/alto): you sing the refrains, starting on G-flat under the tune's B-flat and ending the first line on a held D natural, "be-ra". In the second and third lines you enter a beat after the tenor and bass and sing "el lo-bo" twice; the second "lo-bo" ends on D natural in the second line but D-flat in the third. Both lines end on G-flat, above the soprano's E-flat, the last after a dip to your low B-flat on "de nues-tra".
- [Tenor](/songs/riu-riu-chiu/tenor): you sing the refrains, from your top D-flat on "Ri-u ri-" to a low E-flat on "da ri-", a fifth below "guar-". In the second and third lines you lead off with the bass: "Dios guar-dó el" goes D-flat, D-flat, C natural, D-flat; tune that half step. The second line then stays on B-flat through "el lo-bo de nues-tra cor-de-ra"; the last walks down G-flat, F, E-flat and leaps up to B-flat for the final "ra".
- [Bass](/songs/riu-riu-chiu/bass): you sing the refrains; the print's bass solos are the soprano's here. The first line leaps around E-flat and dips to C-flat on "da ri-", the one accidental the 1556 print writes in the song, before your low B-flat on "be-ra". In the second and third lines you start first, with the tenor, high on B-flat for "Dios guar-", then step down and swing between E-flat and the low B-flat on "lo-bo de nues-tra cor-de-ra".

## Questions

### What does Riu riu chiu mean?

"Riu, riu, chiu" is a call of sounds, not words, and neither the 1556 print nor Mitjana's edition explains it [^uppsala-riu][^mitjana1909]. The rest of the refrain means "the guard on the riverbank: God kept the wolf away from our ewe-lamb". The first verse shows that the lamb is the Virgin Mary and the wolf stands for the sin God kept from her, even original sin [^uppsala-riu][^lopez2026].

### Who wrote Riu riu chiu?

Nobody knows. The 1556 print names no poet or composer for it [^uppsala-riu][^mitjana1909]. ChoralWiki and Wikimedia Commons credit Mateo Flecha the Elder, who died about 1553, though nothing in the print names him [^cpdl][^commons-riu][^gomez]. The Gran Enciclopèdia Catalana gives it instead to Bartomeu Càrceres [^enccat-canconer].

### How do you say Riu riu chiu?

In Spanish, "ri" sounds like "ree", "u" like "oo", and "ch" as in "church": ree-oo, ree-oo, chee-oo. In our arrangement each word is sung as two syllables on two notes, "ri-u, ri-u, chi-u". The soprano's opening call in the player gives you the whole refrain alone before the others join.

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano, as the tiple, the top part, does in the 1556 print. Our soprano also sings the opening call and both verses alone, solos the print gives to the bass [^uppsala-riu]. With two singers you have the tune and the alto; the bass makes three and the tenor four.
