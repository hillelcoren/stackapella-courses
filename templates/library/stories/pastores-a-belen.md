Pastores a Belén, printed in 1916 as Pastores á Belén and known by its first line, "Pastores a Belén, vamos con alegría", is a Spanish-language Christmas villancico, a carol of verse and refrain [^canticos1916][^rae1869][^rae1914]. The shepherds call one another to Bethlehem to see the son of Mary, and agree to bring him turrones (nougat) and honey [^canticos1916][^rae1914]. The earliest printing we have seen is No. 23 in *Cánticos espirituales con música*, a Catholic songbook published in Las Vegas, New Mexico, in 1916 by the Revista Católica, among sixteen songs for Christmas Eve [^canticos1916][^canticos1916-index]. No poet or composer is named [^canticos1916][^canticos1916-index]. Its editors wrote that they left these songs as untouched as they could, because the people of their region knew and sang them that way [^canticos1916-note].

## Where it comes from

**The book.** Its title page reads *Cánticos Espirituales con música*, "Recogidos por la 'Revista Católica'", gathered by the Revista Católica, Las Vegas, N. Mex., 1916, with "A. M. D. G." at its head and a copyright line in the press's name [^canticos1916-index]. The Revista Católica, a Spanish-language Catholic paper and press, was run by the Jesuits in Las Vegas; the press later moved to El Paso, Texas [^tsha][^castaneda]. The book is a keyboard hymnal, its words printed between the staves, with more than 300 pages of music [^canticos1916][^canticos1916-index].

![[2]]

**The note.** Facing the first song is a note headed "¡¡¡IMPORTANTE!!!". At last, it says, the music of the "Cánticos espirituales, recogidos por el P. Ralliere" is coming out. Words and music do not always keep the rules of the art, it admits, but the people of "nuestras comarcas", our region, to whom the book is above all dedicated, "así los conoce, así los canta, así los quiere": know them, sing them and love them that way. So the editors felt it their duty to leave them "intactos", untouched, as far as they could. For further verses it sends singers to two words-only books the press sold: the fifth edition of the *Cánticos espirituales* (1913) and a *Colección de cánticos espirituales* of 1908 [^canticos1916-note].

![[3]]

**The collector.** "P. Ralliere" is Father J. B. Ralliere, as he signed his Spanish letters: Juan Bautista in Spanish, John Baptist in English [^ellis1955b][^ellisbaca1957c][^salpointe1898][^unm2025]. Florence Hawley Ellis and Edwin Baca, who published his notes in 1957, wrote that he used these forms rather than the French one he was baptised with, to be one with his parishioners [^ellisbaca1957]. J. B. Salpointe, who had been Archbishop of Santa Fe, names him in 1898 as one of six recruits Father Machebeuf brought back from France for the diocese of Santa Fe in 1856 [^salpointe1898][^ellisbaca1957]. The anthropologist Florence Hawley Ellis, who wrote his history in 1955 from his notes and his parishioners' memories, says he came from France as a deacon [^ellis1955a][^ellis1955b]. Ellis writes that he drove his buckboard into Tomé, New Mexico, as its priest on 13 June 1858, and Salpointe lists him as Tomé's parish priest in 1859 [^ellis1955a][^salpointe1898]. Ellis says he taught most of the music in his school himself and trained Tomé's two church choirs, one of men and one of women; she credits him with writing some of the hymns the local churches still sang in her day, and with arranging others [^ellis1955b]. A University of New Mexico lecture page of 2025 calls his *Cánticos espirituales* "the most influential nineteenth-century Spanish-language hymnal of the U.S. Southwest", counts at least fourteen editions between 1884 and 1956, and says its more than 300 songs drew on French, Italian, Spanish, Mexican and New Mexican melodies, often putting new words to a borrowed tune [^unm2025].

![[4]]

**What the page does not say.** Ralliere collected the songs, but the page does not say who wrote this one, though the same book does sign some tunes, such as the second Trisagio on its first page, marked "Lambillotte" [^canticos1916-note][^canticos1916]. Nor does the book tie the song to Las Posadas or to any shepherds' play: it simply files it for Christmas Eve [^canticos1916-index]. We have not seen the words-only editions of 1908 and 1913, or the earlier ones the UNM page counts from 1884, so we cannot say when the song entered the collection, or where its words and tune came from [^unm2025].

### Timeline

- 1856: Six seminarians in France, Ralliere among them, are recruited for the diocese of Santa Fe [^salpointe1898][^ellisbaca1957]
- 1858: Ralliere arrives in Tomé, New Mexico, as its priest, on 13 June by Ellis's account; Salpointe lists him there in 1859 [^ellis1955a][^salpointe1898]
- 1908: A words-only *Colección de cánticos espirituales*, "Edición de 1908", later sold by the Las Vegas press, as the 1916 book names it [^canticos1916-note][^canticos1916-appendix]
- 1916: *Cánticos espirituales con música* prints "Pastores á Belén" as No. 23, among the songs for Christmas Eve [^canticos1916][^canticos1916-index]

## What the words mean

The words are Spanish as the 1916 book prints them, one verse and the refrain under the music [^canticos1916]. The scene is from Luke's Gospel, where the shepherds say to one another, "Let us now go even unto Bethlehem", and find Mary, Joseph and the baby lying in a manger [^kjv].

The page writes "a", "to", as "á", the old spelling, sung the same: the Spanish Academy's dictionary still printed "á" in 1869 but "a" by 1914, and the 1916 book's own index already lists the song as "Pastores a Belén" [^rae1869][^rae1914][^canticos1916-index][^canticos1916].

**The verse.** Shepherds, to Bethlehem! Let us go with joy to see "nuestro bien", our good: the Academy's dictionary of 1869 defines "bien" as that which holds in itself the supreme perfection and goodness, so that in this sense God alone is the highest good [^rae1869]. He is the son of Mary. There, there, there, Jesus waits for us; there his light "nos convida", invites us, the word used for asking someone to come and share a meal [^rae1869].

**The refrain.** So let us bring turrones and honey to offer the Child Manuel. Turrón, the Academy's dictionaries say, is a hard sweet of toasted almonds, pine nuts, hazelnuts or walnuts mixed with honey cooked to the right point [^rae1869][^rae1914]. Manuel is Emmanuel, the name Matthew's Gospel gives the child, "which being interpreted is, God with us" [^kjv]; Spanish used both forms of the name for one person [^covarrubias1611].

The book runs the words under the notes; the line breaks are ours, at its capitals. The refrain is printed twice through, then "al Niño Manuel" twice more to close.

## How it is sung

It is a Catholic Christmas song, and the 1916 book places it among the songs to Jesus Christ, in the set headed "Para la Noche Buena", for Christmas Eve [^canticos1916-index][^rae1869].

The page is set for keyboard in F major and 6/8. In the verse the tune is the top note of the right hand, and a crescendo leads into "Al Hijo de María". The refrain is headed "CORO", chorus, and gets a staff of its own in two voices above the keyboard: soft at first, louder at "Para ofrecer", soft again for the second "Llevemos", louder again, and loud for the closing "al Niño Manuel" [^canticos1916].

Our arrangement sings the one verse and the refrain for 2 to 4 singers, in G, a whole tone above the print, with every pitch and rhythm of the tune as printed apart from a few notes shortened or delayed to make room for breaths, and a shorter last note. The four voices are our own; the book's unsigned keyboard part was used only to check the chords.

## Versions and other words

Hymnals from 1986 on print a carol that opens with the same line, "Pastores, a Belén, vamos con alegría", and credit it as traditional from Puerto Rico. Among them is *The New Century Hymnal*, which gives it in English as "As Shepherds Filled with Joy" [^hymnary-text][^nch1995]. It is a different song from the one in the 1916 book: its refrain has other words, and its tune, as Hymnary.org lists it, goes its own way after the opening repeated notes. Its Spanish words carry a 1949 copyright in the name of Silver Burdett [^hymnary-text][^nch1995].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/pastores-a-belen/soprano): you carry the tune. Each of the first two lines starts with a low D and leaps a sixth up to B, then stays on that B: "-tores á Belén", "-mos con ale-". "Alegría" is the first flourish, four notes down from D. Your top E comes on "María", where one "allí" runs into the next, before "Nos espera Jesús" and again before "Nos convida su luz", and on "Niño" the second time through the refrain. The last "Niño" has a small turn, C up to D, before "Manuel".
- [Alto](/songs/pastores-a-belen/alto): after a low B on "Pas-" and "Va-", you hold D under the tune's opening lines, then sit mostly on F-sharp and G. In "allí, allí" you move against the tune, down from B to G as it climbs and back up as it falls. The first time through the refrain you hold F-sharp through "Para ofrecer al Niño Ma-" and step up to G on "nuel".
- [Tenor](/songs/pastores-a-belen/tenor): you join in four parts. The first line and "Vamos con ale-" are all on G, and "Al Hijo de María" moves up to D. In the refrain your top note, E, is on "turrones", then you drop to A for "miel" and stay there until "Manuel" ends on G. The closing "al Niño Manuel, al Niño Manuel" is all on D, held under the tune and the alto.
- [Bass](/songs/pastores-a-belen/bass): you sing the chords' roots almost all the time, from low G to the G an octave above. "Jesús" and "luz" leap up a fourth to the high G, and "Llevemos" drops straight back down an octave. "-rrones y miel" walks up G, B, D, and the ending goes G, A, D and home to G.

## Questions

### Who wrote Pastores a Belén?

No one is named. The earliest printing we have seen, in a New Mexico Catholic songbook of 1916, prints it unsigned, in a collection gathered by Father J. B. Ralliere, a French-born priest of Tomé [^canticos1916][^canticos1916-note]. The anthropologist Florence Hawley Ellis wrote in 1955 that Ralliere composed some local church songs and arranged others, but nothing ties this one to him [^ellis1955b][^canticos1916]. The collection often set new words to borrowed tunes, a 2025 University of New Mexico lecture page says [^unm2025].

### Is Pastores a Belén from Puerto Rico?

Not as far as the record shows. Hymnals since 1986 print a Puerto Rican carol that opens with the same line, but its refrain and tune are different, and its Spanish words carry a 1949 copyright [^hymnary-text][^hymnary-tune][^nch1995]. The song we sing is the one printed in Las Vegas, New Mexico, in 1916, which names no place of origin, only that the people of its region sang it [^canticos1916][^canticos1916-note]. Where either song began is not known.

### Where are the complete words (letra completa) of Pastores a Belén?

The 1916 book prints only one verse and the refrain with the music, and they are given in full above, with our English meaning [^canticos1916]. For more verses, the book sends singers to its words-only editions of 1908 and 1913, which we have not seen [^canticos1916-note].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, as the 1916 book prints it, a whole tone higher, with a few notes shortened for breaths [^canticos1916]. With two singers you have the tune and the alto beneath it; with three, add the bass, mostly on the roots; the tenor makes four.
