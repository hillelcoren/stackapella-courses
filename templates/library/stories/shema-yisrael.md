The Shema, also spelled Sh'ma or Shema Israel, is named for its first word, "Hear". Its first line is Deuteronomy 6:4, "Hear, Israel: Hashem is ours, Hashem is one", and Jews say it every morning and every evening, again at bedtime, and by long custom as their last words.[^je][^ej][^abrahams] In the prayer book it is followed by a line the Bible does not have, Baruch shem kevod malchuto le'olam va'ed, "Blessed be the name of His glorious kingdom for ever and ever", the answer the people gave in the Temple in Jerusalem.[^myoma][^abrahams] The tune Reform and Conservative congregations sing together is known as Sulzer's, after Salomon Sulzer, cantor in Vienna from 1826, but its earliest known printing is a Brooklyn songbook of 1926, which calls it "Traditional".[^rj][^austerklein][^ndb][^idel29][^goldfarb26]

## Where it comes from

**The words.** Shema is the opening word of a short passage, Deuteronomy 6:4 to 9. It tells Israel to love Hashem with all the heart, soul and strength, to keep these words on the heart, to teach them to the children and speak of them at home and on the road, to bind them on the hand and between the eyes, and to write them on the doorposts.[^deut][^abrahams] The prayer called the Shema is longer. It joins three passages of the Torah: Deuteronomy 6:4 to 9, Deuteronomy 11:13 to 21, and Numbers 15:37 to 41, the last about the fringes, tzitzit, and the going out of Egypt.[^je][^ej][^abrahams]

It was said in the Temple before it was said in the synagogue. The Mishnah, the rabbis' early code of Jewish law, describes the priests on duty each morning reciting a blessing, the Ten Commandments and the three passages, and Israel Abrahams, in his companion to the English prayer book, noted that the synagogue took the custom over from the Temple.[^mtamid][^abrahams] It also explains why the first passage comes first: with it, a person first takes on "the yoke of the kingdom of heaven", and only then, in the second passage, the yoke of the commandments.[^mber][^abrahams]

**Baruch shem.** The line after the verse is not Biblical.[^abrahams][^myoma] The Mishnah's account of Yom Kippur in the Temple shows where it was heard. Each time the high priest spoke Hashem's name aloud in his confessions, the priests and the people standing in the court knelt, bowed, fell on their faces and answered, "Blessed be the name of His glorious kingdom for ever and ever".[^myoma][^abrahams] The Talmud gives the line a story as well. Jacob, dying, feared that one of his sons had strayed; they answered him together, "Hear, Israel", meaning their father, and he replied with these words. Since Moses had not written them in the Torah, but Jacob had said them, the sages ruled that they be said quietly.[^pes56][^je][^ej] On Yom Kippur alone the congregation says them aloud.[^je][^ej]

**The tune.** Salomon Sulzer was born in Hohenems, in the Vorarlberg, Austria, on 30 March 1804. His family took its name from Sulz, where it had lived before settling in Hohenems. The Neue Deutsche Biographie places that Sulz near Feldkirch, in the Vorarlberg; Wurzbach, in 1880, put it in Württemberg.[^ndb][^wurzbach][^jesulzer] He became cantor of Hohenems as a youth, by 1820, and in 1826 he became chief cantor of the new Stadttempel in the Seitenstettengasse in Vienna, the synagogue of the preacher Isaak Noah Mannheimer.[^jesulzer][^ndb][^idel29] Two older sources, Constant von Wurzbach's Austrian biographical dictionary of 1880 and the Allgemeine Deutsche Biographie of 1894, put his call to Vienna in 1825.[^wurzbach][^adb] He served there for fifty-five years and died in Vienna on 17 January 1890 (the Allgemeine Deutsche Biographie has 18 January).[^jesulzer][^ndb][^adb]

![[2]]

In Vienna he kept the old synagogue melodies but set them in the harmony of his day, for the cantor, a choir and the responses of the congregation.[^jesulzer][^wurzbach] What he wanted, he wrote in a memorandum of 1876, was a restoration that stayed on historical ground, so that the old would hear what they knew and the young would learn to love it; the same memorandum also asked for an organ and for hymns in German.[^idel29][^ejsulzer] His music circulated in manuscript, as far as Berlin and even America, before it was printed.[^wurzbach][^idel29] He printed it as *Schir Zion*, "Song of Zion", in two volumes. The first came out in Vienna in 1840, though its preface is dated 1838, and Wurzbach gives 1838 for the printing itself; the second followed in 1865 or 1866, as the sources differ.[^idel29][^jesulzer][^ejsulzer][^ndb][^wurzbach][^imslp]

*Schir Zion* sets the Shema verse three times, all from the first volume, each in 3/4 time and marked Andante maestoso. In the revised edition by Joseph Sulzer, printed in Frankfurt, No. 28 for Friday evening and No. 104 for taking the Torah from the ark give the cantor one melody. No. 70, for Sabbath morning, is the full choir's chordal setting, the same one that closes No. 104. In the Torah-service setting the cantor sings the verse, solo voices join him on its last words, and then the full choir sings the whole verse again, much as the tractate Soferim has the people repeat it after the reader.[^sz3][^soferim] In 1904, to mark the hundredth anniversary of his birth, the Society of American Cantors published a Friday evening service "arranged for use in American Synagogues" from his *Schir Zion*, with the Shema and Baruch shem as its No. 4. Its surviving opening bars follow Sulzer's cantor line; the rest of that page is lost in the scan.[^sz1904]

**The tune we sing.** The Shema that congregations sing today is a different, plainer melody. Its earliest known printing is in *Synagogue Melodies for the High Holy Days*, "arranged for congregational singing" by Israel Goldfarb, a Brooklyn rabbi who taught cantorial music at the Jewish Theological Seminary, and Samuel E. Goldfarb, head of music at New York's Bureau of Jewish Education, and printed in Brooklyn in 1926.[^goldfarb26][^goldberg] On page 17, No. 18, "Sh'ma Yisroel", is a single line marked "Cong.", for the whole congregation, in G major and 3/4 time, with "Traditional" at the top right. Under the notes runs a second verse, Baruch shem, headed "On Yom Kippur add" and marked with an asterisk, "For Yom Kippur", and the Hebrew of both lines is printed below the music. The whole book is melody only, one line for the cantor or the congregation, and it names the Goldfarbs where the work is theirs, "I. Goldfarb" or "Adapted by I. Goldfarb"; this Shema it marks "Traditional".[^goldfarb26] Six years later the Union Hymnal of the Central Conference of American Rabbis, the Reform movement's hymnal, printed the same melody, with a four-part accompaniment, in its services for the religious school, again marked "Traditional", a few lines after a setting of the congregation's response to the call to prayer, Borchu, that it credits to Sulzer.[^uh1932][^goldfarb26]

A 2019 study of congregational song in American Conservative synagogues found it in a manuscript of the cantor Jacob Beimel, which shows it taking shape: his earlier version differs from the one sung today in a single bar. An older manuscript by the cantor Alois Kaiser shares its opening but not the rest. The study argues that the tune should be attributed to Beimel, and calls the 1926 page its first printed occurrence.[^goldberg] Beimel was born in Parichi, in today's Belarus, about 1875; he was a cantor in Berlin and Copenhagen, came to the United States in 1915, served congregations in New York and Philadelphia, and died in 1944.[^ejbeimel][^jmrcbeimel] The 2019 study gives his years as 1880 to 1952.[^goldberg]

**Myth and record.** Often told: Salomon Sulzer wrote the Shema that congregations sing, and it goes by his name.[^rj][^austerklein][^hymnary] What the record shows: Sulzer's own Shema, in the first volume of *Schir Zion*, is another melody. The two share their 3/4 time, their first note and the closing steps on "Adoshem echad", but not what lies between.[^sz3][^goldfarb26] The earliest known printing of the familiar tune, in 1926, and the Union Hymnal of 1932 both call it "Traditional", and a 2019 study argues it should be attributed to Beimel.[^goldfarb26][^uh1932][^goldberg] A cantor writing for the Reform movement explains that musicologists think the familiar version grew out of Sulzer's setting, changed little by little as congregations sang it, the way a message changes in a game of telephone; a later Reform songbook, the Union Songster of 1960, still lists a "Sh'mah Yisrawayl" under Sulzer's name.[^rj][^hymnary]

## What the words mean

**Sh'ma Yisrael.** "Hear, Israel." Sh'ma, or Shema, means "hear". Yisrael is the people of Israel, and in the Talmud's story, Jacob himself, whose other name was Israel.[^pes56][^ej]

**Adoshem Elokeinu.** "Hashem is ours." The Hebrew has two of Hashem's names here: the four-letter name, which we sing as Adoshem, and Elokeinu, a second name with -einu, "our", on its end. Translators have long weighed how to read the verse: as one statement, that Hashem who is ours is one, or as two, that Hashem is ours and that Hashem is one.[^abrahams][^ej]

**Adoshem echad.** "Hashem is one." Echad is "one", and it has also been read as "unique", or "alone".[^ej][^abrahams] In the Torah scroll the last letter of Shema, ayin, and the last letter of echad, dalet, are written larger than the rest, and together they spell ed, "witness".[^ej][^baalhaturim]

**Baruch shem kevod malchuto le'olam va'ed.** "Blessed be the name of His glorious kingdom for ever and ever." Kavod is "glory", malchut is "kingdom". Abrahams noted that this line brings in the idea of Hashem as King, which the three passages themselves never put into words.[^abrahams]

**The first paragraph (not sung).** Deuteronomy 6:5 to 9 turns belief into a way of life. Ve'ahavta, "and you shall love", with all your heart, soul and might. Veshinantam, "and you shall teach them", comes from a root that means to prick in or impress, so the words are to be pressed into children.[^abrahams][^deut] The sign on the hand and the frontlets between the eyes became tefillin, and the doorposts gave the mezuzah, the case fixed to the doorpost; both hold this passage on parchment.[^abrahams][^ej]

We sing the verse with the substituted names customary when singing outside prayer, Adoshem and Elokeinu: many Jews do not say Hashem's names in full outside prayer, out of reverence, and a practice song is outside prayer.[^ohr][^outorah] The 1926 page printed the words in the Ashkenazi pronunciation, "Sh'ma yis-ro-el" with "e-chod" at the end, and "Bo-ruch shem k'-vod mal'-chu-so l'-o-lom vo-ed"; Sulzer and the 1932 hymnal did the same.[^goldfarb26][^sz3][^uh1932] We sing them in modern Israeli pronunciation.

### The full words

These are the verse, the response and the first paragraph, Deuteronomy 6:4 to 9, in the Hebrew text of the Bible as Mechon Mamre prints it, with the line sung when the Torah is taken from the ark, in the prayer book's form of a line from the tractate Soferim.[^deut][^soferim] The Hebrew and the transliteration use the substituted names.

**The verse (sung)**
> שְׁמַע יִשְׂרָאֵל,
*Sh'ma Yisrael,*
Hear, Israel:
> יְיָ אֱלֹקֵינוּ, יְיָ אֶחָד.
*Adoshem Elokeinu, Adoshem echad.*
Hashem is ours, Hashem is one.

**The response (sung)**
> בָּרוּךְ שֵׁם כְּבוֹד מַלְכוּתוֹ
*Baruch shem kevod malchuto*
Blessed be the name of His glorious kingdom
> לְעוֹלָם וָעֶד.
*le'olam va'ed.*
for ever and ever.

**The first paragraph (not sung)**
> וְאָהַבְתָּ אֵת יְיָ אֱלֹקֶיךָ,
*Ve'ahavta et Adoshem Elokecha,*
And you shall love Hashem, who is yours,
> בְּכָל לְבָבְךָ וּבְכָל נַפְשְׁךָ וּבְכָל מְאֹדֶךָ.
*bechol levavcha uvechol nafshecha uvechol me'odecha.*
with all your heart, with all your soul and with all your might.
> וְהָיוּ הַדְּבָרִים הָאֵלֶּה, אֲשֶׁר אָנֹכִי מְצַוְּךָ הַיּוֹם,
*Vehayu hadevarim ha'ele, asher anochi metzavecha hayom,*
And these words, which I command you this day,
> עַל לְבָבֶךָ.
*al levavecha.*
shall be upon your heart.
> וְשִׁנַּנְתָּם לְבָנֶיךָ וְדִבַּרְתָּ בָּם,
*Veshinantam levanecha vedibarta bam,*
Teach them to your children and speak of them
> בְּשִׁבְתְּךָ בְּבֵיתֶךָ וּבְלֶכְתְּךָ בַדֶּרֶךְ,
*beshivtecha beveitecha uvelechtecha vaderech,*
when you sit in your house and when you walk on the road,
> וּבְשָׁכְבְּךָ וּבְקוּמֶךָ.
*uveshochbecha uvekumecha.*
when you lie down and when you rise up.
> וּקְשַׁרְתָּם לְאוֹת עַל יָדֶךָ,
*Ukshartam le'ot al yadecha,*
Bind them for a sign upon your hand,
> וְהָיוּ לְטֹטָפֹת בֵּין עֵינֶיךָ.
*vehayu letotafot bein einecha.*
and they shall be as frontlets between your eyes.
> וּכְתַבְתָּם עַל מְזֻזוֹת בֵּיתֶךָ וּבִשְׁעָרֶיךָ.
*Uchtavtam al mezuzot beitecha uvishe'arecha.*
And write them upon the doorposts of your house and upon your gates.

**When the Torah is taken from the ark (not sung here)**
> אֶחָד אֱלֹקֵינוּ, גָּדוֹל אֲדוֹנֵנוּ,
*Echad Elokeinu, gadol adonenu,*
One is Hashem, great is our Master,
> קָדוֹשׁ שְׁמוֹ.
*kadosh shemo.*
holy is His name.

## How it is sung

The Shema is said twice a day, at night and in the morning, "when you lie down and when you rise up". In the morning two blessings come before it and one after; in the evening, two before and two after.[^deut][^mber][^abrahams] The first verse is said aloud, and Baruch shem quietly.[^sa61][^je] Many cover their eyes with the right hand during the first verse, to keep out everything else; the Talmud tells that Rabbi Judah the Prince passed his hands over his face as he said it.[^ej][^sa61][^ber13][^je] Its last word, echad, is drawn out. The Talmud promises long days to whoever lengthens it.[^ber13]

The verse also stands on its own at other moments. When the Torah is taken from the ark, the reader holds the scroll and says it, and the congregation repeats it; the tractate Soferim says it is said "with a melody", and then the reader adds a line that the prayer book keeps as "One is Hashem, great is our Master, holy is His name".[^soferim][^abrahams][^ej] It is in the Kedushah of the Musaf service on Sabbaths and festivals.[^ej][^abrahams] It is said again before sleep, a custom the Talmud bases on Psalm 4, "commune with your own heart upon your bed".[^abrahams][^je][^ej] At the very end of Yom Kippur, the close of the Neilah service, the congregation calls out the verse once and Baruch shem three times, and then seven times the cry of the people on Mount Carmel in 1 Kings 18:39. The old close had only that sevenfold cry; the Shema lines were added later.[^ej][^sa61][^abrahams]

It is also said at the bedside of the dying.[^je][^ej][^abrahams] The Talmud tells how Rabbi Akiva was put to death for teaching Torah. It was the hour for the Shema, and as his flesh was torn with iron combs he accepted the yoke of heaven and drew out the word echad until his soul left him on it.[^ber61][^je][^abrahams]

The tune we sing was printed for the whole congregation from the start. The 1926 page sets it as one line marked "Cong.", with Baruch shem as a second verse for Yom Kippur, the one day it is said aloud.[^goldfarb26][^je] In the 1932 Union Hymnal's services for the religious school, all sing the verse to this tune, standing, and then sing Baruch shem after the reader.[^uh1932]

Our arrangement is read from the 1926 page, in G and 3/4 as printed, slow and broad. We sing the verse and then Baruch shem to the same six bars, as the page sets it for Yom Kippur, with the fermatas held. The page gives one line for everyone, so the alto, tenor and bass are our own: the alto mostly a third or a sixth under the tune, a half close on "Elokeinu", and a full close on "Adoshem echad" and "le'olam va'ed". With two singers it is soprano and alto, with three the bass joins, and with four the tenor.[^goldfarb26]

## Did you know?

- The Talmud says that a child should be taught the Shema as soon as he begins to speak, and it was among the first verses Jewish children learned. [^abrahams][^davidson]

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/shema-yisrael/soprano): the tune, within four notes, G to C, most often on G. It rises to its top C only on the "shem" of the first "Adoshem" and the "vod" of "kevod", and holds the A on "Elokeinu," and "malchuto".
- [Alto](/songs/shema-yisrael/alto): mostly a third or a sixth under the tune, most often on E and F-sharp. Listen at the close, on "Adoshem echad." and "le'olam va'ed.": the alto walks D, C, B a sixth under the tune's B, A, G, down to its low B. On "malchuto" it moves with the tune on one syllable; solo it in the player to hear it.
- [Tenor](/songs/shema-yisrael/tenor): the middle of the chord, most often on D, then G and C. It sings all of "Sh'ma Yisrael," on its top D, drops to A under the half close on "Elokeinu,", and goes down to its low D, an octave under, only on the last "ed.".
- [Bass](/songs/shema-yisrael/bass): the bass line, most often on D, then G. It starts on its top G under "Sh'ma Yisra-" and "Baruch", walks down E, C, A to D, and holds D under the end of each line until it falls to its low G on "echad." and "va'ed.".

## Questions

### Where is the Shema prayer in the Bible?

Its first verse is Deuteronomy 6:4, and the prayer called the Shema joins three passages of the Torah: Deuteronomy 6:4 to 9, Deuteronomy 11:13 to 21 and Numbers 15:37 to 41. The line said after the first verse, Baruch shem kevod malchuto le'olam va'ed, is not in the Bible; it was the people's answer in the Temple when the high priest spoke Hashem's name.[^je][^ej][^abrahams][^myoma]

### Who wrote the Shema prayer?

It is not a composed prayer: its words are verses of the Torah, from Deuteronomy and Numbers, and the Mishnah already treats the three passages as one reading said morning and evening, as the priests said them in the Temple. Baruch shem comes from the Temple service. The melody most congregations sing is a separate story, told below.[^mtamid][^mber][^abrahams]

### What is the full Shema prayer?

In the prayer book the full Shema is the first verse, the quiet response Baruch shem, and three passages: love Hashem and keep these words (Deuteronomy 6:5 to 9), reward and the rain (Deuteronomy 11:13 to 21), and the fringes and the going out of Egypt (Numbers 15:37 to 41). Blessings surround it: two before and one after in the morning, two before and two after at night.[^je][^ej][^abrahams][^mber]

### Who wrote the Shema melody?

Nobody knows for certain. It is called "Sulzer's Shema", after the Vienna cantor Salomon Sulzer, but his own printed Shema is a different melody that shares only its time, its first note and its close. The earliest known printing of the tune sung today is in Brooklyn in 1926, marked "Traditional", and a 2019 study, working from his manuscript, argues it should be attributed to the cantor Jacob Beimel, who died in 1944.[^sz3][^goldfarb26][^goldberg][^austerklein]
