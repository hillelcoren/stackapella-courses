Abide with Me is a prayer for God's company as life ends. Henry Francis Lyte wrote it in 1847, the last year of his life, while he was curate of Lower Brixham, a fishing town in Devon [^remains1850][^canterbury]. It is usually sung to EVENTIDE, the tune William Henry Monk wrote for the first music edition of Hymns Ancient and Modern in 1861 [^ham1861][^hymnary-eventide]. It has long been sung at funerals [^canterbury][^julian]. Crowds have also sung it before the FA Cup Final in England since 1927 [^richards][^pdr].

## Where it comes from

**The words.** Lyte was born on 1 June 1793 at Ednam, near Kelso in Scotland, as the hymnologist John Julian gives it; his daughter's memoir says simply Kelso [^hymnary-lyte][^hymnarchive-lyte][^remains1850]. After Trinity College, Dublin, he became a clergyman, and from 1823, as Julian has it, or 1826, as the 1884 account gives it, served the new church at Lower Brixham, a rough seafaring parish on Torbay [^hymnary-lyte][^hymnarchive-lyte][^remains1850]. His health had failed once before, as a young curate in Ireland: the memoir tells of "a rapid decline" [^remains1850], and an account of 1884 of "symptoms of consumption" [^hymnarchive-lyte]. It broke down again in the 1840s: the 1884 account sends him abroad for his health in 1842, the memoir speaks of bronchitis in the winter of 1843, and he spent much of his time abroad after that [^hymnarchive-lyte][^remains1850]. In the spring of 1847 he came home from the Continent very ill, to Berry Head, his house of many years above Brixham [^remains1850][^hymnarchive-lyte].

His family told the rest in the memoir that opens his Remains of 1850 [^remains1850][^julian]. In September, the month he was to leave, he insisted against their fears on preaching once more, on Holy Communion. The memoir goes on: "In the evening of the same day he placed in the hands of a near and dear relative the little hymn, 'Abide with me,' with an air of his own composing adapted to the words." Within hours he set out for the south [^remains1850]. A footnote to the printed sermon dates it 4 September 1847, though that was a Saturday, so the date may be a day out; the memoir names only the month. The book signs the hymn "Berry Head, September, 1847" [^remains1850][^julian]. Near Nice, the memoir says, he caught influenza, which turned to dysentery. He died at Nice on 20 November 1847 and was buried in the English cemetery there [^remains1850][^hymnarchive-lyte].

John Julian, who saw it, records a leaflet printed at Berry Head in September 1847, the earliest known printing of the words [^julian][^hymnarchive]. In 1850 the Remains printed all eight verses, without music [^remains1850][^hymnarchive]. Julian records that Lyte's own tune, his "air", was published in Bristol in 1863 [^julian][^hymnarchive], but Hymnology Archive knows of no surviving copy of that edition [^hymnarchive].

**The tune.** William Henry Monk (born at Brompton, London, 1823; died 1889) was a London church organist [^romance1889][^hymnary-monk] and the music editor of Hymns Ancient and Modern, the Church of England hymnal whose first music edition came out in 1861 [^ham1861][^romance1889]. It prints the hymn as No. 14, among its evening hymns: five of Lyte's eight verses, set to EVENTIDE, a tune marked as composed for the book or printed there for the first time [^ham1861]. The standard edition of 1875 renumbered it 27 [^ham1875].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Lyte wrote the hymn in his study on the day of his farewell sermon, the evening before it in one telling [^canterbury], the hours after it in another. A book of 1889 adds that he "dragged his weary frame to his room" and was "no doubt" at work on the hymn that afternoon [^romance1889]. What the record shows: the memoir says only that he handed it over that evening, never when he wrote it [^remains1850]. Hymnology Archive reports a letter of Lyte's from late August 1847, published by a descendant in The Times in 1947, that already enclosed the hymn as his "latest effusion" [^hymnarchive]. The Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology now thinks it more likely that he wrote it at Berry Head in August [^canterbury].

Often told, too: Monk wrote EVENTIDE in ten minutes at a committee meeting [^hymnary-eventide]. What the record shows: the 1889 book has it second-hand, from a friend Monk told that he and Sir Henry Baker, about to go out, remembered there was no tune for "hymn 27", and that he wrote one in ten minutes while a piano lesson went on [^romance1889][^hymnarchive]. Yet 27 is its number in the 1875 edition; in 1861 it was 14 [^ham1861][^ham1875][^hymnarchive]. Monk's widow told a different story, of a tune written in a time of grief as the couple watched the sunset [^hymnarchive][^songsandhymns]. Neither can now be checked.

The hymn went on to mark deaths far from Brixham. On 11 October 1915, the evening before the British nurse Edith Cavell was executed in Brussels, the British chaplain H. Stirling Gahan visited her in prison. He wrote that after their short service he began to say "Abide with me", and she joined in softly at the end [^times1916][^gahan]; some retellings have her singing it [^pdr]. It is also said that the Titanic's band played it as the ship sank in 1912 [^pdr][^behe]. Survivors remembered different pieces, and what it played last is still argued over [^mystic].

## What the words mean

Lyte set Luke 24:29 above the hymn: "Abide with us: for it is toward evening, and the day is far spent" [^remains1850][^kjv]. Two disciples say it on the road to Emmaus to the risen Jesus, before they know who he is. "Abide" means stay; "eventide" means evening, and here the evening is the end of life. The hymnologist John Ellerton noted in 1881 that beyond its first lines it says nothing of the close of day, and suits funerals better [^julian][^romance1889].

1. Night comes fast; when every other help and comfort fails, stay with me.
2. Life's "little day" ebbs out like a tide and its joys fade. All things change; God does not.
3. Not a passing glance: stay as Jesus stayed with his disciples, "familiar, condescending", which in the old sense means coming kindly down to their level. "Sojourn": a short stay.
4. Not as a fearsome King of kings, but kind, "with healing in Thy wings", a phrase from Malachi, and as the "Friend of sinners", a name given to Jesus in Matthew [^kjv]. "Bide" means stay.
5. God smiled on my youth and never left me, though I often left him.
6. Only grace can beat "the Tempter", the devil; stay through cloud and sunshine.
7. With God near, no enemy frightens. "Where is death's sting, where, grave, thy victory?" is St Paul, in 1 Corinthians 15 [^kjv].
8. Hold the cross before my closing eyes: heaven's morning breaks, and earth's shadows flee.

Hymns Ancient and Modern left out verses 3, 4 and 5, and most hymnals still do [^ham1861][^julian]. We sing verse 1.

### The full words

All eight of Lyte's verses. Verses 1, 2 and 6 to 8 are as Hymns Ancient and Modern printed them in 1861, without the stress marks it adds for chanting [^ham1861]. Verses 3, 4 and 5, which it left out, are as Lyte's Remains printed them in 1850 [^remains1850].

**Verse 1**
Abide with me; fast falls the even-tide;
The darkness deepens; Lord, with me abide;
When other helpers fail, and comforts flee,
Help of the helpless, O abide with me.

**Verse 2**
Swift to its close ebbs out life's little day;
Earth's joys grow dim, its glories pass away;
Change and decay in all around I see;
O Thou Who changest not, abide with me.

**Verse 3**
Not a brief glance I beg, a passing word,
But as Thou dwell'st with Thy disciples, Lord,
Familiar, condescending, patient, free,
Come, not to sojourn, but abide, with me!

**Verse 4**
Come not in terrors, as the King of kings;
But kind and good, with healing in Thy wings:
Tears for all woes, a heart for every plea.
Come, Friend of sinners, and thus bide with me!

**Verse 5**
Thou on my head in early youth didst smile,
And, though rebellious and perverse meanwhile,
Thou hast not left me, oft as I left Thee.
On to the close, O Lord, abide with me!

**Verse 6**
I need Thy presence every passing hour;
What but Thy grace can foil the tempter's power?
Who like Thyself my guide and stay can be?
Through cloud and sunshine, Lord, abide with me.

**Verse 7**
I fear no foe with Thee at hand to bless;
Ills have no weight, and tears no bitterness;
Where is death's sting, where, grave, thy victory?
I triumph still, if Thou abide with me.

**Verse 8**
Hold Thou Thy Cross before my closing eyes;
Shine through the gloom, and point me to the skies;
Heaven's morning breaks, and earth's vain shadows flee,
In life, in death, O Lord, abide with me.

## How it is sung

Hymns Ancient and Modern placed it among its evening hymns in 1861 [^ham1861][^ham1875], but it is above all a funeral hymn [^canterbury][^julian]. EVENTIDE is in 10.10.10.10 meter: four lines of ten syllables [^ham1861][^ham1875].

Our arrangement is Monk's own four parts in E-flat, read from the 1861 book and checked note for note against the 1875 one. We sing verse 1 slowly, a breath before each line, in crotchets where Monk wrote minims. Each syllable gets one note, so five notes the hymnal slips in under held syllables are left out; one tenor note is changed to avoid a leap of a minor sixth, and the Amen is left out.

## Versions and other words

Julian compared four early texts, which differ slightly: Lyte's manuscript, the 1847 leaflet, the Remains of 1850 and Miscellaneous Poems of 1868 [^julian]. Hymns Ancient and Modern's text differs from the Remains in three places: Lyte's "Hold then Thy cross" became "Hold Thou Thy Cross", "Through cloud and sunshine, O abide with me" became "Through cloud and sunshine, Lord, abide with me", and "In life and death" became "In life, in death" [^remains1850][^ham1861].

## Did you know?

- The Massed Bands of India's armed forces played it at the Beating Retreat ceremony, which closes the Republic Day celebrations, every year from 1950. It was dropped from the programme in 2022 [^dawn2020][^scroll2022].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/abide-with-me/soprano): you carry Monk's calm tune, from E-flat up to C on "falls" and "deepens", and back down to E-flat on the last "me". After "Lord" it climbs step by step, F, G, A natural, B-flat, on "with me abide".
- [Alto](/songs/abide-with-me/alto): you sing the words close under the tune on just four notes, mostly E-flat and D. Under "comforts flee" you step through E natural to F, the part's one chromatic moment. Solo it in the player and tune that E natural.
- [Tenor](/songs/abide-with-me/tenor): you sing the words in the middle of the chords, mostly on B-flat. You drop to a low E-flat on "fast falls" and on the "a-" of "Lord, with me abide", and rise to C on "Lord", on "fail, and comforts" and on the last "abide".
- [Bass](/songs/abide-with-me/bass): you sing the bass line, mostly roots, on E-flat, B-flat and C above all. You go down to a low G on "fast" and "helpers", then climb to your top note, A-flat, on "Help" and walk down the scale, A-flat, G, F, E-flat, under "Help of the helpless".

## Questions

### Who wrote Abide with Me, and why?

Henry Francis Lyte, curate of Lower Brixham in Devon, wrote it in 1847, very ill and about to leave England for the winter [^remains1850][^canterbury]; he died at Nice that November [^remains1850][^hymnarchive-lyte]. His daughter's memoir reads it as his own prayer as death came near, and he built it on Luke 24:29, the disciples' plea at Emmaus to "abide with us" as evening falls [^remains1850][^kjv].

### Why is Abide with Me sung at the FA Cup Final?

Because it was sung at the 1927 final at Wembley, and the custom stuck [^richards][^cardiff]. It came in with a short-lived fashion for crowd "community singing", which newspapers promoted [^richards][^cardiff]. Cardiff City's own history of that final says the FA's secretary asked the palace what King George V would like to hear, and he chose this hymn [^cardiff].

### Is "Abide with Me, 'Tis Eventide" the same hymn?

No. It is a separate American hymn, with words by Martin Lowrie Hofford and music by Harrison Millard [^hymnary-tis][^tabernacle]. Lyte's hymn begins "Abide with me! Fast falls the eventide" and is usually sung to William Henry Monk's EVENTIDE [^remains1850][^ham1861].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune in our arrangement, Monk's melody exactly as Hymns Ancient and Modern printed it in 1861 [^ham1861]. With two singers you have the tune and the alto. With three, add the bass, and the tenor makes four.
