The Water Is Wide, also printed as O Waly, Waly, is an English folk song about a love that cools. The singer cannot cross the water, wishes for a boat that will carry two, and ends knowing that love "fades away like morning dew". No author is known. Cecil Sharp took it down in Somerset from Mrs Cox of High Ham, and he and Charles Marson printed it in 1906, in the third series of Folk Songs from Somerset [^sharp1906][^sharp1907][^foxstrangways]. Several of its lines are older: they are in Waly, Waly, a Scottish lament already in print in the 1720s [^child][^glen]. The tune Sharp printed is now also a hymn tune, named O WALY WALY in hymnals [^hymnary-tune][^balladindex].

## Where it comes from

**Somerset, 1906.** Sharp collected many of his first songs at Hambridge in Somerset, where his friend Charles Marson was vicar [^sharp1907][^foxstrangways][^canterbury]. In the third series of their Folk Songs from Somerset, the song is No. 66, on pages 32 and 33, headed "O Waly, Waly" and marked Andante con espressione, slowly and with feeling. It has six verses, set for voice and piano [^sharp1906]. Sharp's note at the back reads: "Words and air from Mrs. Cox, of High Ham." He had noted the song five times in Somerset, with tunes and words that varied a great deal, and Mr Thomas of Cannington had sung him one very close to Mrs Cox's [^sharp1906]. The title was his, not hers: "Our Somerset words have so much affinity with the well-known Scottish ballad 'Waly Waly,' that we are publishing them under the same title" [^sharp1906].

**The Scottish song.** Waly, Waly, gin love be bonny is a woman's lament for a lover who has proved false [^child][^rpo]. William Thomson's Orpheus Caledonius, a London book with no date on it, usually dated 1725, printed four verses with a tune [^child][^glen][^stenhouse]. Allan Ramsay printed ten verses in the second volume of his Tea-Table Miscellany; the song is not in the first volume, dated 1724. Sources date the second volume 1726, "1724 or 1725", or simply before 1727 [^ramsay1724][^dsl-waly][^stenhouse][^child]. A London reprint dated 1727, A New Miscellany of Scots Sangs, has it on page 182, and the Dublin edition of 1729 on page 176 [^newmisc1727][^ramsay1729][^child]. Two of its verses are close to the Somerset words, almost line for line: the singer leans on an oak that "bow'd and syne it brak", bent and then broke, and love is lovely "a little time while it is new" but "fades away like morning dew" [^ramsay1729][^child][^sharp1906]. It names Edinburgh landmarks, Arthur's Seat and Saint Anton's Well, a spring on the hill's side [^chambers1862][^hes].

**A different tune.** The Scottish words had their own air. Sharp's note sends readers elsewhere for "the Scottish tune", printed, for one, in Robert Chambers's Songs of Scotland Prior to Burns [^sharp1906][^chambers1862].

**Myth and record.** Often told: the verses are the lament of Lady Barbara Erskine, who married James, second Marquis of Douglas, in 1670 and was formally separated from him in 1681; Robert Chambers wrote that the verses "are the lament of the unfortunate marchioness" [^chambers1862][^child]. Chambers dates the wedding to September, Child to near the end of the year [^chambers1862][^child]. What the record shows: verses of the song do turn up in the ballad of her story, Jamie Douglas, but Child found that the song was printed some fifty years before any copy of the ballad, and that the song is the lament of an unmarried woman, not a wife [^child]. A second legend placed the song in the reign of Mary Queen of Scots, because a burlesque of its "love is bonny" verse sits in a book of church music copied in 1566. David Laing answered in 1853 that the lines were written in that manuscript much later, and Child dated them about 1620 [^stenhouse][^child].

**In the West Country.** Sabine Baring-Gould had the song as A Ship Came Sailing three times: sent to him from the Cornish parish of Saint Enoder, where the vicar took it down from a very old man, and heard from Sally Satterley on Dartmoor and from William Nichols of Whitchurch, near Tavistock. It is No. 86 in Songs of the West, whose revised edition of 1905 Sharp edited for music [^baringgould][^sharp1907]. Sharp's note calls all three versions Devon ones, though one came from Cornwall [^sharp1906][^baringgould]. Sharp counted Waly, Waly among the "so-called Scottish ballads" he had found in Somerset [^sharp1907].

## What the words mean

The song moves from a wish to a warning, one picture of lost love in each verse.

1. The water is too wide to cross, "o'er" meaning over, and the singer has no wings. If only there were a boat big enough for two, and both of them to row it.
2. In the meadows the singer gathered flowers, bright ("gay"), red and blue, never thinking what love could do.
3. Reaching into a bush for the sweetest flower, the singer pricked a finger right to the bone and left the flower where it was.
4. The singer leaned against an oak, sure it was a "trusty tree". It bent, then broke: that is what the false lover did.
5. A ship is loaded as deep as it can go, but not as deep as the singer's love, and the singer no longer knows whether to sink or swim.
6. Love is handsome and fine, a jewel while it is new; when it is old it "groweth", grows, cold, and fades like morning dew.

### The full words

These are the six verses as printed in Folk Songs from Somerset, third series (1906), No. 66, page 33, read from the Sibley Music Library copy, with its spelling and punctuation. We sing verses 1 and 6. The words are traditional, and the book's editors died long ago, Marson in 1914 and Sharp in 1924, so all six verses are in the public domain [^sharp1906][^canterbury][^foxstrangways][^onlinebooks][^hymnary-tune].

**Verse 1**
The water is wide I cannot get o'er
And neither have I wings to fly.
Give me a boat that will carry two
And both shall row, my Love and I.

**Verse 2**
O, down in the meadows the other day
A-gath'ring flow'rs both fine and gay,
A-gathering flowers, both red and blue,
I little thought what love can do.

**Verse 3**
I put my hand into one soft bush
Thinking the sweetest flower to find.
I pricked my finger right to the bone,
And left the sweetest flower alone.

**Verse 4**
I leaned my back up against some oak
Thinking that he was a trusty tree:
But first he bended and then he broke;
And so did my false Love to me.

**Verse 5**
A ship there is and she sails the sea,
She's loaded deep as deep can be,
But not so deep as the love I'm in:
I know not if I sink or swim.

**Verse 6**
O, love is handsome and love is fine,
And love's a jewel while it is new,
But when it is old, it groweth cold
And fades away like morning dew.

## How it is sung

It is a folk song for one voice [^sharp1906]. The book's introduction, signed by Marson, asks for folk songs to be sung "with the utmost simplicity and directness and with close attention to time and rhythm" [^sharp1906].

Our arrangement takes the melody and words from the 1906 printing. It is in G major and 3/4, slow and lyrical, for 2 to 4 singers. We sing verses 1 and 6. The soprano sings Mrs Cox's tune, the alto and tenor sing the words beneath it, and the bass holds the low notes on "oo".

## Versions and other words

- **A Ship Came Sailing**, the Devon and Cornish version in Songs of the West, shares the ship, the oak and the rose bush that pricks the finger, and adds a verse about "ten thousand ladies in the room" [^baringgould].
- **Sharp's own later version.** In One Hundred English Folksongs (1916), Sharp printed O Waly, Waly in eight verses. Its first verse asks "O go and get me some little boat", and it adds a verse beginning "Must I be bound" [^sharp1916].
- **Britten's O Waly, Waly** is No. 6 in the third volume of his Folk Song Arrangements, British Isles, made in 1945 and 1946 [^bnf][^ircam].
- **Hymn words.** Fred Pratt Green's An Upper Room Did Our Lord Prepare (1973) is sung to O WALY WALY, and a paraphrase of 1 Corinthians 13, The Gift of Love, to an arrangement of the tune called GIFT OF LOVE [^hymnary-upperroom][^hymnary-tune][^balladindex]. Both texts are under copyright.

## Did you know?

- Verse 3 of the Somerset words, the hand in the bush and the finger pricked "to the bone", is close to a verse of Martin Parker's broadside ballad The Distressed Virgin. The Pepys Library copy is dated 1678 to 1680; Baring-Gould dated a printing 1646 to 1674 [^baringgould][^ebba].
- Child set beside our last verse a line of Chaucer's Clerk's Tale, "Love is noght old as whan that it is newe" [^child][^skeat].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/the-water-is-wide/soprano): you carry Mrs Cox's tune, smooth and mostly stepwise, sung twice. It starts on low D for "The" and lifts a fourth to G on "wa-". Your top note, the D an octave above where you started, comes once in each verse, on "boat" and on "old". Little two-note turns ("to", "a", "car-ry") need a light touch. In the last line you sit on low D for "row, my love" ("-way like morn-" in our second verse), then lift through E and F-sharp to rest on G.
- [Alto](/songs/the-water-is-wide/alto): you sing the words beneath the tune, mostly a third or a sixth below, on just six notes, from B below middle C up to G. Much of your part rests on that low B, as on "row, my love" and the final "dew." Your top G comes on "two," and "cold,", under the tune's B.
- [Tenor](/songs/the-water-is-wide/tenor): you sing the words in the middle, often holding G while the tune moves above you, as on "The water is". You rise to middle C, your top note, in the same places in both verses: in the first on "cannot", "fly", "will carry" and the "And" of the last line. Your last note drops to low D, your lowest note, sung only there, under the tune's final G.
- [Bass](/songs/the-water-is-wide/bass): you sing on "oo" in long notes. The song opens on a low G held for two bars. From "boat" to "two" you walk B, C, A, then up to E, your top note. Under "row, my love and" you hold D for two bars beneath three different chords.

## Questions

### Is The Water Is Wide Scottish or English?

Both, in different ways. The song as sung today is English: Cecil Sharp noted its tune and words in Somerset and printed them in 1906 [^sharp1906][^sharp1907]. Some of its lines are also in an older Scottish song, Waly, Waly, printed in the 1720s [^child][^glen]. For traditional poetry, Sharp wrote, England and the Lowlands of Scotland "must be regarded as one area" [^sharp1907].

### What does "O Waly, Waly" mean?

It means "alas, alas". In Scots, waly is a cry of sorrow, like "woe is me" [^dsl-waly][^chambers1862]. The Scottish song begins with it, crying out over the bank, the hillside and the burn where the singer and her love used to walk [^ramsay1729][^child]. The Somerset words never use it: Sharp borrowed the Scottish title because the two songs share so much [^sharp1906].

### Is The Water Is Wide a hymn?

No, it is a love song, but its tune is also a hymn tune, called O WALY WALY in hymnals, which credit it as an English or Somerset folk melody [^hymnary-tune]. Among the hymns sung to it is Fred Pratt Green's An Upper Room Did Our Lord Prepare, written in 1973 [^hymnary-upperroom][^balladindex].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, as Sharp printed it in 1906 [^sharp1906]. The alto and tenor sing the words beneath it, and the bass sings "oo" underneath. With two singers you have the tune and the alto; add the bass for three and the tenor for four.
