Cantique de Jean Racine, "Jean Racine's hymn", is a piece for four-part choir and organ by Gabriel Fauré, who wrote it in 1865 as a student at a church-music school in Paris [^koechlin][^nectoux]. Nectoux writes that he wrote it for the school's end-of-studies competition and that it won him the first prize in composition that summer [^nectoux]. Its words are by the playwright and poet Jean Racine [^bnf-racine]: a French version of Consors paterni luminis, a Latin hymn long credited to Saint Ambrose and sung at the night office on Tuesdays [^mesnard][^julian]. It begins "Verbe égal au Très-Haut", "Word equal to the Most High", and is also called the Cantique de Racine, Fauré's Op. 11 [^koechlin][^schoen1876]. We sing Fauré's own four voice parts, in French, from its earliest known printing.

## Where it comes from

**The Latin hymn.** Consors paterni luminis is a morning hymn in three short verses and a doxology. It is one of twelve hymns the Benedictine editors of Saint Ambrose held to be surely his, and Hincmar quoted it as Ambrose's in 857 [^julian]. An 8th-century manuscript at Trier gives it to the night office on Tuesday, and so does the Roman Breviary [^julian][^mesnard]. Its first verse says "we break the night by singing", "noctem canendo rumpimus" [^mesnard].

**Racine's French.** Jean Racine (1639 to 1699) was brought up at the abbey of Port-Royal, and his son Louis recorded Boileau saying that Racine translated the breviary's weekday hymns there [^bnf-racine][^mesnard]. Charles Perrault wrote in 1700 that Racine made them as a student and gave them their final polish much later [^mesnard]. They were printed in Racine's lifetime, mixed in with other men's translations, in Nicolas Le Tourneux's French and Latin Roman Breviary, dated 1688 [^mesnard][^sentence1688]. A note in the papers of the abbé de Tralage, who died the same year as Racine, names "Verbe égal au Très-Haut" as one of his [^mesnard].

**A line that got into trouble.** On 10 April 1688 the church court of Paris condemned Le Tourneux's translation [^sentence1688][^mesnard]. Among its reasons, it said the hymn for Tuesday had turned "Aufer tenebras mentium", take away the darkness of our minds, into "Répand sur nous le feu de ta grâce invincible", pour on us the fire of your invincible grace, "against the text" [^sentence1688]. The court saw everywhere in the book's hymns a push for "la seule grâce efficace", grace that cannot fail, and Mesnard explains that suspicious readers could find a little Jansenism in that line [^sentence1688][^mesnard]. When Louis Racine printed his father's hymns in 1747, that verse read "grâce puissante", mighty grace, and "ce sommeil d'une âme languissante", the sleep of a listless soul, in place of "ce sommeil qui rend l'âme insensible", the sleep that numbs the soul [^lracine1747][^mesnard]. Paul Mesnard, who edited Racine's works in 1865, believed the son had made the change himself [^mesnard]. Fauré set the son's words [^schoen1876][^lracine1747].

**Fauré's student piece.** Fauré, born at Pamiers in 1845, studied in Paris at the school of religious music founded by Louis Niedermeyer. Niedermeyer died in 1861, and Fauré's piano teacher there was Camille Saint-Saëns [^koechlin][^bnf-faure]. On Friday 28 July 1865 the school gave out its prizes. The jury included Saint-Saëns, and the list reads: "Composition musicale... 1er prix... Gabriel Fauré, boursier de l'évêque de Pamiers", a scholar paid for by the bishop of Pamiers; he also won the prize for counterpoint and fugue [^menestrel1865]. The report doesn't name the piece. His biographer Charles Koechlin dates the Cantique to 1865, and Jean-Michel Nectoux, who edited it in 2005, writes that it was the piece Fauré wrote for that competition. The rules asked for an orchestra, Nectoux adds, but Fauré sent it with organ alone, and the jury gave him the prize anyway [^koechlin][^nectoux][^hyperion].

**Into print.** By 1866 Fauré had added strings to the organ [^nectoux][^hyperion]. The earliest known printing, for choir with organ or piano, is in the first issue of the Écho des Maîtrises, "a new collection of unpublished religious songs by the most famous modern composers", edited by Romain Bussine, a singing teacher at the Paris Conservatoire, and printed by F. Schoen in Paris [^schoen1876][^bnf-bussine][^bnf-echo]. The issue is undated; the national library dates it to 1876 from its copy's note "Dépôt de la Société des Concerts", and IMSLP also gives 1876 with a question mark [^schoen1876][^imslp]. It is No. 10 of ten pieces, beside music by Saint-Saëns and César Franck, and its first page is headed "A Monsieur César Franck", with Fauré named as "organiste de la Maîtrise de St Sulpice", the choir organist of the church of Saint-Sulpice [^schoen1876]. The print doesn't say why he chose Franck.

## What the words mean

Racine's hymn is a prayer at night, before dawn. It speaks to Christ as the Word, "le Verbe", equal to God the Father, the "Très-Haut" or Most High; the Latin calls him the partner of the Father's light, light of light and day itself [^mesnard]. The singers say they are breaking the silence of the night with their song, and ask him to look on them. The second verse asks for his grace like a fire, so that hell runs from his voice, and for the sleep of a sluggish soul to be driven away. "Languissante" means weak and listless, and "l'oubli de tes lois" is forgetting his laws. The third verse asks Christ to be kind to his people gathered to bless him, to accept their song, and to send them home "comblé", filled to the brim with his gifts. Racine's fourth verse, a doxology to the Trinity, is not in Fauré's piece [^mesnard][^schoen1876].

Fauré's words differ from Racine's in a few places. His second verse is Louis Racine's, with "puissante" and "languissante" [^schoen1876][^lracine1747]. He also has "le sommeil", the sleep, for "ce sommeil", this sleep, "à l'oubli" for "dans l'oubli", and "rassemblé", gathered together, for "assemblé", gathered [^schoen1876][^mesnard][^lracine1747]. Where his source came from is not known.

### The full words

These are the words as Fauré set them and as we sing them, from the earliest known printing; Racine died in 1699 and Fauré in 1924, so all of it is in the public domain. The English is our own.

> Verbe égal au Très-Haut, notre unique espérance,
Word equal to the Most High, our only hope,
> Jour éternel de la terre et des cieux,
eternal Day of the earth and the heavens,
> De la paisible nuit nous rompons le silence:
we break the silence of the peaceful night:
> Divin Sauveur, jette sur nous les yeux.
divine Saviour, cast your eyes on us.

> Répands sur nous le feu de ta grâce puissante,
Pour on us the fire of your mighty grace,
> Que tout l'enfer fuie au son de ta voix;
let all hell flee at the sound of your voice;
> Dissipe le sommeil d'une âme languissante
drive away the sleep of a listless soul
> Qui la conduit à l'oubli de tes lois!
that leads it to forget your laws!

> Ô Christ, sois favorable à ce peuple fidèle,
O Christ, be kind to this faithful people,
> Pour te bénir maintenant rassemblé;
gathered now together to bless you;
> Reçois les chants qu'il offre à ta gloire immortelle,
receive the songs it offers to your undying glory,
> Et de tes dons qu'il retourne comblé!
and let it go home filled with your gifts!

## How it is sung

The Latin hymn belongs to Matins, the night office of prayer, on Tuesdays [^julian][^mesnard]. Racine's French was printed beside the Latin in a breviary translated for French readers [^mesnard][^sentence1688]. Fauré wrote his Cantique for choir with organ, and the 1876 printing offers "orgue ou piano", organ or piano [^nectoux][^schoen1876].

The piece is in D-flat major, Andante, four beats to the bar. In Fauré's score the organ plays twelve bars alone, then the basses begin, softly, "Verbe égal au Très-Haut"; the tenors join them on "Notre unique espérance", the altos come in on "De la paisible nuit" and the sopranos last, on "nous rompons le silence" [^schoen1876]. After the first verse the choir rests while the organ plays alone. Our arrangement leaves the organ out. The third verse begins with the basses alone, "Ô Christ, sois favorable", and the piece ends repeating "Et de tes dons qu'il retourne comblé!", slowing down, poco rall., onto a long held chord [^schoen1876].

Our arrangement is read from the 1876 printing: Fauré's own four voice parts, every note as printed, in D-flat, in French, for four singers only, because the parts enter one by one and cross [^schoen1876]. The organ is left out: we start on the basses' entry, its interlude becomes a rest of five beats, and the last chord is held three beats. It is sung slowly, 60 beats a minute, and runs about four and a half minutes, with breaths written in at Racine's commas.

## Versions and other words

Besides the 1866 version with strings, Fauré's later publisher, Hamelle, printed it as his Op. 11, listed by Koechlin for mixed chorus with harmonium and string quintet [^koechlin][^imslp]. An orchestral version followed: Nectoux writes that its first performance was announced for 28 January 1906, at the Société des Concerts du Conservatoire in Paris, while another note dates the orchestration to 1905 [^nectoux][^hyperion].

## Did you know?

- The 1876 printing cost 2 francs, and the choir's separate parts were sold at 20 centimes each [^schoen1876].
- The issue that printed Fauré's Cantique is dedicated "À Sa Sainteté Pie IX", Pope Pius IX, and its ten pieces include a Veni Creator by César Franck, the man Fauré's piece is dedicated to [^schoen1876][^bnf-franck].

## Singing it

- [Bass](/songs/cantique-de-jean-racine/bass): you begin the piece alone, softly, on "Verbe égal au Très-Haut", mostly on F, A-flat and D-flat. You reach your top B-flat thirteen times, among them "Sau-" of the second "Divin Sauveur" and "Dis-" of "Dissipe", and your low F on "len-" of "silence". In the third verse you sing "Ô Christ, sois favorable" alone.
- [Tenor](/songs/cantique-de-jean-racine/tenor): you join the basses on "Notre unique espérance", mostly on D-flat, B-flat and C. Your top G-flat comes only twice, on "Dis-" of "Dissipe" and on "bli" of the first "à l'oubli". In the third verse you join the basses again on "à ce peuple fidèle".
- [Alto](/songs/cantique-de-jean-racine/alto): you enter third, on "De la paisible nuit", mostly on F, D-flat and B-flat. You first go down to your low C on "yeux!" at the end of the first verse, and you go up to C-flat only as a passing quarter note in the runs on "Sau-" in the second "Divin Sauveur" and of "tes" in "et de tes dons". In the third verse you lead the upper voices back in on "Reçois les chants".
- [Soprano](/songs/cantique-de-jean-racine/soprano): you come in last, on "nous rompons le silence", mostly on C, D-flat and F. Your top G-flat comes only on "ta voix, Dis-", in the second verse, and your low D-flat only on the last two "comblé!", so keep it soft.

## Questions

### What does Cantique de Jean Racine mean?

It means "Jean Racine's hymn": a "cantique" is a sacred song, and the words are Racine's French version of a Latin hymn [^mesnard][^julian]. They are a night prayer to Christ, the Word equal to the Most High: the singers break the night's silence with their song, ask for his grace like fire to wake their sleepy souls, and ask him to accept their song and send them home filled with his gifts [^schoen1876]. The full French with our English is above.

### When did Fauré write Cantique de Jean Racine?

In 1865, when he was a student at the École Niedermeyer in Paris; he turned twenty that May [^koechlin][^bnf-faure]. Nectoux writes that he wrote it for the school's end-of-studies competition and won the first prize in composition with it. Le Ménestrel's report of the prize-giving of 28 July 1865 gives Fauré that prize but does not name the piece [^nectoux][^menestrel1865]. The earliest known printing came about eleven years later, in the Écho des Maîtrises of about 1876 [^schoen1876][^imslp].

### Is Cantique de Jean Racine in Latin or French?

It is in French. Jean Racine translated the Latin hymn Consors paterni luminis into French verse, printed in a breviary of 1688, and Fauré set the French [^mesnard][^sentence1688][^schoen1876]. The second verse Fauré set is the version Racine's son Louis printed in 1747, with "grâce puissante" where the 1688 breviary has "grâce invincible" [^lracine1747][^mesnard].

### Which part sings the tune?

No one part has the tune all the way through. The basses begin alone, and the tenors, altos and sopranos come in one by one; from then on the sopranos sing the highest line, while the other parts cross and answer each other [^schoen1876]. That is why our arrangement needs all four singers. The soprano part runs from low D-flat up to G-flat, the bass from low F up to B-flat.
