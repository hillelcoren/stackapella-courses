Greensleeves, also written Green Sleeves and known by its first line, "Alas, my love, you do me wrong", is an English love song from the reign of Elizabeth I [^chappell][^handful]. A man asks why the Lady Greensleeves has cast him off after he dressed her in silk and gold [^handful][^chappell]. Nobody knows who wrote the words or the tune [^balladindex][^pittaway1]. Its earliest record is a ballad entered at Stationers' Hall in London in September 1580, and its earliest known printing is a songbook of 1584 [^chappell][^pittaway2][^balladindex]. Its tune is in lute books copied around 1590, and the same tune later carried the Christmas carol What Child Is This? [^hymnarchive][^tcd][^bramley]

## Where it comes from

**The ballad of 1580.** On 3 September 1580 the London printer Richard Jones entered, in the register of the Stationers' Company, "A new Northern Dittye of the Lady Greene Sleeves" [^chappell][^hymnarchive][^pittaway2][^balladindex]. That same day another printer, Edward White, entered a reply in her voice, "the Ladie Greene Sleeves Answere to Donkyn" [^chappell][^pittaway2][^balladindex]. William Chappell, the Victorian historian of English song, took that as a sign the song was already popular, since "the date of the entry, however, is not always the date of the ballad" [^chappell]. No copy of the 1580 printing is known to survive [^pittaway2][^balladindex].

![[1]]

Within twelve days a version "moralised to the Scripture" was licensed, and more sequels followed within the year [^chappell][^pittaway2]. Chappell put the craze down to "the merry swing of the tune" more than the words [^chappell].

**The words in 1584.** Richard Jones printed the song in his songbook A Handefull of Pleasant Delites in 1584, the earliest known printing of the words [^chappell][^pittaway2][^balladindex]. Its heading reads "A new Courtly Sonet, of the Lady Green sleeues. To the new tune of Greensleeues" [^handful][^pittaway2]. It has eighteen four-line stanzas, each followed by the refrain, which is also printed above the first stanza; it is unsigned, ending only "Finis" [^handful].

![[2]]

**The tune.** The tune survives in lute and cittern books of the late 1500s and early 1600s [^pittaway2][^hymnarchive]. The copy usually called the oldest is in Trinity College Dublin MS 408, known as William Ballet's lute book [^hymnarchive][^tcd]. The library describes two unrelated manuscripts bound together; Greensleeves is in the second, a book of the ballad and dance tunes of Elizabeth's last years, not in the one that bears Ballet's name [^tcd]. An early-music writer dates that second manuscript about 1592 to 1603 [^pittaway2]. Chappell printed his "Oldest copy" from this volume [^chappell]. It is Italian in form: the verse moves over the passamezzo antico and the refrain over the romanesca, repeating chord patterns from Italy [^pittaway2][^skinner].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Henry VIII wrote Greensleeves, many say for Anne Boleyn [^royal][^pittaway1]. What the record shows: Henry died on 28 January 1547, and no trace of the song is known before 1580 [^royal][^pittaway1][^balladindex]. The song names no one [^pittaway1]. The royal family's own history calls it "almost certainly not one of his compositions", and a historian of Tudor music writes that "Henry VIII did not compose 'Greensleeves'", though more than thirty pieces in the Henry VIII Manuscript, a songbook from his court, do carry the king's name [^royal][^skinner][^diamm]. One root of the story may be in the book our arrangement is read from [^pittaway1]. In 1598 Edward Guilpin wrote of an old ballad, The Lord of Lorne, "Whose last line in King Harries days was borne"; that ballad was sung to Greensleeves, so Chappell wrote, in a part of his book issued about 1856, that "it would appear that Green Sleeves must be a tune of Henry's reign" [^chappell][^nls][^pittaway1]. His own footnote allowed that Guilpin might mean an older ballad [^chappell]. The early-music writer calls the inference an error, and the Hymnology Archive says Guilpin's mention of Henry "might be entirely fanciful" [^pittaway1][^hymnarchive].

### Timeline

- 1580: On 3 September Richard Jones enters the ballad of the Lady Greensleeves at Stationers' Hall; a reply is entered the same day [^chappell][^pittaway2][^balladindex]
- 1584: Jones prints it in A Handefull of Pleasant Delites, the earliest known printing of the words [^handful][^chappell][^pittaway2]
- About 1592 to 1603: the tune is copied into a lute book now bound in Trinity College Dublin MS 408, the volume called William Ballet's lute book [^tcd][^pittaway2]
- 1642: a carol book gives a New Year's Day carol "to the tune of Green Sleeves" [^chappell][^hymnarchive]
- 1856: William Chappell prints the oldest copy of the tune with harmony by G. A. Macfarren, in Part V of Popular Music of the Olden Time [^chappell][^nls]
- 1867: In November the first series of Bramley and Stainer's Christmas Carols New and Old prints W. Chatterton Dix's What Child Is This? to the tune, as No. 14 [^mt1867][^bramley]

## What the words mean

The 1584 ballad is one long complaint [^handful]. Stanza by stanza, in our own words:

**Stanzas 1 and 2.** You wrong me, my love, by throwing me off so rudely ("discourteously"), when I have loved you so long and loved your company. I have been ready to give you whatever you asked, and I have staked ("waged") my life and land to win your love and good will.

**Stanzas 3 to 12.** He lists his gifts: kerchiefs ("kerchers") for her head; her keep at "board and bed", food and lodging; petticoats, one of sendal, which Chappell glosses "thin silk"; jewels; a silk smock worked with gold [^chappell][^handful]. A girdle (belt) of gold set with pearls, a purse, gilt knives and a pin case, finer than a burgess's wife's ("Burgesse wiues"); crimson silk stockings, and pumps (light shoes) as white as milk. Her gown was "grossie green" (grassy green), with sleeves of satin hanging down, and they made her "our haruest Queen", queen of the harvest feast [^handful][^chappell]. Gold-fringed garters, a gelding (a horse) to ride, and his own men dressed in green to wait on her.

**Stanzas 13 to 16.** His men served her so humbly her foot hardly touched the ground. He sent her dainties every morning and gave her music; she could wish for nothing she did not get. And who paid for all this finery ("geare")? He did, the one she now rejects [^handful].

**Stanzas 17 and 18.** He will pray that she sees how constant he is and agrees ("vouchsafe") to love him before he dies. Then farewell, Greensleeves, God prosper you: I am still your true lover, so come back and love me [^handful].

**The refrain.** Greensleeves was his joy, his delight, his heart of gold: she was everything to him [^chappell].

### The full words

These are the words as William Chappell printed them in Popular Music of the Olden Time, pages 230 and 231: the first verse and the refrain under the music, then seven more stanzas, each followed by "Greensleeves was all my joy, &c." He left out ten of the eighteen stanzas of 1584 and summed up most of them in prose, and the meaning of all eighteen is given above [^chappell][^handful]. His text is modernized from 1584: "you do me wrong" for "ye do me wrong", "discourteously" for "discurteously", and "And who but my Lady Greensleeves" where 1584 has "And who but Ladie Greensleeues" [^chappell][^handful]. His "booth" and "gargeously" are printed so; the 1584 text has "both" and "gorgeously" [^chappell][^handful]. Beside "sendal" he adds "[thin silk]", left out here.

**Verse 1**
Alas! my love, you do me wrong,
To cast me off discourteously,
And I have loved you so long,
Delighting in your company.

**Refrain**
Greensleeves was all my joy,
Greensleeves was my delight,
Greensleeves was my heart of gold,
And who but my Lady Greensleeves.

**Verse 2**
I have been ready at your hand
To grant whatever you would crave,
I have both waged life and land,
Your love and good-will for to have.
Greensleeves was all my joy, &c.

**Verse 3**
I bought thee kerchers to thy head,
That were wrought fine and gallantly,
I kept thee booth at board and bed,
Which cost my purse well favoredly.
Greensleeves was all my joy, &c.

**Verse 4**
I bought thee petticoats of the best,
The cloth so fine as might be;
I gave thee jewels for thy chest,
And all this cost I spent on thee.
Greensleeves was all my joy, &c.

**Verse 5**
Thy smock of silk, both fair and white,
With gold embroidered gargeously;
Thy petticoat of sendal right,
And these I bought thee gladly.
Greensleeves was all my joy, &c.

**Verse 6**
They set thee up, they took thee down,
They serv'd thee with humility;
Thy foot might not once touch the ground,
And yet thou wouldst not love me.
Greensleeves was all my joy, &c.

**Verse 7**
Well I will pray to God on high,
That thou my constancy mayst see,
And that yet once before I die
Thou wilt vouchsafe to love me.
Greensleeves was all my joy, &c.

**Verse 8**
Greensleeves, now farewell! adieu!
God I pray to prosper thee!
For I am still thy lover true,
Come once again and love me.
Greensleeves was all my joy, &c.

## How it is sung

Chappell marks his oldest copy "Smoothly and in moderate time", in 6/8, and wrote that in the West of England it was still sung at harvest-homes [^chappell].

Our arrangement reads the tune and Macfarren's harmony from Chappell's page 230, a whole tone higher, in F sharp minor and a slow 6/8 [^chappell]. We sing the first verse and the refrain, for 2 to 4 singers: the soprano sings the tune, the bass mostly follows the lowest notes of Macfarren's piano part, and the alto and tenor are our own on his harmony. Chappell squeezes "company" under one chord; we sing "com-pa-ny", as Frederick Bridge prints it in his "generally accepted version" of 1923 [^bridge][^chappell]. Breaths are written in for beginners.

## Versions and other words

**Other words for the tune.** A carol book of 1642, which the Hymnology Archive names Good and True, Fresh and New Christmas Carols, set a "Carol for New Year's Day" to it, beginning "The old year now away is fled" [^chappell][^hymnarchive].

**What Child Is This?** William Chatterton Dix's "What Child is this, who, laid to rest" is No. XIV, set to the tune, marked "Old English", in the first series of Bramley and Stainer's Christmas Carols New and Old, which came out in November 1867 [^bramley][^mt1867][^hymnarchive]. The date 1871, often given, belongs to a later illustrated edition [^hymnarchive]. Hymnary.org and the early-music writer say Dix made the words from his poem The Manger Throne of 1865; the same book prints a different Dix carol under that title as No. II [^hymnary][^pittaway2][^bramley].

![[4]]

**The later tune.** The Beggar's Opera, which Chappell dates 1728, sets words to "Green Sleeves" as Air LXVI, in the condemned hold [^gay][^chappell]. That version, with a different second half, was the best known in Chappell's day; its earliest source he knew was The Dancing Master of 1686 [^chappell][^pittaway2]. Bridge's "generally accepted version" of 1923, like our arrangement, has the older second half of the lute books [^chappell][^bridge].

## Did you know?

- Shakespeare names the tune twice in The Merry Wives of Windsor, in print by 1602. In 2.1 Mistress Ford says Falstaff's words and his character go together no better than "the Hundredth Psalm to the tune of 'Greensleeves'", and in 5.5 Falstaff cries "Let the sky rain potatoes, let it thunder to the tune of 'Greensleeves'" [^folger][^folger55][^chappell][^folgertext].
- On 23 April 1660, after ninepins and supper, Samuel Pepys heard his lord sing "a song made upon the Rump" to the tune of "The Blacksmith", which, his editor notes, "was the same tune as 'Green Sleeves'" [^pepys][^chappell].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/greensleeves/soprano): you carry the old tune, from C sharp up to the top E on the refrain's first and third "Greensleeves". Listen for one change: in the verse, "love" turns on D natural, but in the refrain "sleeves" falls from E through D sharp. "Discourteously" and "delight" end on your low C sharp.
- [Alto](/songs/greensleeves/alto): you sing the words under the tune, mostly on C sharp and A, with many repeated notes. You rise to F sharp on "Alas! my" and "I have", sit low on G sharp for "do me wrong" and "you so long", and lift to E on "my heart of", dropping to B for "gold".
- [Tenor](/songs/greensleeves/tenor): your whole line fits between E and A, moving mostly by step. You open on A for "Alas! my love", and the refrain starts low, five notes on E for "Greensleeves was all my". Mind the E sharp, a half step above your low E, on "-courteously" and in "my delight".
- [Bass](/songs/greensleeves/bass): you sing Macfarren's bass line, mostly E, C sharp and F sharp. The opening walks down F sharp, D, A on "Alas! my love". The ending is the big moment: after "who but my" on F sharp and "Lady" on C sharp, you drop to the low F sharp under the last "Greensleeves".

## Questions

### Did Henry VIII write Greensleeves?

Almost certainly not. Henry died in January 1547, and the song first appears in the record in September 1580, more than thirty years later [^royal][^chappell][^pittaway1]. The song names no one, so nothing ties it to Anne Boleyn, and the royal family's own history calls it "almost certainly not" his [^pittaway1][^royal].

### What does Greensleeves mean?

In the ballad, Greensleeves is the name the singer gives a woman he has dressed at great cost, down to her green gown and hanging satin sleeves, and who still will not love him [^handful]. A popular modern reading makes her a woman of loose morals. The early-music writer finds no sign of that in the words and takes the green literally, and the Traditional Ballad Index reports the reading only as hearsay [^pittaway1][^balladindex].

### Is Greensleeves the same as What Child Is This?

They share a tune, and Greensleeves came first by nearly three hundred years. The love song was entered in London in 1580 [^chappell][^pittaway2]. William Chatterton Dix's Christmas words were printed to the Greensleeves tune in November 1867, in the first series of Bramley and Stainer's Christmas Carols New and Old [^mt1867][^bramley].

### Is Greensleeves an English folk song?

It is an anonymous English song, but it began as a printed ballad, entered in London by a printer in 1580, not a song collected from country singers [^chappell][^balladindex][^pittaway1]. Chappell wrote in the 1850s that it was still sung at harvest-homes in the West of England, while the Traditional Ballad Index doubts the words ever passed into oral tradition, though the tune did [^chappell][^balladindex].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, Chappell's oldest copy, a whole tone higher [^chappell]. With two singers it is the tune and the alto; three add the bass, Macfarren's own line; the tenor, moving mostly by step in the middle, makes four.
