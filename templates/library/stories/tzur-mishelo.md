Tzur Mishelo is one of the zemirot, the Hebrew songs Jews sing at home around the Sabbath table, most of all before or after the grace after the main meals.[^je][^ej] Its refrain takes the old invitation to say grace together, "Bless the One from whose food we have eaten", and turns it into a chorus for the whole table.[^mishnah][^abrahams][^loewe] Its verses then follow the grace in order: thanks for food, thanks for the land, and a plea for mercy on Zion, ending with the Temple rebuilt and a full cup of wine.[^ej][^loewe][^metsudah] No one knows who wrote it. Our tune is the German melody that the Jewish Encyclopedia of 1906 named as the air most often used for it.[^je]

## Where it comes from

The words have no author's name and no acrostic to give one.[^loewe][^abrahams] What they do have is a model: the grace after meals, Birkat Hamazon. The refrain borrows its first half from the zimmun, the invitation the leader says when three or more have eaten together. When four or more have eaten, the Mishnah has the leader say: "Bless the One from whose food we have eaten."[^mishnah][^abrahams] Its second half, "we have eaten our fill and left some over, as God said", comes from the story of Elisha feeding a hundred people with twenty loaves.[^tanakh][^abrahams][^loewe] The verses follow the grace in order: the first thanks God for feeding the world, as the grace's first paragraph does, the second for the land, as the second does, and the third asks, as the third does, for mercy on Zion and for the heir of David. The fourth carries on the third's prayer for the Temple rebuilt, and ends over the full cup of wine on which the grace is said.[^ej][^loewe][^metsudah]

How old it is, nobody can say. In 1926 Herbert Loewe reported that L. Hirschfeld, who had put the Sabbath table songs into German verse, thought Tzur Mishelo as old as the Mishnah.[^loewe][^abrahams] His reason was the grace itself. The song follows only its first three blessings, which the rabbis drew from the Torah, and has nothing of the fourth, which the Talmud says the sages added at Yavneh after the fall of Betar.[^loewe][^talmud] Loewe doubted it: the style seemed too polished for so early a date, and he did not think the song was in the oldest prayer books.[^loewe]

One more thing is missing from the words: the Sabbath. Israel Abrahams, in his notes to the Singer's prayer book in 1914, points out after Seligman Baer that the song never mentions it, and Loewe says the same in 1926.[^abrahams][^loewe] Yet it belongs to the Sabbath table, and the Encyclopaedia Judaica notes that it is not sung on weekdays.[^ej]

The tune has its own history. In 1906 the Jewish Encyclopedia's article on the zemirot, by Joseph Jacobs and Francis L. Cohen, explained that the tunes of the table songs were younger than their words, often folk songs a father brought home and sang at his own table. Where a family chose a few songs to sing, it said, the most frequent air was a German melody, used either for Tzur Mishelo or for another table song, "Yom Zeh le-Yisrael".[^je] It printed that melody under the heading "Sabbath Rest", slow, in 6/8 and in G minor.[^je] The three English verses printed under it are not Tzur Mishelo at all. They follow Yom Zeh le-Yisrael: their refrain, "A light and a joy to us is Sabbath rest", is that song's "light and gladness, a Sabbath of rest".[^je][^yomzeh] The Hebrew of Tzur Mishelo is fitted to the tune here by us.

## What the words mean

The title is the refrain's first words. Tzur is "rock", an old name for God: Hannah's song says "there is no rock like our God".[^tanakh] Mishelo is "from His own", and achalnu "we have eaten". So the refrain opens: the Rock, from whose own food we have eaten.[^metsudah][^loewe]

The song has four verses, with the refrain before, between and after them. We sing verses 1 and 4.

**Refrain (sung).** The Rock from whose food we have eaten: bless Him, my faithful friends. We have eaten our fill and there is food left over, just as God said. Barchu, "bless!", is the zimmun's call, and kidvar, "as the word of", is the very word of the Elisha story, where the people ate and had some left over "as God had said".[^mishnah][^tanakh]

**Verse 1 (sung).** He feeds His world, our shepherd, our father. We have eaten His bread and drunk His wine. So we will thank His name and praise Him aloud, saying and answering: none is holy like God. The opening echoes the grace's first blessing, which thanks God for feeding all.[^ej][^loewe][^metsudah] The last line is from Hannah's song in the Book of Samuel.[^tanakh][^loewe]

**Verse 2.** With song and thanks we bless our God for the good land He gave our ancestors, and for the food that filled us. This is the grace's second blessing, for the land.[^ej][^metsudah]

**Verse 3.** Have mercy on Your people and on Zion, home of Your glory, and let David's heir come to redeem us. This is the third blessing, for Jerusalem.[^ej][^metsudah]

**Verse 4 (sung).** Let the Temple be rebuilt and the city of Zion filled again, and there we will sing a new song and go up with joy. May the Merciful One, the Holy One, be blessed and raised high, over a full cup of wine, as God has blessed us. The "new song" is Isaiah's.[^tanakh][^loewe] The cup is the cup of blessing over which the grace is said, and the Talmud asks that it be full.[^ej][^talmud]

Where the Hebrew has God's four-letter name, we sing Adoshem: "kidvar Adoshem", "kevirkat Adoshem", and in verse 1 "ein kadosh kadoshem", where the name is joined to ka, "like". Many Jews change God's names when saying or singing them outside prayer, out of reverence, and a practice song is outside prayer; the meaning is the same.[^ohr][^enkinnames]

## How it is sung

Tzur Mishelo is sung at home at the end of the Sabbath meal. Ashkenazi prayer books print it among the songs for the Friday-night meal, and the Encyclopaedia Judaica calls it an introduction to the grace.[^ejzem][^metsudah][^ej] Because its verses come so near the grace itself, a few authorities have warned that singing it might count as saying grace, and so advise singing it in mid-meal, or only after the grace. Most dismiss the worry, and the custom is to sing it at the meal.[^metsudah][^enkin]

These songs were sung by families, not choirs. Rose L. Henriques, who set the tunes for Loewe's book in 1926, pictured the father leading off each verse, the rest of the family following, and those who could "sing seconds" adding harmonies; each household, she wrote, had its own version of the tunes.[^loewe] The Jewish Encyclopedia went further: few table songs, it said, kept the same melody for more than two generations.[^je]

Our arrangement reads the tune from page 658 of the Jewish Encyclopedia's twelfth volume, in G minor as printed, sixteen bars in 6/8 after a short pickup.[^je] The four parts are our own, for 2 to 4 singers: soprano and alto; then bass; then tenor. The Hebrew is in modern Israeli pronunciation. The refrain takes the first eight bars, and each verse takes all sixteen: its first eight are the refrain's music, then four new bars climb higher than anything in the refrain, and the last four come home to the refrain's close. We sing the refrain first, between the two verses, and last.

## Versions and other words

**Other words for this tune.** The Jewish Encyclopedia gives the same German melody to Yom Zeh le-Yisrael, another song for the Friday-night meal, whose English verses it printed with the tune.[^je][^yomzeh][^ejzem]

**Other tunes for these words.** Loewe's book of 1926 gives Tzur Mishelo two tunes, marked A and B. Its tunes are those of one family, which, Henriques wrote, traced its history to Königsberg around 1650.[^loewe]

**In English.** The 1914 annotated Singer's prayer book prints an English version by Nina Salaman, taking some phrases and its metre from a rendering by Israel Zangwill.[^abrahams][^loewe][^salaman]

## Did you know?

- Loewe's book of 1926 set its table songs in three and four parts, to be sung as the tune alone, as the tune and the lowest voice, or in full, as far as each family's skill allowed. [^loewe]

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/tzur-mishelo/soprano): carries the German melody, most often on G, then A. The refrain and both verses end the same way: a leap up to D on the "-do-" of "Adoshem" ("kadoshem" in verse 1), a quick fall to F-sharp, and home to G on "-shem". The second half of each verse climbs to the top F, on "lishmo" in verse 1 and "hanikdash" in verse 4; it is a semitone above the range we usually write for sopranos, so take it lightly.
- [Alto](/songs/tzur-mishelo/alto): sings under the tune, mostly a third or a sixth below it, most often on F, then B-flat. At every "Adoshem." it drops to its low A just as the tune leaps up to D, holds it while the tune falls to F-sharp, and steps up to B-flat as the tune lands on G; solo it with the soprano in the player to hear the two lines meet.
- [Tenor](/songs/tzur-mishelo/tenor): the middle of the chord, most often on G, then C. In verse 1 it stays on its top C from "amarnu" to the first syllable of "kadosh" while the other parts move around it. Its only low D is on "naale." in verse 4.
- [Bass](/songs/tzur-mishelo/bass): the bass line, most often on B-flat, then G. In every refrain it reaches its low F on "achalnu," and, a moment later, its top E-flat on "barchu" and "emunai,".

## Questions

### What does "Tzur Mishelo" mean?

"The Rock, from His own": the opening of the refrain, "the Rock from whose own food we have eaten". Tzur, "rock", is a biblical name for God, as in Hannah's song in the Book of Samuel: "there is no rock like our God".[^tanakh][^metsudah][^loewe]

### What is the origin of Tzur Mishelo?

No one knows who wrote it or when. It is built on the grace after meals: its refrain borrows the invitation to say grace together, and its verses follow the grace's first three paragraphs. One scholar, L. Hirschfeld, thought it as old as the Mishnah; Herbert Loewe doubted that in 1926. The tune we sing is the German melody that the Jewish Encyclopedia of 1906 called the air most often used for it.[^ej][^abrahams][^loewe][^je]

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the German melody the Jewish Encyclopedia printed, all the way through. The alto sings under it, mostly a third or a sixth below. With two singers it is soprano and alto, with three the bass joins, and with four the tenor fills the middle of each chord.[^je]
