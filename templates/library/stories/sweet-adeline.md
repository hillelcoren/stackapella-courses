Sweet Adeline, printed in 1903 as You're the Flower of My Heart, Sweet Adeline, is a ballad with music by Harry Armstrong and words by Richard H. Gerard, published by M. Witmark and Sons of New York [^lilly1903][^spaeth1940][^witmark1939]. Its verses tell of a lover remembering a sweetheart who is gone; its chorus, with long held notes that other voices can answer, is the part everyone knows [^lilly1903][^spaeth1940]. In 1940 the song historian Sigmund Spaeth called it the barbershop quartet's "national anthem" [^spaeth1940], and the women's barbershop society founded in Tulsa in 1945 took the name Sweet Adelines [^okhist][^sai2015].

## Where it comes from

**The tune.** Harry Armstrong was born in Somerville, Massachusetts, in 1879 [^ascap1948][^witmark1939][^time1951]. He began the tune in 1896, as a teenager singing in a street-corner quartet; the publisher's history, built on his own account, says he was eighteen and worked in a Boston jewelry store, and Time's obituary says seventeen [^witmark1939][^time1951][^time1934]. By that history, the barbershop chord his quartet kept coming back to gave him the idea [^witmark1939]. The tune's first title varies: My Old New England Home in Spaeth's 1945 account and in Time [^rotarian1945][^time1951], Down Home in Old New England in The Etude in 1942 and in Variety's obituary of Gerard [^etude1942][^variety1948]. Armstrong moved to New York, played piano at Coney Island and in music halls, and joined the Witmark publishing house [^witmark1939][^ascap1948].

**The words.** Richard H. Gerard was born in 1876; his real surname was Husch [^ascap1948][^variety1948][^loc-gerard][^witmark1939]. He was a clerk in the New York post office [^spaeth1940][^time1934]. His first line was "You're the Flower of My Heart, Sweet Rosalie" [^witmark1939][^spaeth1940]. In the Witmarks' telling, publishers, their own firm among them, turned it down for years until the girl was renamed [^witmark1939].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Gerard saw a poster for the singer Adelina Patti's farewell tour and borrowed her name. Armstrong himself told Spaeth in 1945 of a Broadway sign reading "Farewell appearance, Adelina Patti" [^rotarian1945]. What the earlier record shows: in the publisher's history of 1939, Gerard says he took the name from Patti, with no sign or poster, and kept "Adeline" rather than "Adeleen" so that it would rhyme with "pine" [^witmark1939][^time1934]. Spaeth, in 1940, said the name was changed chiefly for its long, singable last syllable [^spaeth1940].

**The sheet music.** Our melody is read from a copy in the Lilly Library at Indiana University, printed from the original plates (plate 6003) in 1914 or later, with no renewal notice: an advertisement on its last page of music is for a Witmark song of 1914, You Planted a Rose in the Garden of Love [^lilly1903][^wmich][^yorku]. Its cover credits the music to "Henry W. Armstrong" and page 3 to Harry Armstrong [^lilly1903]. Page 3 is headed "To Miss Emma F. Tolen", above "Copyright, MCMIII, by M. Witmark & Sons" [^lilly1903][^byu]. Witmark's first catalogue notice of the song said: "The refrain is of the responsive sort, making it valuable for choruses" [^witmark1939].

![[1]]

**The minstrel stage.** The cover's top line reads "Sung with Great Success by William R. Moore with Haverly's Mastodon Minstrels" [^lilly1903]. The showman J. H. Haverly had founded his large touring Mastodon company in Chicago in October 1878 [^rice1911][^paskman1928]. His own last company began its season in 1898 [^rice1911], and by 1901 the troupe under his name belonged to W. E. Nankeville [^gazette1901]. In December 1903 a Reno paper listed a "William Moore, the famous Canadian tenor" among its singers [^nsj1903]. Blackface minstrelsy was a stage show in which white performers darkened their faces to mock Black people. The song's words have nothing of that stage, but it was sold partly through it.

**The first hit.** By Armstrong's own accounts, the song sat on a stockroom shelf for a year until a quartet from Philadelphia, the Quaker City Four, took it up and sang it at Hammerstein's Victoria theatre in New York, where it was a hit at once [^witmark1939][^etude1942][^rotarian1945]. Edison and Columbia recorded it on cylinders in 1904; the Edison duet by Albert C. Campbell and James F. Harrison is free to hear in the UC Santa Barbara Library's archive [^ucsb4243][^ucsb10500]. In Boston, John F. Fitzgerald sang it as he campaigned; he was mayor from 1906 and again from 1910 to 1914 [^witmark1939][^nps].

**Barbershop.** This kind of close harmony grew among Black singers in the late 1800s [^bhs-roots][^carnegie]. The poet James Weldon Johnson remembered in 1925 that in the South "every barber shop had its quartet", and that "barber-shop harmonies" spread "first on the minstrel stage, then in vaudeville", before white young men took up "harmonizing" too [^johnson1925]. Among quartets, Black and white, Sweet Adeline became the standard [^spaeth1940][^carnegie]. In 1938 men in Tulsa, Oklahoma, led by O. C. Cash, founded the Society for the Preservation and Encouragement of Barber Shop Quartet Singing in America, which kept Black singers out until the early 1960s [^okhist][^spaeth1940][^carnegie][^fhc]. In July 1945 Edna Mae Anderson gathered women in Tulsa to sing in quartets, the start of Sweet Adelines; one history gives 1947, the year of its first national convention [^okhist][^sai2015][^carnegie]. From 1957 Sweet Adelines, too, admitted only white women, until it removed the restriction from its bylaws in 1966, by its own history; a Carnegie Hall history gives 1967 [^sai-dei][^carnegie]. In 2026 Sweet Adelines International became SingUnited International [^singunited][^region21].

**The writers.** A photograph in the publisher's history shows Armstrong and Gerard at the signing of the copyright renewal on 20 November 1930 [^witmark1939][^byu]. Gerard died in 1948 [^loc-gerard][^variety1948], and Armstrong died in the Bronx, New York, in 1951 [^loc-armstrong][^time1951].

## What the words mean

Witmark's first catalogue notice called the story "pathetic", meaning full of pathos [^witmark1939]: it is a lament for a love that is gone.

1. **Verse 1.** Alone in the evening, the singer daydreams of days gone by. A picture keeps coming to him "in fancy", in his imagination [^webster-fancy], of the time his love was near. He wonders where she is and whether her heart is still the same; the sighing wind and the nightingale seem to breathe her name.
2. **Verse 2.** He sees her smiling face from their walks by the brook, so real that he wakes to find it gone, "a dream gone by". If they meet again "in after years", in later years, he trusts her love will still be his; then, though clouds hang over his sad heart, the sun would shine again.
3. **Chorus.** At night, "dear heart", he pines for her: he longs and wastes away with longing [^webster-pine]. Her face shines through all his dreams, and she is the flower of his heart.

### The full words

These are both verses and the chorus as printed in the Witmark sheet music (copyright 1903) in the Lilly Library copy, pages 3 to 5, with the printed spellings "'oft", "'till", "Tho'" and "hov'ring". The verses share one tune, and the chorus is printed once, after them. The song was published in 1903 and both writers died by 1951, so all of it is in the public domain.

**Verse 1**
In the evening when I sit alone a-dreaming
Of days gone by love to me so dear,
There's a picture that in fancy 'oft appearing,
Brings back the time love when you were near;
It is then I wonder where you are my darling,
And if your heart to me is still the same,
For the sighing wind and nightingale a-singing
Are breathing only your own sweet name.

**Verse 2**
I can see your smiling face as when we wandered
Down by the brookside just you and I,
And it seems so real at times 'till I awaken,
To find all vanished a dream gone by;
If we meet sometime in after years my darling,
I trust that I will find your love still mine,
Tho' my heart is sad and clouds above are hov'ring
The sun again love for me would shine.

**Chorus**
Sweet Adeline,
My Adeline
At night, Dear heart
For you I pine,
In all my dreams,
Your fair face beams,
You're the flower of my heart,
Sweet Adeline.

## How it is sung

Sweet Adeline was sold as a ballad for voice and piano; in its printed chorus the piano fills the gaps under the voice's long notes with short moving lines [^lilly1903]. Quartets fill those gaps with voices. Spaeth called it the model of the "echo" style, in which one voice sings a phrase and the others answer it in harmony; the opening "Sweet Adeline", he wrote, belongs to the lead alone or to all in unison [^spaeth1940][^spaeth1926].

![[2]]

Our arrangement sings the chorus only, every pitch and rhythm as printed in the Lilly Library copy, in B-flat and 4/4 [^lilly1903]. All four parts sing the words together in close harmony rather than echoing: the soprano carries the tune, the alto and tenor sing close under it, and the bass walks the roots. For 2 to 4 singers, it adds the bass to soprano and alto, then the tenor. The print's fermatas and grace notes are left out, and short breaths are written in after "line", "pine" and "beams". The chords include dominant sevenths on D, C and F, and near the end a C minor seventh that turns into C7 on "heart".

## Versions and other words

- Armstrong wrote a sequel with Clarence Gaskill, I Love You Just the Same, Sweet Adeline, recorded for Edison by the American Quartet and issued in 1920 [^ucsb6310][^ascap1948].
- A later Witmark printing, marked "Copyright Renewed", offers arrangements for men's (TTBB), women's (SSAA) and mixed (SATB) voices, among others [^byu].
- Jerome Kern's musical comedy Sweet Adeline borrowed the song's title [^witmark1939][^goldberg1930].

## Did you know?

- Spaeth heard How Dry I Am and Sweet Adeline as two versions of the Westminster chime, Big Ben's four-note pattern, one starting at the bottom and the other at the top; so, he wrote, a session that starts with How Dry I Am should end with Sweet Adeline [^spaeth1940].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/sweet-adeline/soprano): you carry the tune, from the E above middle C to a high E-flat. "Sweet Ad-e-line" falls from D to C, drops a fifth to F, and rises to a B-flat held for two and a half beats; most little lines end on a long note like it. In "My Adeline" lean the F-sharp up into G. Your top E-flat is "of" in "flower of my heart", your lowest E is "I" in "For you I pine", and the last "Ad-e-line" has a quick "e" before a three-beat "line".
- [Alto](/songs/sweet-adeline/alto): you sing close under the tune. Through "Sweet Adeline, My Ad-" and "In all my dreams, Your fair" you hold D while the chords change around you, then step down to C and B-natural. "For you I pine" drops to your lowest note, A. At the end you move by half steps, E-flat on "of my", E on "heart, Sweet", E-flat on "Ad-e-" and D on "line": tune each one.
- [Tenor](/songs/sweet-adeline/tenor): you sing in the middle, from the F below middle C to the E-flat above it, mostly on B-flat and F. In "My Ad-e-line" you climb F, F-sharp, A, G: the half step into F-sharp slides into the D7 chord. On "pine" you rise to E-flat, the seventh of F7, just under the tune's F. From "the flower" you sit on B-flat, and you end on C, A and a held F.
- [Bass](/songs/sweet-adeline/bass): you walk the roots, mostly B-flat, C and F. You rise from B-flat to D on "Ad-e-" and drop to your lowest note, low G, on "line" and again on "beams". "At night, dear heart" and "For you I pine" leap from C up to F, and you end on a held low B-flat.

## Questions

### Why is Sweet Adeline the barbershop song?

Because its chorus is built for answering: each short line ends on a long note that other voices can echo in close harmony [^spaeth1940][^lilly1903]. By 1940 Spaeth called it the barbershop "national anthem" [^spaeth1940]. When women in Tulsa formed their own barbershop society in 1945, it was named Sweet Adelines [^okhist][^sai2015].

### What is Sweet Adeline about?

A lost love. In the verses a man alone in the evening remembers walks by a brook with a sweetheart who is gone, and hopes they will meet again; in the chorus she fills his dreams and is "the flower of my heart" [^lilly1903]. Even sung by a cheerful crowd, it is a song of longing.

### Who first sang Sweet Adeline?

Armstrong's own accounts say the Quaker City Four, a quartet from Philadelphia, first sang it at Hammerstein's Victoria theatre in New York, where it was a hit at once [^witmark1939][^etude1942][^rotarian1945]. The cover of one printing credits William R. Moore, a singer with Haverly's Mastodon Minstrels [^lilly1903].
