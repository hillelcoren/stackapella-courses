For Unto Us a Child Is Born is the chorus from Part One of Messiah, George Frideric Handel's oratorio of 1741, that sings the prophet Isaiah's words about a child: "For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given: and the government shall be upon his shoulder: and his name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace", Isaiah 9:6 in the King James Bible [^kjv][^wordbook1750]. Handel wrote it in London in August 1741, reworking an Italian duet he had finished on 3 July [^tobin][^vickers2023]. It moves between light runs on "born" and four loud shouts of "Wonderful, Counsellor" by all four parts [^prout1902]. We sing the whole chorus in Handel's own four parts, in his key, G, for four singers.

## Where it comes from

**The words.** Messiah's words are verses from the Bible, chosen and ordered by Charles Jennens, a rich amateur of music, born in 1700 into a family of Gopsall, in Leicestershire [^dnb-jennens][^dnb-handel]. On 10 July 1741 he wrote to his friend Edward Holdsworth that he hoped Handel would set "another Scripture Collection I have made for him": "The Subject is Messiah" [^vickers2023][^jhcorr]. In Part One Jennens moves from Isaiah's prophecies of comfort to the birth: the bass sings Isaiah 9:2, "The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light", and the chorus answers with verse 6, before the shepherds hear the angel's news [^wordbook1750][^kjv][^vickers2023].

**The music, and the duet behind it.** Handel began Messiah on 22 August 1741 and finished Part One, this chorus with it, on 28 August [^grove1880][^vickers2023][^autograph]. He did not start from nothing. On 3 July 1741, seven weeks earlier, he had dated an Italian chamber duet for two voices, No, di voi non vo' fidarmi ("No, I will not trust you"), HWV 189,, and its music became the model for this chorus and for a second Messiah chorus, "All we like sheep have gone astray" [^tobin][^vickers2023]. Handel often reused his own music: the editor of the Halle Handel Edition traces two more Messiah choruses to another duet, dated 1 July 1741 [^tobin][^vickers2023]. Some critics have thought the duet tunes fitted their Italian words better than Scripture, among them Edward Dent in 1934, as that editor reports [^tobin].

**Dublin and London.** Messiah was first performed in Dublin, in the Musick Hall in Fishamble Street, at noon on Tuesday 13 April 1742, for prisoners' relief, Mercer's Hospital and the Charitable Infirmary [^townsend1852][^grove1880]. Ladies were asked to leave their hooped skirts at home and gentlemen their swords, to make room for more people [^townsend1852][^schoelcher1857], and above 700 came [^townsend1852][^vickers2023]. Faulkner's Dublin Journal wrote that "Words are wanting to express the exquisite Delight it afforded" [^townsend1852][^schoelcher1857]. London heard it at the Covent Garden theatre on 23 March 1743, billed as "A New Sacred Oratorio" [^grove1880][^vickers2023][^tobin]. The London wordbook of 1750 that we read prints this chorus on its page 6, under the motto Jennens chose for the title page, "Majora canamus", "let us sing of greater things" [^wordbook1750][^vickers2023].

**The Foundling Hospital.** From 1750 to the end of his life Handel gave Messiah every spring in the chapel of the Foundling Hospital in London, a home for abandoned babies, to raise money for it [^grove1880][^dnb-handel][^tobin]. He died on 14 April 1759, leaving the hospital "a fair copy of the score, and all the parts" of Messiah [^dnb-handel][^brownlow1847].

**In print, Mozart and Prout.** The first complete printed score came out in London in 1767, from Randall and Abell, the successors of Handel's publisher Walsh [^prout1902][^vickers2023]. In March 1789 Mozart rescored Messiah for Baron Gottfried van Swieten's concerts in Vienna; it was first heard at Count Johann Esterházy's on 6 March 1789 and printed in Leipzig in 1803, with changes by Johann Adam Hiller [^koechel1862][^holschneider][^imslp-k572]. In that score much of this chorus is given to solo voices; Ebenezer Prout noted in 1902 that in England it was "invariably sung as Handel intended", by the whole choir [^prout1902]. Prout's edition went back to Handel's manuscript and corrected the mistakes printed since Walsh's day; it is the edition we sing from [^prout1902][^dnb-prout].

## What the words mean

In Isaiah's book this verse closes a passage that begins in darkness: "The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light" [^kjv]. The prophet then tells why: a child is born. The verse after it promises that "Of the increase of his government and peace there shall be no end, upon the throne of David" [^kjv]. Christians have long read the child as Christ, and Jennens places the verse just before the angel tells the shepherds of Jesus's birth [^wordbook1750][^vickers2023].

**"For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given."** The two halves say the same thing in two ways: a child has been born for us, a son given to us as a gift.

**"And the government shall be upon his shoulder."** He will carry the rule: the authority and the weight of governing will rest on him.

**"And his name shall be called."** Then come his names, each a title. "Wonderful" means marvellous, full of wonder. A "Counsellor" is an adviser, one who gives wise counsel. "The mighty God" names him with God's power, "The everlasting Father" as one who cares for his people for ever, and "The Prince of Peace" as a ruler whose reign brings peace.

### The full words

This is the chorus as the London wordbook of 1750 prints it, with its capitals and the long s made modern [^wordbook1750]. It is the King James text of Isaiah 9:6, with the same words in the same order [^kjv]. Handel repeats every phrase many times; the page shows the words as we sing them.

For unto us a Child is born,
unto us a Son is given,
and the Government shall be upon his Shoulder,
and his Name shall be called,
Wonderful, Counsellor, the mighty God,
the everlasting Father, the Prince of Peace.

## How it is sung

It is the twelfth number of Messiah in Prout's edition, after the bass air "The people that walked in darkness" and before the Pastoral Symphony and the shepherds [^prout1902][^wordbook1750]. Handel gave Messiah in spring, but this chorus, with its child "born", is now just as often sung at Christmas: in Boston, the Handel and Haydn Society has sung Messiah "every year since 1854 at Christmastide without interruption", by its own account [^hh].

The chorus is built from two kinds of music [^prout1902]. One is the duet's light, dancing tune, "For unto us a Child is born", sung by one part and then another, with long running semiquavers on "born" and a dotted, lilting "and the government shall be upon His shoulder". The other is the block of all four parts together, loud, shouting "Wonderful, Counsellor, The Mighty God, The Everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace" in full chords. Handel sets that block four times. In Handel's Dublin score most of the chorus is accompanied by only part of the string band, and the full band comes in for "Wonderful"; Prout marks it so, and saves his organ for those same moments [^prout1902][^tobin].

Our arrangement is read from Prout's full score of 1902, No. 12, "Andante Allegro", crotchet = 76, in G major [^prout1902]. We sing the whole chorus in Handel's four voice parts, in his key, without the orchestra: its six bars of introduction and its closing bars are left out, and every rest between the voices' entries is kept. We take it a little faster, at 80, so the longest lines fit in a breath, and each long run on "born" is broken once for a quick breath, the next note sung again on the vowel. At the start of bar 82 Handel's manuscript has two notes for the alto, and it is unclear which he crossed out; Prout chose A, and so do we [^prout1902]. It lasts about four and a quarter minutes, for four singers only, because the soprano starts alone and the others enter one by one.

## Versions and other words

Mozart's arrangement of 1789, printed in 1803 as Der Messias, sets Handel's music to German words and gives a large part of this chorus to solo singers [^prout1902][^holschneider]. Handel's duet, for two voices, keeps its own Italian words [^tobin][^vickers2023].

## Did you know?

- The same Italian duet of July 1741 gave Messiah two choruses: this one and "All we like sheep have gone astray", in Part Two [^tobin][^vickers2023].
- At the first "Wonderful" Prout's score is marked "ff", very loud, and "con Rip.", the whole string band, while the violins break into running semiquavers under the choir's shout. For most of the chorus the strings are marked "senza Rip.", only a few players [^prout1902][^tobin].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/for-unto-us-a-child-is-born/soprano): you start the chorus alone with Handel's tune and have the first long run on "born", with your one top A, a quick note at the top of it. You sing most often on D, then C, A and B, and your low E comes only on "us" and "a" in your first "unto us a Son is given". Take the written breath in the run and come back in lightly.
- [Alto](/songs/for-unto-us-a-child-is-born/alto): you come in third with the tune, in D, and on the last "born" you run in thirds under the soprano. You sing most often on G, then F-sharp and A. Your top C comes on "His" in your second "be upon His shoulder" and on two quick notes of your last run, and your low A at the end of that same "shoulder".
- [Tenor](/songs/for-unto-us-a-child-is-born/tenor): you come in second with the tune, an octave under the soprano. You sing most often on D, then E and C, and reach your top G often, on "His" in "be upon His shoulder", on "E-" and "ver-" of your first "Everlasting", and on "Won-" and "Coun-" in the later "Wonderful, Counsellor". Your low E comes on "us" and "a" in your first "unto us a Son is given".
- [Bass](/songs/for-unto-us-a-child-is-born/bass): you come in last with the tune, an octave under the alto, and your first run on "born" climbs to your top E. In the first and third "Wonderful, Counsellor" you leap down an octave on both "Won-der-ful" and "Coun-sel-lor". You sing most often on G, then D and C, and end on your low G, on the last word, "Peace".

## Questions

### What Bible verse is For Unto Us a Child Is Born?

Isaiah 9:6, in the King James Version: "For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given: and the government shall be upon his shoulder: and his name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace" [^kjv]. Charles Jennens set it in Messiah right after Isaiah 9:2, "The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light" [^wordbook1750][^kjv].

### Who wrote For Unto Us a Child Is Born?

George Frideric Handel wrote the music in London in August 1741 for his oratorio Messiah, reworking his own Italian duet No, di voi non vo' fidarmi, HWV 189, which he had dated 3 July 1741 [^tobin][^vickers2023][^grove1880]. The words are the prophet Isaiah's, in the King James Bible, chosen for Messiah by Charles Jennens [^kjv][^dnb-jennens].

### Which part sings first in For Unto Us?

The soprano, alone. The sopranos sing the opening phrase alone; the tenors enter with the same tune an octave lower, and the soprano's first long run follows; then the altos and last the basses take it up in D, the key a fifth above Handel's G [^prout1902]. All four meet at the first "Wonderful, Counsellor".
