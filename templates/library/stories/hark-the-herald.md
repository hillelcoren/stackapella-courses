Hark! The Herald Angels Sing is an English Christmas hymn. Its words began as Charles Wesley's "Hymn for Christmas-Day", printed in London in 1739 with a different first line: "Hark how all the welkin rings" [^duke-hsp][^julian]. The opening everyone knows was written by the preacher George Whitefield, who printed it in 1753 [^duke-hsp][^julian]. The tune is Felix Mendelssohn's, written in 1840 for a festival in Leipzig honouring Gutenberg and the invention of printing; an English organist, William H. Cummings, fitted it to Wesley's words in the 1850s [^mt1897][^lightwood]. By 1907 the Dictionary of Hymnology called this altered text the most popular form of the hymn, in common use in all English-speaking countries [^julian].

## Where it comes from

**The words.** In 1739, at the start of the Methodist revival, the brothers John and Charles Wesley published a book of verse in London, Hymns and Sacred Poems [^duke-hsp][^julian]. The book bore both brothers' names but did not say who wrote which hymn; this one is Charles's [^duke-hsp][^julian][^canterbury]. His "Hymn for Christmas-Day" had ten verses of four lines [^duke-hsp][^julian][^canterbury]. It began with the angels' song ringing across the sky: "Hark how all the welkin rings, / 'Glory to the King of kings'" [^hsp1739][^duke-hsp][^kjv]. "Welkin" is an old, poetic word for the sky, the vault of heaven [^webster][^summerly].

![[1]]

**The changes.** George Whitefield, a leading Methodist preacher of Calvinist views, put it in his Collection of Hymns for Social Worship in 1753 [^summerly][^nge][^duke-hsp][^julian]. He rewrote the first two lines as "Hark! the herald angels sing / Glory to the new-born King!", made smaller changes, and left out Wesley's eighth and tenth verses [^duke-hsp][^julian][^hymnarchive]. In 1760 Martin Madan's Collection of Psalms and Hymns kept Whitefield's opening but went back to Wesley elsewhere, except for two lines. Wesley had written "Universal nature say / 'Christ the Lord is born to day!'", and Whitefield had made them "Nature rise and worship him, / Who is born at Bethlehem". Madan gave "With th' angelic host proclaim, / 'Christ is born in Bethlehem!'" [^madan][^julian][^hymnarchive][^wf1753]. By 1782 a supplement of hymns printed with Tate and Brady's New Version of the Psalms had made it three verses of eight lines, with its first two lines repeated after each as a refrain [^julian][^hymnarchive][^hymnary-text].

![[2]]

**The tune.** In June 1840 Leipzig held a festival for Gutenberg and what was taken to be the 400th anniversary of printing [^summerly][^praise]. Mendelssohn wrote a Festgesang, a "festival song", for men's voices and brass, and it was sung in the open air on 24 June 1840 [^mt1897][^imslp]. Its second number, headed simply "Lied", song, is our tune [^mt1897][^imslp].

**The pairing.** William H. Cummings, a young organist at Waltham Abbey, was playing through the Festgesang when he saw that its second chorus would carry Wesley's hymn [^mt1897][^lightwood][^grove]. By his account to the Musical Times, the congregation there sang it "with great enthusiasm", and so many people asked for copies that he took it to the publishers Ewer and Co., who printed it in 1856 [^mt1897][^summerly]. Lightwood's history of hymn tunes dates the adaptation to 1855, Hymnary.org to 1856 [^lightwood][^hymnary-tune].

![[3]]

Which hymnal printed the pair first is disputed: the Musical Times believed in 1897 that it was Hymns Ancient and Modern in 1861, while Hymnology Archive and the Praise Trust name an earlier one, Richard Chope's Congregational Hymn and Tune Book [^mt1897][^hymnarchive][^praise]. Either way, Hymns Ancient and Modern fixed the pairing [^hymnary-tune][^hymnarchive][^praise]. It printed the hymn as No. 43 in 1861, the tune listed as "Mendelsohn", and as No. 60, MENDELSSOHN, in its standard edition of 1875 [^ham1861][^ham1875].

![[4]]

**Myth and record.** Often told: Mendelssohn objected to his tune being sung to this hymn. What the record shows: Mendelssohn died in 1847, years before Cummings's arrangement [^hymnary-mendelssohn][^mt1897][^summerly]. In a letter dated 30 April 1843, printed in part in the Musical Times in December 1897, he wrote to his English publisher, E. Buxton, about an English translation of the Festgesang and asked for other words for No. 2. The piece would be liked, he wrote, "but it will never do to sacred words. There must be a national and merry subject found out" [^mt1897]. The Musical Times noted that Buxton had owned Ewer and Co., and that Cummings had known nothing of Mendelssohn's view [^mt1897].

## What the words mean

We sing the first verse and refrain of the three-verse form, made in stages. Its first two lines are Whitefield's of 1753, the next four Wesley's of 1739, the last two Madan's of 1760, and the refrain repeats the first two, as hymnals did by 1782 [^duke-hsp][^madan][^julian][^hymnarchive]. The three verses are Wesley's first six of ten, two to a verse [^julian][^ham1875].

**Verse 1, the one we sing.** "Hark!" means listen, and a herald proclaimed news, so the "herald angels" are the angels who announce the birth [^webster]. The scene is Luke's Gospel: a multitude of the "heavenly host", a great company of angels, appear to shepherds and sing "Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace" [^kjv]. "God and sinners reconciled": God and people, at odds, are made friends again, as in Paul's "God was in Christ, reconciling the world unto himself" [^kjv]. All nations are called to rise and join "the triumph of the skies", heaven's celebration. Where Madan has "Christ is born in Bethlehem", Wesley had all of nature say "Christ the Lord is born to day", close to the angel's "unto you is born this day ... Christ the Lord" [^duke-hsp][^kjv].

**Verse 2.** Christ comes "late in time", after long waiting. "Veiled in flesh the Godhead see": God hidden in a human body, "incarnate", made flesh. "Emmanuel" is Matthew's name meaning "God with us" [^kjv]. Wesley wrote "Pleas'd as man with men t' appear"; "Pleased as Man with man to dwell" is in John Kempthorne's collection of 1810, perhaps earlier, and Hymns Ancient and Modern made it the usual reading [^duke-hsp][^julian][^hymnarchive].

**Verse 3.** Christ is hailed as Isaiah's "Prince of Peace" and Malachi's "Sun of righteousness", risen "with healing in his wings" [^kjv]. He lays his glory by, setting heaven's glory aside, and is born to give people "second birth", the new birth Jesus speaks of in John's Gospel [^kjv].

**The verses left out.** Wesley's last four verses turn to the singer, asking the "Desire of nations", Haggai's title, to make a home in us, and the "Second Adam", Paul's name for Christ, to restore what Adam lost [^duke-hsp][^kjv]. Whitefield dropped two of them, and the three-verse form all four [^julian][^hymnarchive].

### The full words

The three verses and refrain as Hymns Ancient and Modern printed them in its standard edition of 1875, Hymn 60, the page our arrangement is read from, with small capitals set as ordinary letters and its singing marks and "Amen" left out [^ham1875]. They are Wesley's first six verses of 1739 with Whitefield's opening of 1753, Madan's lines of 1760 and later small changes [^duke-hsp][^madan][^julian]. Wesley's last four verses follow as the 1739 book printed them, the long s set as s, and its slip "the the" in the last verse kept as printed [^hsp1739]. Everyone who wrote or changed these words died before 1840, so all of them are in the public domain.

**Verse 1**
Hark! the herald-angels sing
Glory to the new-born King,
Peace on earth, and mercy mild,
God and sinners reconciled.
Joyful, all ye nations, rise,
Join the triumph of the skies;
With the Angelic host proclaim,
"Christ is born in Bethlehem."
Hark! the herald-angels sing
Glory to the new-born King.

**Verse 2**
Christ, by highest Heav'n adored,
Christ, the Everlasting Lord,
Late in time behold Him come,
Offspring of a Virgin's womb.
Veil'd in flesh the Godhead see!
Hail, the Incarnate Deity!
Pleased as Man with man to dwell,
Jesus, our Emmanuel.
Hark! the herald-angels sing
Glory to the new-born King.

**Verse 3**
Hail, the heaven-born Prince of peace!
Hail, the Sun of righteousness!
Light and life to all He brings,
Risen with healing in His wings.
Mild He lays His glory by,
Born that man no more may die,
Born to raise the sons of earth,
Born to give them second birth.
Hark! the herald-angels sing
Glory to the new-born King.

**Wesley's verse 7, 1739**
Come, Desire of Nations come,
Fix in Us thy humble Home,
Rise, the Woman's Conqu'ring Seed,
Bruise in Us the Serpent's Head.

**Wesley's verse 8, 1739**
Now display Thy saving Pow'r,
Ruin'd Nature now restore,
Now in Mystic Union join
Thine to Ours, and Ours to Thine.

**Wesley's verse 9, 1739**
Adam's Likeness, Lord, efface,
Stamp thy Image in its Place,
Second Adam from above,
Reinstate us in thy Love.

**Wesley's verse 10, 1739**
Let us Thee, tho' lost, regain,
Thee, the Life, the the Inner Man:
O! to All Thyself impart,
Form'd in each Believing Heart.

## How it is sung

It is a Christmas hymn, printed among the Christmas hymns of Hymns Ancient and Modern, and sometimes sung without its refrain [^ham1875][^julian]. Its meter is 7.7.7.7 D with refrain: eight lines of seven syllables, then the two-line refrain [^hymnary-text][^ham1875]. In both 1861 and 1875 the hymnal asked for it in unison, everyone on the tune, except the ninth line, the refrain's first [^ham1861][^ham1875].

Our arrangement reads the four parts of the hymnal's 1875 standard edition, in G as printed, its minims written as crotchets in 4/4 [^ham1875]. We sing the first verse and refrain, every line in parts, with some notes of the lower three parts changed so each moves easily and the bass an octave lower where it climbs too high for a beginner. Where the hymnal prints the refrain for organ, we sing its own parts for the same music two lines before. Every line is one breath. The 1875 text reads "With the Angelic host", but the line has seven notes, so we sing "With th'angelic", as Madan printed it [^ham1875][^madan].

## Versions and other words

- **Wesley's own opening:** the 1904 edition of Hymns Ancient and Modern brought back "Hark! how all the welkin rings" as its first lines and refrain. Its 1906 edition restored the familiar text beside Wesley's [^julian-supp][^hymnarchive].
- **Another tune:** in 1861 Hymns Ancient and Modern offered a second tune, "Batchelor", after Mendelssohn's [^ham1861].
- **Jesus, the Light of the World:** Wesley's verses with a gospel refrain, printed in 1890 by George D. Elderkin as their arranger, to the tune WE'LL WALK IN THE LIGHT. Hymnary.org credits the refrain to Elderkin, while Hymnology Archive traces its words to a Fanny Crosby song of 1880. It is in many African American hymnals, among them the African American Heritage Hymnal of 2001 [^hymnary-light][^hymnarchive].

## Did you know?

- In 1897 the Musical Times, siding with Cummings, complained that Hymns Ancient and Modern had left out his name and added a note Mendelssohn never wrote: a B on the third beat of the twelfth bar [^mt1897]. Counted as the hymnal prints the tune, two bars to a line, the twelfth bar is "of the skies" in both 1861 and 1875, and our soprano, read from the 1875 hymnal, sings that B on "skies" before stepping down to A [^ham1861][^ham1875].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/hark-the-herald/soprano): Mendelssohn's tune, from the low D of the opening "Hark!" to the top E on "With th'angelic" and the refrain's "Hark! the herald". Its repeated high Ds on "Glory to", "Joyful, all" and "Join the tri-" should stay light, so the E has somewhere to go.
- [Alto](/songs/hark-the-herald/alto): starts on the tune's D and holds it through "Hark! the herald an-" while the tune climbs, then sings mostly a third or a sixth below. Tune the sharpened notes: C-sharp on "reconciled", G-sharp on "proclaim" and the refrain's "angels". The last "King" drops to a low B, the part's lowest note.
- [Tenor](/songs/hark-the-herald/tenor): the middle of the chord, mostly on D, B and E. It holds A on "Joyful, all" and "Join the tri-" under the tune's high D, and holds its top E through "With th'angelic host" and the refrain's "Hark! the herald an-": keep it steady as the others move.
- [Bass](/songs/hark-the-herald/bass): the roots, from the low G of the opening. It holds D under "Joyful, all" and "Join the", then C under "With th'angelic host" before climbing through E to A on "proclaim"; the refrain does the same climb on "angels sing". The last "King" is the high G, an octave above where it began.

## Questions

### What does Hark the Herald Angels Sing mean?

It means "Listen! The angels who bring the news are singing." "Hark" is an old word for listen, and a herald is one who proclaims news [^webster]. The angels are those of Luke's Gospel, who appear to shepherds near Bethlehem and sing "Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace" [^kjv].

### What does Harold mean in Hark the Herald Angels Sing?

There is no Harold in it: the word is "herald", which sounds the same. A herald was an officer who proclaimed war or peace and carried a commander's messages [^webster]. Here the heralds are angels announcing Christ's birth. The line is George Whitefield's of 1753; Wesley's began "Hark how all the welkin rings", the welkin being the sky [^duke-hsp][^julian][^webster].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings Mendelssohn's tune in our arrangement. With two singers you have the tune and the alto; with three, add the bass; with four, the tenor fills the middle. The tune runs from the low D of the opening "Hark!" to the top E on "With th'angelic" and the refrain's "Hark! the herald".
