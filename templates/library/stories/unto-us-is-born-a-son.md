Unto Us Is Born a Son is the English form of Puer nobis nascitur, "a boy is born to us", a Latin Christmas song whose oldest copy cited today is in a German choir book of about 1360 [^canterbury-puer][^hymnary-text][^lmu]. Its tune, PUER NOBIS, is sung in the form printed in Piae Cantiones, a songbook for the church and schools of Sweden and Finland printed at Greifswald in 1582 [^woodward][^canterbury-pc][^hymnary-tune]. George Ratcliffe Woodward, an English priest, made its English words, printed as No. 25 in the enlarged Cowley Carol Book of 1902 [^cowley1912][^canterbury-boy][^woodward]. Each verse ends by singing its last line twice, "Of lords the Lord eternal", just as the Latin sings "Dominus dominorum" twice in 1582 [^cowley1912][^woodward]. Another English version, Unto Us a Boy Is Born, followed in 1928 [^obc][^canterbury-boy].

## Where it comes from

**A song in place of a blessing.** At the end of the Mass on some days, and at the close of every Hour of the church's daily prayer, a singer gave the words Benedicamus Domino, "Let us bless the Lord", and the answer came back Deo gratias, "Thanks be to God" [^ce-deo][^ce-ite]. At Christmas, Puer nobis nascitur was sung at that moment. Woodward, who edited Piae Cantiones in 1910, begins his note on it "At Christmas instead of Benedicamus Domino" [^woodward]. The oldest copy that the Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology and Hymnary.org cite is in a gradual, a choir book for the Mass, made at Moosburg in Germany around 1360 [^canterbury-puer][^hymnary-text][^lmu]. Its last verse still ends with those words: "Benedicamus Domino".

**Many versions.** The Oxford Book of Carols and Woodward both start its written history with a fifteenth-century manuscript at Trier, which the hymn scholar Mone printed in his collection of Latin hymns [^obc][^woodward]. Woodward listed many more copies, in manuscripts and in printed books, and took the great number of variations as proof of how widely it was sung; among them is J. Spangenberg's songbook of 1544, which the Oxford book says printed a German translation, Uns ist geborn ein Kindelein [^woodward][^obc]. The number and order of the verses change from copy to copy [^woodward].

**Piae Cantiones, 1582.** The form we sing comes from Piae Cantiones, "pious songs", edited by Theodoricus Petri of Nyland, a Finn studying at Rostock, and printed at Greifswald, then part of the kingdom of Sweden, in 1582 [^woodward][^canterbury-pc][^obc]. Puer nobis nascitur is No. XI among its Christmas songs, in five verses [^woodward][^canterbury-puer]. In Woodward's reprint the tune is a single line of diamond-shaped notes with the first verse under it and the other four printed below; it ends by singing "Dominus dominorum" twice [^woodward]. Woodward wrote that its mode, the Lydian with B flat, is "practically the modern major scale" [^woodward]. A copy of the book reached England around 1852 or early 1853, brought by the British envoy at Stockholm and given to John Mason Neale, who with Thomas Helmore made their Carols for Christmas-tide of 1853 from its tunes; the tune of Good King Wenceslas is one of them [^obc][^woodward].

**Which century?** The Cowley Carol Book heads the carol "Words and tune (XIV Cent.) from Piae Cantiones" [^cowley1912][^cowley1922]. When Woodward wrote his notes in 1910, every manuscript he listed was from the fifteenth century, and the Oxford Book of Carols also begins with Trier [^woodward][^obc]. The Moosburg gradual of about 1360, cited today by the Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology and Hymnary.org, fits the heading's fourteenth century [^canterbury-puer][^hymnary-text][^lmu].

**The English words, 1902.** Woodward was born at Birkenhead in 1848, studied at Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, and was ordained in 1874 [^canterbury-woodward][^hymnary-woodward][^praise-woodward]. He was asked to make a small book of carols for the Church of S. John the Evangelist at Cowley, G. F. Bodley's church in Oxford, and in the autumn of 1901 he put out thirty-nine carols as The Cowley Carol Book [^cowley1912][^praise-woodward][^ohct][^archiseek]. It was well received, and a revised and enlarged edition of sixty-five followed, its preface signed at Cadenabbia on 24 October 1902 [^cowley1912][^cowley1925][^hymnary-woodward]. Unto Us Is Born a Son is No. 25 there [^cowley1912][^woodward][^canterbury-boy]. Twenty-one of its carols had words by John Mason Neale and a few were Latin or old English; the rest, Woodward wrote, were his own, "for the most part, translations of Latin or German Carols", and to critics of the Latin lines left among his English he answered that "the rhythm and association of the original tongue" excused them [^cowley1912]. We have not seen the 1901 first issue, so we cannot say whether the carol was already in it; 1902 is its earliest printing we know.

**The harmony.** The page is marked "To be sung in Unison", with a four-part harmony under it, signed "Harmony by Rev. G. H. Palmer" [^cowley1912][^cowley1922]. George Herbert Palmer, born in 1846, was a priest and church musician, and he and Woodward had been curates together at St Barnabas, Pimlico, Palmer from 1876 to 1883 and Woodward from 1874 to 1882 [^canterbury-palmer][^hymnary-palmer][^praise-woodward]. In his preface Woodward thanks "especially the Rev. G. H. Palmer, and Dr. Charles Wood" for help in harmonizing the melodies, and says that Wood revised and passed the proofs of the music [^cowley1912]. Wood joined Woodward as compiler of a second series, first published in 1919, and the complete edition of 1947 names them both [^cowley1925][^cowley1950]. Palmer died in 1926 and Woodward at Highgate, London, in 1934 [^canterbury-palmer][^hymnary-palmer][^canterbury-woodward][^hymnary-woodward].

## What the words mean

Woodward follows the Latin of Piae Cantiones closely in verses 1 and 3, loosely in verses 2 and 4, and in verse 5 he goes back to the older manuscripts [^woodward][^cowley1912].

**Verse 1.** "Unto us is born a Son" is "Puer nobis nascitur", and the title also echoes Isaiah, "For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given" [^woodward][^kjv]. "King of Quires supernal" puts the Latin "Rector angelorum", ruler of the angels, into old English words: a quire is a choir, a body of singers, and supernal means heavenly, of the realms above [^woodward][^webster]. So he is king of the choirs of heaven. "Of lords the Lord eternal" is "Dominus dominorum", Lord of lords, as Christ is called in the Book of Revelation [^woodward][^kjv]. Where the older copies say the Lord "pascitur", is fed, in this world, Piae Cantiones prints "panditur", is shown; Woodward writes "See on earth His life begun" [^woodward].

**Verse 2.** "Christ, from heav'n descending low, Comes on earth a stranger", born where there was "no room for them in the inn" [^kjv]. The Latin says that, laid in a manger under the asses' hay, they knew their Lord [^woodward]. Woodward's "Ox and ass their Owner know" takes the picture from Isaiah: "The ox knoweth his owner, and the ass his master's crib" [^kjv]. "Becradled" means laid in a cradle, here the manger.

**Verse 3, which we leave out.** It tells how the news "did Herod sore affray", badly frightened him, and how he gave the word to kill "the little childer", the young children of Bethlehem, as in Matthew's Gospel [^cowley1912][^webster1913][^kjv]. The Latin says Herod feared the child with great trembling and fell upon the infants in fury [^woodward].

**Verse 4.** "Of His love and mercy mild This the Christmas story": this is the story of his gentle love. The Latin prays to the one born of Mary this very day to lead us by his grace to the joys above, and Woodward turns it into a wish: "And O that Mary's gentle Child Might lead us up to glory!" [^woodward][^cowley1912]

**Verse 5.** "O and A" are the Greek letters omega and alpha, the last and the first. In Revelation the Lord says "I am Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the ending" [^kjv]. Piae Cantiones has "Te salvator α et ω", you, Saviour, Alpha and Omega; Woodward's "O and A and A and O" and his "Cum cantibus in choro" are readings of the older copies, the Trier manuscript among them, as Woodward's own notes show [^woodward]. "Cum cantibus in choro" means "with songs in the choir", and "Benedicamus Domino" means "Let us bless the Lord", the versicle the song once stood in for [^woodward][^ce-deo]. "Let our merry organ go" is Woodward's English for the Latin's "and with the organ" [^woodward].

### Puer nobis nascitur

These are the five Latin verses as Piae Cantiones prints them, read in Woodward's reprint of 1910, with the long s written as s and æ as ae; the English is our own [^woodward]. In the music, the last line of each verse is sung twice.

> Puer nobis nascitur
A boy is born to us,
> Rector angelorum,
the ruler of the angels;
> In hoc mundo panditur
in this world is shown
> Dominus dominorum.
the Lord of lords.

> In praesepe positum
Laid in a manger
> Sub foeno asinorum,
under the asses' hay,
> Cognoverunt Dominum
they knew the Lord,
> Christum Regem coelorum.
Christ, the King of heaven.

> Hunc Herodes timuit
Herod feared him
> Magno cum tremore,
with great trembling;
> In infantes irruit,
he fell upon the infants,
> Hos caedens in furore.
cutting them down in his fury.

> Qui natus ex Maria
You who were born of Mary
> Die hodierna,
on this very day,
> Duc nos tua gratia
lead us by your grace
> Ad gaudia superna.
to the joys above.

> Te salvator α et ω
You, Saviour, Alpha and Omega,
> Cantemus in choro,
let us sing in the choir,
> Cantemus in organo,
let us sing with the organ:
> Benedicamus Domino.
let us bless the Lord.

### The full words

These are Woodward's five verses as The Cowley Carol Book prints them at No. 25, verse 1 under the music and verses 2 to 5 below it, in its 1912 impression [^cowley1912]. The music sings the last line of every verse twice. Woodward died in 1934, so all of it is in the public domain.

**Verse 1**
Unto us is born a Son,
King of Quires supernal:
See on earth His life begun,
Of lords the Lord eternal.

**Verse 2**
Christ, from heav'n descending low,
Comes on earth a stranger:
Ox and ass their Owner know
Becradled in the manger.

**Verse 3**
This did Herod sore affray,
And grievously bewilder;
So he gave the word to slay,
And slew the little childer.

**Verse 4**
Of His love and mercy mild
This the Christmas story:
And O that Mary's gentle Child
Might lead us up to glory!

**Verse 5**
O and A and A and O,
Cum cantibus in choro,
Let our merry organ go,
Benedicamus Domino.

## How it is sung

It is a Christmas carol. Woodward's note gives its old place: at Christmas, instead of the Benedicamus Domino that closed the services [^woodward][^ce-deo]. Its tune is PUER NOBIS, in 7.6.7.7, with the last line repeated: lines of seven, six, seven and seven syllables [^hymnary-tune][^hymnary-text][^cowley1912]. Each line is a short phrase: the first climbs from the keynote and returns to it, the second climbs to the high keynote and holds it, and the third starts there and comes down [^cowley1912].

The Cowley Carol Book wants it "sung in Unison", everyone on the tune, with Palmer's harmony as the accompaniment [^cowley1912]. The Oxford Book of Carols also gives its version to voices in unison, with an accompaniment [^obc].

Our arrangement is read from No. 25 of The Cowley Carol Book, in its key of D, in common time at a walking pace [^cowley1912]. We sing verses 1, 2, 4 and 5, leaving out Herod's verse to keep the song a singable length, and we sing the last line twice in every verse, as verse 1 does under the music. The soprano sings the tune. The alto, tenor and bass are Palmer's harmony made into voices, in his key and with his chords, one note to each syllable. A few of his notes are changed so that two or three singers still sound a full chord: the alto, for one, starts a third above the tune instead of on its note. A short breath is written in at the end of each line. It is for 2 to 4 singers: two take soprano and alto, a third adds the bass, and the tenor makes four.

## Versions and other words

- **Unto Us a Boy Is Born.** Percy Dearmer's translation, made for the Oxford Book of Carols in 1928, is sung to the same tune and is the better known of the two English versions, the Canterbury Dictionary says [^obc][^canterbury-boy]. The Oxford book's words follow the Trier form printed by Mone [^obc].
- **The other PUER NOBIS NASCITUR.** Michael Praetorius set Puer nobis nascitur in 1609, in part VI of his Musae Sioniae [^woodward][^hymnary-praetorius]. The tune hymn books now call PUER NOBIS NASCITUR is a reshaped, long-metre form of the old melody, 8.8.8.8, credited to Praetorius in 1609 and sung to other words, such as That Easter Day with Joy Was Bright and On Jordan's Bank the Baptist's Cry [^hymnary-praetorius]. Woodward knew that form as Geborn ist Gottes Sönelein and thought it "a corrupt following of the older melody"; he set it to No. 21 of the same Cowley book, The Son of God Is Born for All, whose heading calls the tune "a variation of Puer nobis nascitur" [^woodward][^cowley1912].

## Did you know?

- Woodward made his 1910 edition of Piae Cantiones from the copy that had come to England in the 1850s and was believed to have been given to Neale, who passed it to Helmore. In 1910 Woodward found no copy in the British Museum; by 1928 the Oxford Book of Carols said the original copy was there [^woodward][^obc].
- By 1950 the Cowley book sold No. 25 on its own, as one of seven carols offered in leaflet form with music [^cowley1950].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/unto-us-is-born-a-son/soprano): the tune, from the low D to the high E, most often on D. The first line climbs by step from D to G and back ("Unto us is born a Son"); the second climbs from A to the high D and holds it on "per-" in "supernal". The high E comes once a verse, on "on" in "See on earth" in verse 1. The last line comes twice: the first time "ter-" takes two notes, D and E, and "nal" lifts to F-sharp; the repeat starts a step higher, on G, and ends held on the low D.
- [Alto](/songs/unto-us-is-born-a-son/alto): from B to G, mostly F-sharp and D. It starts a third above the tune, on F-sharp, crosses under it through E (on "to" it sings the tune's own note), and comes back above it to F-sharp on "Son". On the repeated last line it climbs C-sharp, D, D, E, F-sharp while the tune falls A, G, F-sharp, E, D, so they cross on "e-" and the alto ends a third over the tune. Its low B comes on "ter-" the first time through the last line, held under the tune's two notes.
- [Tenor](/songs/unto-us-is-born-a-son/tenor): from low D to the D above middle C, on A almost half the time. Its top D comes in the second line, on "of Quires" in verse 1. At the end of the third line it steps down G, F-sharp, E ("life be-gun,") and leaps up a fifth to B for "Of". Before the repeated last line it drops to its low D on "Of", then goes E, B: another leap of a fifth. It ends each verse on A, the fifth of the closing chord.
- [Bass](/songs/unto-us-is-born-a-son/bass): from low G to A, mostly D and A, on the root of the chord about three quarters of the time. Its low G comes on "is" in "Unto us is born" and on "the" the first time through "Of lords the Lord". The third line climbs B, E, A ("See on earth") to its top A. The first time through the last line it sings three B's ("-ter-nal, Of"), then four A's ("lords the Lord e-"), before it lands on D to end the verse.

## Questions

### What does "King of Quires supernal" mean?

It means "King of the heavenly choirs". A quire is an old spelling of choir, a body of singers, and supernal means heavenly, from the realms above [^webster][^cowley1912]. Woodward used the phrase for the Latin "Rector angelorum", ruler of the angels, in the carol's first verse: the angels are pictured as the choirs of heaven, and the child born on earth is their king [^woodward].

### What is the translation of Puer nobis nascitur?

"Puer nobis nascitur" means "A boy is born to us". The first verse goes on: the ruler of the angels is shown in this world, the Lord of lords [^woodward]. The five Latin verses tell of the manger and the ox and ass, of Herod's fear and the killing of the children, pray the child to lead us to the joys above, and end "Benedicamus Domino", let us bless the Lord [^woodward]. Our line-by-line English is above.

### Is it the same song as "For unto us a child is born"?

No. "For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given" is a verse of the prophet Isaiah, chapter 9, verse 6 [^kjv]. Unto Us Is Born a Son is a medieval Latin Christmas song, Puer nobis nascitur, in Woodward's English of 1902 [^cowley1912][^woodward]. Its opening line echoes Isaiah's words, but the rest of it, the angels, the ox and ass, Herod and the closing "Benedicamus Domino", comes from the Latin carol, not from the prophet [^woodward][^kjv].

### Is the Praetorius Puer nobis nascitur the same tune?

They come from the same old melody, but they are not the same tune. Ours, PUER NOBIS, is the form printed in Piae Cantiones in 1582, in 7.6.7.7 with the last line repeated [^hymnary-tune][^woodward]. The tune hymn books call PUER NOBIS NASCITUR, credited to Michael Praetorius in 1609, is a reshaped form in long metre, 8.8.8.8, sung to other words [^hymnary-praetorius][^woodward]. Woodward called that form "a corrupt following of the older melody" [^woodward].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings PUER NOBIS, note for note as The Cowley Carol Book prints it [^cowley1912]. The book itself wants everyone to sing the tune together, "in Unison", with Palmer's harmony as the accompaniment; our alto, tenor and bass turn that harmony into voices [^cowley1912]. With two singers, take soprano and alto; a third adds the bass, and a fourth the tenor.
