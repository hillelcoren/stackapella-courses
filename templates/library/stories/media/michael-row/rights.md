- Licence: PD-old.
- Reasoning: title page "Entered according to Act of Congress, in the year 1867, by A. Simpson & Co."; published New

  - US: published 1867, before 1931; public domain.
  - EU and Israel: life + 70; the last editor died 1921 (1921 + 70 = 1991); public domain.
  - A faithful scan of a flat page adds no new copyright.
- Every word in the crop read: "xvi SLAVE SONGS OF THE / The same songs are used for rowing as for snouting [sic, a
  printer's error for shouting]. I know only one pure boat-song, the fine lyric, "Michael row the boat ashore" (No.
  31); and this I have no doubt is a real spiritual--it being the archangel Michael that is addressed. Among the most
  common rowing tunes were Nos. 5, 14, 17, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 36, 46. "As I have written these tunes," says
  Mr. Ware, "two measures are to be sung to each stroke, the first measure being accented by the beginning of the

- Cropped away: the rest of the page, which lists other songs by dialect titles ('Jine 'em', 'Bell da ring') and

  nothing of it shows in the crop.
- Caption sources: the words in the crop themselves (story source [^slavesongs], pp. xvi to xvii).

## 3.jpg: Slave Songs of the United States (1867), page 23, No. 31

- Item: https://archive.org/details/slavesongsofunit00alle/page/n82, the story's "original" link and [^scan31].
- Downloaded: https://archive.org/download/slavesongsofunit00alle/page/n82.jpg (2726 x 4372). Cropped to No. 31
  (offset x 150, y 1420; 2460 x 2440).
- Holder, rights statement, licence and reasoning: as for 2.jpg (same book, same scan).
- Cropped away: the top of the page, the end of No. 30 ("Bound to go"), whose words include "blow dem down" and
  "Bid 'em fare you well", and the page number "23".
- Every word in the crop read: the heading "31. MICHAEL ROW THE BOAT ASHORE.", "1. Michael row de boat a-shore,
  Hal-le-lu-jah!", "2. Mich-ael boat a gos-pel boat, Hal-le-lu-jah!", and lines 3 to 16 exactly as the story prints
  them in "The full words". These are the song's own words in the editors' spelling of Sea Island (Gullah) speech, which
  the story prints in full and glosses; no slur, no heading or advert, no figures. Faint show-through of page 24 (lines
  17 to 22, "Words from Hilton Head" and the start of No. 32 "Sail, O believer") is visible in reverse and unreadable;
  its words were read in the scan's text and nothing in it demeans.
- Caption sources: earliest known printing, page and number (story front matter and [^scan31]); E-flat and the second
  line in two parts (seen on the page; story "The page."); editors white Northerners who spelled the words as they
  heard the Sea Island speech (story "The book." and "What the words mean", [^slavesongs]).

## Considered and not used

- The Scottish Art Review (1889), p. 214 ([^sar1889]): the article's printed title uses a period term for Black people
  as its heading, and the page was not needed.
- A photograph of Coffin Point (HABS): it would show the enslavers' plantation house, a "plantation" scene the
  spirituals note rules out. No photograph is used on this page.
- Page 24 (lines 17 to 22 and the Hilton Head words): it carries No. 32 below, in dialect spelling; not needed.
