## 3.jpg: The Union Haggadah (1923), p. xii, "The Cup of Elijah"

- Item: The Union Haggadah: Home Service for the Passover, revised. Title-page verso (leaf n7), seen: "Edited and
  published by the Central Conference of American Rabbis. Copyright 1923. Printed in the United States of America".
  The passage is in the introduction, "Rites and Symbols of the Seder" (contents: p. xi), on p. xii.
- Holder: University of Florida, George A. Smathers Libraries (IA contributor; bookplate of the Sam Proctor Jewish
  Memorial Library collection); digitized 2011 with LYRASIS funding. Item:
  https://archive.org/details/unionhaggadahhom00unse ; page:
  https://archive.org/details/unionhaggadahhom00unse/page/n15/mode/1up ; file
  https://archive.org/download/unionhaggadahhom00unse/page/n15.jpg (2502 x 3695), saved as
  raw/ia-union-haggadah-1923-pxii.jpg. The final crops to the two "Cup of Elijah" paragraphs only (the "Matzo" and
  "Watercress" entries and the page number are cut away).
- Rights:
  - IA record field possible-copyright-status, exact words: "Copyright status reviewed by UF staff - Out of
    copyright".
  - US: published 1923 with notice, before 1931, so PD.
  - EU and Israel: the introduction is unsigned and the book names no individual editor; the 1923 note on its
    history (p. 158) credits only "the Committee on Revision". For an anonymous or corporate work the term is 70
    years from publication (EU Term Directive, Art. 1(3) and 1(4); Israel Copyright Act 2007, s. 40), which ended at
    the end of 1993.
  - Faithful scan of a flat page: no new rights (as above).
- Every word on the final (the page's two dashes around "among other things" written here as commas): "The Cup of Elijah. The fifth promise of God (V'hevesi) to bring Israel into Canaan, which
  follows the four promises of redemption, gave rise to the question of the need of a fifth cup of wine in the
  Seder. Popular belief left the decision of all mooted questions of law and ritual to the prophet Elijah, the
  central hero of Jewish legend. The popular mind believed this great champion of righteousness and of pure worship
  of God to be immortal, and viewed him as the coming forerunner of the Messiah, whose task it will be, among other
  things, to announce the good tidings of peace and salvation, to effect a union of hearts between parents and their
  children, to comfort the sorrowing, to raise the dead, and to establish the divine kingdom of righteousness on
  earth. / The fifth cup, the need of which was left to his decision, came to be known as the Cup of Elijah; and
  gave rise to the custom of opening the door during the Seder service, that the long expected messenger of the
  final redemption of mankind from all oppression might enter the home as a most welcome guest. Our fathers were
  thus helped, in times of darkness and persecution, to keep in mind the Messianic era of freedom, justice, and
  good-will. Stripped of its legendary form, it is still the hope for the realization of which Israel ever yearns
  and strives." A Reform rabbinical body's own explanation; nothing demeaning. "God" is printed in the 1923 text
  and stays as printed; our caption names no divine name. No figures on the crop (this page itself has no
  decoration).
- Caption facts: the story's Myth and record paragraph, "Its usual explanation is a question about a fifth cup of
  wine, which tradition says Elijah will one day settle" [^uh1923][^zivotofsky], and the page itself.

## Rejected

- The Union Haggadah, p. 63, "The door is opened for Elijah" (leaf n82): a frieze of drawn musicians across the top
  and a drawn figure in the initial. Drawn figures; rejected. The book's decorations run throughout (its list of
  illustrations: "Seder Eve", "The Exodus", "Moses and the Tables of the Law"), so only an undecorated text page was
  used.
- The Union Haggadah, p. xiv, "Directions for Setting the Table": a drawn seder table whose front carries a frieze
  that could hold small figures; not clearly free of figures, so not used. Its p. xv ("a large brimming goblet in
  the center of the table for the prophet Elijah") is plain text but adds little to p. xii.
- Commons, "Elijah cup and besamim boxes from the Friedmann Collection (1927)": its licence tag is PD-HU-unknown
  (unknown author) but the same page names the photographer, Alexander Borka, whose death year is not given. Not
  clearly PD in the EU; dropped.
- Commons/Flickr, "Elijah cup (4570671265)" (Center for Jewish History, Yeshiva University Museum 1997.539): "No
  known copyright restrictions; may be subject to third party rights", and the cup is a modern work by Ismar David
  (died 1996). Dropped.
- Commons, "כוס של אליהו.jpg": CC BY-SA 4.0. Not allowed.
- Mount Carmel (El-Muhraqa), the site of Elijah's sacrifice: the site has a statue of Elijah, and the story does not
  tell the Carmel episode. Not pursued.
- The Met's only Havdalah spice tower (2013.242) is not Open Access (isPublicDomain false). Not used.
