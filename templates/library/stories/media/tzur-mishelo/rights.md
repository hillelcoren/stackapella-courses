## Batch 8 rules: divine names, figures, lines kept

Every printed word in each final was read, the Hebrew at full scan size (work/c3.jpg, work/d-edge.jpg).

- **1.jpg keeps** (Abrahams 1914, p. cclx, top): "cclx", "Table Hymns.", "Here, a few of the most popular of these
  hymns are included: (a) The hymn Rock from whose store we have eaten is of unknown authorship. It is based on the
  paragraphs of the Grace after Meals, and the refrain is made up from the first phrase of the Grace and the verse
  (2 Kings iv. 44) which comes towards the close of the Grace in the Sephardic version. As Baer (p. 205) points out
  there is no reference to the Sabbath in this hymn, which is, he adds, appropriate for any occasion. The translation
  is by Mrs R. N. Salaman (some phrases and the metre being taken from a rendering by Mr I. Zangwill)."
  No divine name in any language. **Left out:** note (b), which opens "The Aramaic hymn God of the World" (cut at
  y 1294 of the scan, between the Zangwill line's descenders at about y 1290 and the "(b)" line at about y 1300,
  work/a-edge.jpg), and notes (c) and (d) with their Hebrew words.
- **2.jpg keeps** (JE vol. 12, p. 659, right column, one paragraph): "When the whole collection of zemirot is used,
  they are not chanted to definite melodies, but are read in a sort of cantillation (comp. OREN), and where a
  selection is made, the most frequent air is a German melody employed either with the "Yom Zeh le-Yisrael" of
  Isaac LURIA or with the anonymous "Ẓur mi-Shello Akalnu."" No divine name. **Left out:** the whole left column,
  which names the hymn "Baruk Adonai Yom Yom" (Adonai in transliteration), the music above with "God's great Law",
  and the paragraphs before and after.
- **3.jpg keeps** (Abrahams 1914, p. cclxi, top): "Table Hymns.", "cclxi", then, pointed:
  "צוּר מִשֶּׁלּוֹ אָכַלְנוּ · בָּרְכוּ אֱמוּנַי · שָׂבַעְנוּ וְהוֹתַרְנוּ · כִּדְבַר יְיָ׃" (refrain);
  "הַזָּן אֶת־עוֹלָמוֹ · רוֹעֵנוּ אָבִינוּ · אָכַלְנוּ אֶת־לַחְמוֹ · וְיֵינוֹ שָׁתִינוּ · עַל־כֵּן נוֹדֶה לִשְׁמוֹ · וּנְהַלְּלוֹ בְּפִינוּ ·
  אָמַרְנוּ וְעָנִינוּ אֵין־קָדוֹשׁ כַּיְיָ׃" (verse 1); and the refrain again, ending "כִּדְבַר יְיָ׃".
  The only form of the name shown is the abbreviation יְיָ (three times: twice "כדבר יי", once "כיי"), which the
  rule allows. **Left out:** verse 2, whose first line has "נְבָרֵךְ אֱלֹהֵינוּ" (Eloheinu, spelled in full); the crop ends
  at y 1412 of the scan, below the second refrain's vowel points (about y 1395) and above the verse-2 line's
  ascenders (about y 1430), work/d-edge.jpg. Also left out: verses 3 and 4 and the refrains below.
  Faint mirrored show-through from the back of the leaf (p. cclxii: the end of verse 4 in Hebrew, then the English)
  is visible but not legible; p. cclxii's Hebrew has no full divine name in any case (only "כברכת יי").
- **4.jpg keeps** (JE vol. 12, p. 658, top): "Zemirot", "THE JEWISH ENCYCLOPEDIA", "658", "SABBATH REST",
  "Lento.", and the three English verses under the music: "1. This day is to our race Of all seasons the best,
  Replete with ev'ry grace, The day that brings us rest; O come, most welcome guest, Refresh each weary breast!
  A light and a joy to us is Sabbath rest." "2. The lives torn with distress, With sweet solace it heals, With fresh
  spirit doth bless The soul that anguish feels; A way sorrow it steals From hearts sadly oppressed: A light and a joy
  to us is Sabbath rest." "3. This day, more than all else, Did God holy declare, When He whose love it tells,
  Completed His work so fair. The hearts full of despair In its quiet are blessed: A light and a joy to us is Sabbath
  rest." No Hebrew divine name and no transliteration of one. **The English word "God" shows once** (verse 3): a
  vernacular word on a quoted historical page, which the batch 8 ruling lets stay. **Left out:** "AIRS OF THE
  HASIDIM" and "KI ESHMERAH SHABBAT" below (the latter has "God will then" in its refrain).
- **Figures:** none in any of the four. 1.jpg and 3.jpg are type only; 2.jpg is type only; 4.jpg is music and type,
  with no ornament.
- **Nothing demeaning:** every word above was read; the texts are a hymn, notes on it, and an encyclopedia's account
  of its melody.
- **Photograph:** none used. A Sabbath or blessing cup was looked for: the Met's open-access Judaica has no cup
  identified as a Sabbath or Kiddush cup (its 1723 "Cup with cover with Hebrew inscriptions", 17.190.575, has no
  stated use, so a caption tying it to the grace would be a guess); Smithsonian's Kiddush cups have no open-access
  images; Cleveland has only a copyrighted 1998 print. Dropped rather than guessed.

- Item: https://archive.org/details/annotatededition00unknuoft, "Annotated edition of The authorised daily prayer
  book : with historical and explanatory notes, and additional matter"; London: Eyre and Spottiswoode, 1914;
  contributor "Pratt - University of Toronto"; call_number "AIC-2315".
- Rights statement, IA metadata https://archive.org/metadata/annotatededition00unknuoft:
  "possible-copyright-status": "NOT_IN_COPYRIGHT"; "copyright-region": "US"; "copyright-evidence": "no visible
  notice of copyright and date found; stated date is 1914; not published by the US government".
- Public domain by age: published in London in 1914. Israel Abrahams (1858-1925) wrote the notes; Simeon Singer
  (1848-1906) made the prayer book. Nina Salaman (died 1925) and Israel Zangwill (died 1926) are only named in 1.jpg;
  their English is not shown. The Hebrew hymn is anonymous and centuries old. US: published before 1931, so PD.
  EU and Israel: life plus 70 ended by 1 January 1997 for every named person. A faithful scan of a flat page adds no
  new copyright (US Bridgeman v. Corel; EU DSM Directive art. 14; Israel, no originality in a faithful copy).
- Files: https://archive.org/download/annotatededition00unknuoft/page/n279.jpg (2072 x 3412, raw/abr-n279.jpg),
