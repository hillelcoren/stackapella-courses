Christians, Awake!, in full Christians, Awake, Salute the Happy Morn, is an English Christmas hymn by the Manchester poet John Byrom, sung to YORKSHIRE, the tune John Wainwright made for it [^julian1892][^julian-supp][^hymnary-tune]. It retells the night the angels came to the shepherds in Luke's Gospel, and ends with the singers hoping to join the angels' song [^am1875][^kjv]. A manuscript in Byrom's hand, headed for his daughter Dolly, is kept at Chetham's Library in Manchester [^chethams][^julian-supp]. At Christmas 1750, Wainwright and the singing men and boys of the old church came to Byrom's house and sang it there [^julian-supp][^chethams][^praise]. It is still in the books: the Episcopal Church's Hymnal 1982 prints it at No. 106 and the Methodist Singing the Faith at No. 195, both to YORKSHIRE [^eh1982][^methodist].

## Where it comes from

**The poet.** John Byrom was born near Manchester on 29 February 1691/2, which is 1692 by today's reckoning [^dnb-byrom][^julian1892][^remains1]. He went to Trinity College, Cambridge, became a fellow there in 1714, and earned his living in London by teaching a shorthand of his own invention; in 1724 he was elected to the Royal Society [^dnb-byrom][^julian1892]. He wrote verse, an 1872 hymnal companion by Charles Hutchins says, for his own pleasure rather than for print [^julian-supp]. His poems were first collected ten years after his death, in 1773 [^dnb-byrom][^julian1892].

**For Dolly.** Byrom's daughter Dorothy, Dolly in the family, was born in 1730: in the shorthand lessons Byrom set his elder daughter Beppy, an exercise dated 25 April 1730 wishes "much joy of Sister Dolly, who has just been christened" [^chethams][^poems1895]. Chetham's Library in Manchester holds the poem's manuscript in Byrom's hand [^chethams][^julian-supp]. Adolphus William Ward, who edited Byrom's poems for the Chetham Society, gives its heading as "Christmas Day for Dolly", and concluded that Byrom meant it as a Christmas gift for her [^julian-supp][^poems1895][^canterbury].

**When?** The dates differ. Chetham's Library says the poem is "tentatively" believed to be from 1749; Julian's New Supplement of 1907, weighing the evidence, gives 1750 as the earliest date it can support [^chethams][^julian-supp].

**Christmas 1750.** The first known singing is in Byrom's own record. Praise!, quoting his diary, gives it as "the singing men and boys, with Mr Wainwright, came here and sang 'Christians awake'"; Ward's edition, as Julian's New Supplement quotes it, cites a letter of 1886 by J. E. Bailey that places the visit on Christmas Eve, "before Byrom's door"; Chetham's Library tells it as singers led by the tune's composer, outside Byrom's house [^praise][^julian-supp][^chethams].

**The composer.** John Wainwright was born in 1723, probably at Stockport, and died in 1768 [^canterbury-wainwright][^hymnary-tune]. He sang and played the organ at the Collegiate Church in Manchester, now Manchester Cathedral, and later became its organist [^canterbury-wainwright][^hymnary-tune]. The Psalter Hymnal Handbook, quoted by Hymnary.org, says he wrote YORKSHIRE for these words in 1750 [^hymnary-tune]. The tune's earliest known printing is about 1760, in Caleb Ashworth's Collection of Tunes [^hymnary-tune][^praise]. Words and tune appear together in Wainwright's own Collection of Psalm Tunes, Anthems, Hymns and Chants, dated 1766 by Hymnary.org and the Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology and about 1767 by the IMSLP listing of the British Library's copy, which calls it the first printing of his setting of the hymn [^hymnary-tune][^canterbury-wainwright][^imslp-wainwright]. Other names stuck to the tune: STOCKPORT, and MOTTRAM [^julian-supp][^praise].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Dolly asked for a poem for Christmas 1749 and found this one on her plate on Christmas morning, and the tune was first sung in Stockport parish church on Christmas Day 1750 [^canterbury][^praise][^hymnary-tune]. What the record shows: a manuscript headed for Dolly, from which Ward read it as a Christmas gift, with the year only guessed [^julian-supp][^chethams]. The 1750 singing that Byrom's diary and Bailey describe was at Byrom's own house, by Wainwright and the church's singing men and boys; the Canterbury Dictionary says the oft-repeated claim that Wainwright was organist at Stockport parish church "seems to be spurious" [^praise][^julian-supp][^canterbury-wainwright].

**The 1773 printing.** Byrom died in September 1763, on the 26th by the newspaper notice of his death and the Dictionary of National Biography, on the 28th by Julian [^remains2][^dnb-byrom][^julian1892]. In 1773 the Manchester printer J. Harrop brought out his Miscellaneous Poems in two volumes [^byrom1773][^julian1892]. In the second, under "Hymns for Christmas Day", comes first a hymn in six numbered verses, "In this auspicious, memorable Morn", and then, on pages 58 and 59, this poem, headed only "On the Same." [^byrom1773][^julian1892]. It is 48 lines of rhyming couplets, set in two blocks of 32 and 16 lines, with the angel's message in italics after a long dash at "Behold!" [^byrom1773][^julian1892]. The Canterbury Dictionary and Praise! describe the original poem as longer, 52 lines [^canterbury][^praise].

**Six verses, 1819 and 1861.** The six verses of six lines that hymnals sing were first cut from the poem in Thomas Cotterill's Selection of Psalms and Hymns in 1819, and from there passed into most hymnals [^julian1892][^praise]. Hymns Ancient and Modern, whose musical editor was William Henry Monk, printed it in its first edition with tunes in 1861 as Hymn 47 under "Christmas", headed "Behold I bring you glad tidings of great joy", with the tune above and an Amen at the end [^am1861][^dnb-monk]. Its Index of Tunes lists "Yorkshire", in the metre 10 10, 10 10, 10 10, as by "Dr. Wainwright", "Harmonized or Arranged by" W. H. Monk, for Hymn 47 [^am1861]. Praise! credits that book with taking the hymn beyond the north of England [^praise].

**The Revised Edition.** In the Revised Edition of 1875 it is No. 61, between Hark! the Herald Angels Sing and While Shepherds Watched [^am1875][^hymnary-monk]. The music page is headed "Yorkshire" and "10 10 10 10 10 10", with a speed of 100 crotchets a minute, and "47 Orig. Ed." at its foot; the words face it on page 79 under "Behold I bring you good tidings of great joy", with an Amen and dynamics in the margin, mf for most verses and p for the angels' "Peace upon earth" and the whole fifth verse [^am1875]. The tune is the same note for note as in 1861; Monk changed his harmony in three bars [^am1861][^am1875]. Monk died in 1889 [^dnb-monk][^ha-monk].

## What the words mean

Byrom's poem follows Luke 2:8 to 20: the angel's message to the shepherds, the angels' song, the shepherds' visit, and Mary keeping these things in her heart [^kjv][^byrom1773]. Hymns Ancient and Modern sings it in six verses [^am1875].

**Verse 1.** "Salute the happy morn": greet Christmas morning. The "mystery of love" is the birth itself, which the angels were the first to announce; "God Incarnate" is God made flesh, and "the Virgin's Son" is Jesus, son of Mary [^am1875][^kjv]. The 1861 book has "the Saviour of mankind", where Byrom and the Revised Edition have "the Saviour of the world" [^am1861][^byrom1773][^am1875].

**Verse 2.** Nearly the angel's words in Luke: "Behold, I bring you good tidings of great joy, which shall be to all people. For unto you is born this day in the city of David a Saviour, which is Christ the Lord" [^kjv]. Byrom adds that in this birth God "fulfill'd His promised word" [^am1875]. In 1773 the poem goes on with four more lines of the angel's speech, on the city of David, the swaddling clothes and the manger; hymnals leave them out [^byrom1773][^am1875].

**Verse 3.** The "celestial choir" is Luke's "multitude of the heavenly host" [^kjv]. To "conspire" here is to sound together, from the Latin for breathing together; "Heav'n's whole orb" is the whole sphere of the sky [^webster1913]. The last line is the angels' song, "on earth peace, good will toward men"; Byrom wrote "mutual Good-will", and "Hallelujahs" where the book has "Alleluias" [^kjv][^byrom1773][^am1875].

**Verse 4.** The shepherds run to Bethlehem, "enlighten'd" by the angel's news, and find Mary, "the Blessèd Maid", with Joseph and the child; then they go back to their flocks "glorifying and praising God", as Luke says [^am1875][^kjv]. The 1861 book left out the lines about Joseph and Mary and ended instead with the shepherds as "The first apostles of the Saviour's Name", reworking Byrom's "The first Apostles of his Infant Fame"; the Revised Edition put Byrom's lines back [^am1861][^byrom1773][^am1875].

**Verse 5.** Luke says that Mary "kept all these things, and pondered them in her heart"; the singers ask to do the same [^kjv][^am1875]. "Retrieved our loss" means made good what was lost, and "man's first heavenly state" is the state of Adam and Eve before the Fall: Byrom's companion hymn speaks of Adam, "who kept not his first State of Bliss" [^am1875][^byrom1773].

**Verse 6.** "The Angelic hosts among": among the angels. The 1861 book has "To join, redeemed, a glad triumphant throng"; the Revised Edition keeps Byrom's "To sing, redeem'd, a glad triumphal song" [^am1861][^byrom1773][^am1875]. Byrom's last line was "Of Angels, and of Angel-Men, the King"; the hymn ends instead with "Eternal praise to Heav'n's Almighty King" [^byrom1773][^am1875].

### The full words

These are the six verses as Hymns Ancient and Modern, Revised Edition (1875), No. 61, prints them, with its spelling, capitals and quotation marks; the book's small capitals of God, Christ and Lord are written here as ordinary words [^am1875]. We sing verses 1, 2 and 6, with "the Angelic" sung as "th'Angelic". Byrom died in 1763, so every verse is in the public domain.

**Verse 1**
Christians, awake, salute the happy morn,
Whereon the Saviour of the world was born;
Rise to adore the mystery of love,
Which hosts of Angels chanted from above;
With them the joyful tidings first begun
Of God Incarnate and the Virgin's Son.

**Verse 2**
Then to the watchful shepherds it was told,
Who heard the Angelic herald's voice, "Behold,
I bring good tidings of a Saviour's birth
To you and all the nations upon earth:
This day hath God fulfill'd His promised word,
This day is born a Saviour, Christ the Lord."

**Verse 3**
He spake; and straightway the celestial choir
In hymns of joy, unknown before, conspire:
The praises of redeeming love they sang,
And Heav'n's whole orb with Alleluias rang:
God's highest glory was their anthem still,
Peace upon earth, and unto men good will.

**Verse 4**
To Bethlehem straight the enlighten'd shepherds ran,
To see the wonder God had wrought for man,
And found, with Joseph and the Blessèd Maid,
Her Son, the Saviour, in a manger laid:
Then to their flocks, still praising God, return,
And their glad hearts with holy rapture burn.

**Verse 5**
O may we keep and ponder in our mind
God's wondrous love in saving lost mankind;
Trace we the Babe, Who hath retrieved our loss,
From His poor manger to His bitter Cross;
Tread in His steps, assisted by His grace,
Till man's first heavenly state again takes place.

**Verse 6**
Then may we hope, the Angelic hosts among,
To sing, redeem'd, a glad triumphal song:
He that was born upon this joyful day
Around us all His glory shall display;
Saved by His love, incessant we shall sing
Eternal praise to Heav'n's Almighty King.

## How it is sung

It is a hymn for Christmas, and its first line makes it a hymn for Christmas morning [^am1875][^praise]. Its metre is 10.10.10.10.10.10: six lines of ten syllables [^am1875][^hymnary-tune]. Hymnals sing it to YORKSHIRE, the tune it was made with: Hymns Ancient and Modern, the Methodist Singing the Faith (No. 195, five verses) and the Episcopal Church's Hymnal 1982 (No. 106, six verses) all do [^am1875][^methodist][^eh1982]. The tune's last line repeats the notes of its second line in a new rhythm, as the Psalter Hymnal Handbook points out and as our song file shows ("Whereon the Saviour of the world was born", "Of God Incarnate and the Virgin's Son") [^hymnary-tune][^am1875]. The Handbook suggests a small group for the first verses and everyone for the last [^hymnary-tune].

Our arrangement is read from Hymn 61 of Hymns Ancient and Modern, Revised Edition (1875), in a later printing, in the book's key of D and at its marked speed of 100 [^am1875]. The soprano sings YORKSHIRE note for note as the book prints it; the lower parts are Monk's, with a few alto and tenor notes revoiced for beginners and the opening of line 4 taken from his 1861 chords, and the Amen is left out [^am1875][^am1861]. We sing verses 1, 2 and 6. It is for 2 to 4 singers: two take soprano and alto, a third adds the bass, and the tenor makes four.

## Versions and other words

- **Byrom's other Christmas hymn.** The six-verse hymn printed just before it in 1773, "In this auspicious, memorable Morn", is the other of the two "Hymns for Christmas Day" in that book; "On the Same." means on the same day, Christmas Day [^byrom1773][^julian1892].
- **The 1861 wording.** Hymns Ancient and Modern's first edition has "Saviour of mankind" in verse 1, a different end to verse 4, and "To join, redeemed, a glad triumphant throng" in verse 6; the Revised Edition of 1875 brought these lines back toward Byrom's [^am1861][^am1875][^byrom1773].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/christians-awake/soprano): YORKSHIRE, from low D to top F-sharp, most often on D, then C-sharp and B. Every verse starts on the low D ("Chris-tians", "Then to", "Then may") and climbs a sixth through the first line. The top F-sharp comes three times a verse, all single short notes: "of" in "Saviour of the world", "to" in "Rise to adore" and "and" in "Incarnate and" (verse 2: "her-ald's", "bring", "Sav-iour"; verse 6: "glad", "that", "Heav'n's"). The second of them is followed by a leap down a sixth to A ("Rise to a-dore", "bring good", "that was"); tune it in the player. Its one note outside the key is the A-sharp in line 4, on "gels" in "Angels" ("the" in "all the nations", "His" in "His glory").
- [Alto](/songs/christians-awake/alto): under the tune, most often on D, then F-sharp. It starts in unison with the soprano on D and stays low through the first line, rocking between D and C-sharp. Its top A comes four times a verse: three times under the tune's top F-sharp ("of", "to", "and"), and on the first note of line 4 ("Which", "To", "A-round"). Its low A comes once a verse, on "first" in "first begun" ("prom-" in "promised", "we" in "we shall sing").
- [Tenor](/songs/christians-awake/tenor): the middle of the chord, on A more than half the time. Its top E comes once a verse, held on the first word of line 4 ("Which", "To", "A-round"); it then steps down to D and drops a fifth to G ("hosts of"). Its two notes outside the key come near the ends of lines 4 and 5: A-sharp on "a-" in "above" ("on" in "upon", "dis-" in "display") and G-sharp on "be-" in "begun" ("ised" in "promised", "shall"). Its low F-sharp opens each verse and line 5 ("With", "This", "Saved").
- [Bass](/songs/christians-awake/bass): the bass line, most often on D, then A and F-sharp, on the chord's root a little under two-thirds of the time. Twice a verse it drops an octave, from its top A to the A below, just before the cadence: "world was" and "Vir-gin's" (verse 2: "voice, Be-" and "Christ the"; verse 6: "um-phal" and "migh-ty"). Its low G comes once a verse, on "of" in "mystery of love" ("iour's" in "Saviour's birth", "ful" in "joyful day").

## Questions

### Who wrote Christians Awake?

John Byrom (1691/2 to 1763), a Manchester poet and teacher of shorthand, wrote the words; a manuscript in his hand, headed for his daughter Dolly, is at Chetham's Library in Manchester [^dnb-byrom][^julian1892][^chethams]. It dates from 1749 or 1750 [^chethams][^julian-supp]. The tune, YORKSHIRE, is by John Wainwright, a singer and organist at Manchester's Collegiate Church, who with the church's singing men and boys sang it at Byrom's house at Christmas 1750 [^hymnary-tune][^canterbury-wainwright][^julian-supp].

### What do the words of Christians Awake mean?

They tell the Christmas story from Luke's Gospel, chapter 2, and ask the singers to join in [^kjv][^am1875]. The first verse calls Christians to greet Christmas morning; the next three tell of the angel's message to the shepherds, the angels' song of "Peace upon earth" and the shepherds' visit to the manger; the fifth asks the singers to ponder it as Mary did; and the last hopes they will one day sing among the angels themselves [^am1875][^kjv].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings YORKSHIRE, note for note as Hymns Ancient and Modern prints it in 1875 [^am1875]. It spans more than an octave, from the low D that starts every verse to the top F-sharp three times a verse. With two singers, take soprano and alto; a third adds the bass, and a fourth the tenor.
