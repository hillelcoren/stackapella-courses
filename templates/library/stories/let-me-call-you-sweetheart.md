Let Me Call You Sweetheart, printed with the subtitle I'm in Love with You, is a waltz song of 1910 with words by Beth Slater Whitson and music by Leo Friedman [^lilly1910][^cce1910]. Friedman, a Chicago songwriter, published it himself that April, and in December sold the copyright to the Harold Rossiter Music Company [^lilly1910][^cce1910][^jhu1911]. Its publisher later called it one of the big hits of 1911, the year a quartet recorded it for Columbia [^rossiter1925][^sager][^locblog]. Then it faded, and in the mid-1920s it came back so strongly that the same publisher claimed no song had ever returned like it [^rossiter1925][^sager]. Its chorus asks for one thing: to call someone sweetheart, and to hear them whisper that they love you too [^lilly1910].

## Where it comes from

**The writers.** Beth Slater Whitson was born in 1879 in Goodrich, in Hickman County, Tennessee [^tnenc][^loc-whitson]. In 1911 her Chicago publisher, Will Rossiter, advertised her as "The Poetess of the South", who lived "among song birds and flowers on her little farm home, down among the purple shadowed hills of Tennessee, at Goodrich" [^rossiter1911]. Leo Friedman (1869 to 1927) was a composer in Chicago who published his own songs from 59 Dearborn Street [^loc-friedman][^jwj][^lilly1910][^cce1910]. Whitson's first big success, written with Friedman, was Meet Me To-Night in Dreamland of 1909, which Friedman registered for copyright in his own name [^tnenc][^cce1909]. Will Rossiter then bought it [^jasen1987][^tawa1990][^rossiter1911].

**The first printing.** The copy we read, in the Lilly Library at Indiana University, is Friedman's own edition, "Published by Leo Friedman, 59 Dearborn St. Chicago, Ill." and "Copyright 1910 by Leo Friedman" [^lilly1910]. Its cover, signed H. Sass, frames a portrait of a young woman in a rose-trimmed hat with green roses and swallows; a small photograph of Friedman sits in a diamond lettered "Songs with the Harmony", and both writers are billed as the writers of Meet Me To-Night in Dreamland and My! But I'm Longing for Love [^lilly1910]. Inside, the song is in B-flat and 3/4, marked Moderato, with two verses on one tune and the chorus on page 4, "Sweetheart" in quotation marks [^lilly1910]. The Copyright Office's catalogue records it as published on 8 April 1910; a Library of Congress essay gives 2 April 1910 as the registration date, six days earlier [^cce1910][^sager].

![[1]]

**Sold to Rossiter.** A Harold Rossiter Music Company advertisement of 1911, on the back cover of another of its songs, prints the song's chorus above two notices: "Copyright MCMX by Leo Friedman, Chicago" and "Copyright Assigned Dec. 13, 1910 to Harold Rossiter Music Co., Chicago" [^jhu1911]. Rossiter advertised it there as "the new song sensation by the writers of 'Meet Me To-Night in Dreamland'", with a new title page in colour [^jhu1911]. Later Rossiter printings name the firm itself as copyright owner from 1910 [^byu][^rossiter1925].

![[2]]

**Myth and record.** Often told: Friedman sold his great hit outright and never shared in its earnings. What the record shows: the histories we read tell of an outright sale, but of Meet Me To-Night in Dreamland. A 1990 history of American popular song says Dreamland was sold outright to Will Rossiter, who kept its large earnings from the writers [^tawa1990]. A 1987 anthology of the period's hits agrees that Will Rossiter bought Dreamland, and says that for Let Me Call You Sweetheart Friedman went to Harold Rossiter instead and was paid a royalty [^jasen1987]. Will Rossiter's own 1911 advertising told it another way: that he had paid $7,000 for Dreamland, and that Whitson, who sold her writings from her Tennessee farm, was making "a very comfortable living from the royalties", without saying which songs paid them [^rossiter1911]. No copy we read says what Harold Rossiter paid [^lilly1910][^jhu1911][^rossiter1925].

**The second wave.** The song was recorded only a few times in the 1910s, first on an Edison cylinder by the tenor Arthur Clough, then by the Columbia Quartette [^sager][^ers2004][^locblog]. From 1924 it was recorded again, by dance orchestras among others, for Columbia, Victor and Edison [^sager][^victor1924][^edison1924]. Rossiter's advertising of 1925 called it "the first and only song that has ever 'come back' into a second wave of popularity": "one of the big song hits of 1911", it was "now once again the biggest selling popular song on the market" [^rossiter1925][^byu][^alberta1925]. Sales figures differ: a Rossiter advertisement of the 1920s says over one million copies, the 1987 anthology well over six million [^alberta1925][^jasen1987]. Friedman died in 1927 [^variety1927][^loc-friedman][^jwj], and Whitson, who had moved to Nashville, died there in 1930 [^tnenc][^loc-whitson]. In March 2016 the Library of Congress added the 1911 record to the National Recording Registry, among its selections for 2015 [^sager][^locblog].

## What the words mean

1. **Verse 1.** The singer dreams of the one they love "day by day", on blue days and gray ones alike. Even by moonlight they wander on in dreams, through "a land of love" where there is no one but the two of them. "Silv'ry" is silvery, squeezed to fit the notes.
2. **Verse 2.** The longing grows "more and more", above all for the loved one's smile. Birds sing and roses bloom everywhere, but none of it helps: only "you just you" can cheer the singer's heart.
3. **Chorus.** The singer asks permission to say the word at last: to call the loved one "Sweetheart", which the sheet music prints in quotation marks [^lilly1910]. They ask to hear a whispered answer, "you love me too", and ask the loved one to keep "the love-light", the light of love, glowing in eyes "so true": faithful and constant [^webster-true].

### The full words

These are the two verses and the chorus as printed in Leo Friedman's first edition (copyright 1910), pages 2 to 4, with its spelling, capitals and quotation marks. The chorus is printed once, after the verses. The song was published in 1910, Friedman died in 1927 and Whitson in 1930, so all of it is in the public domain.

**Verse 1**
I am dreaming Dear of you
Day by day
Dreaming when the skies are blue
When they're gray;
When the silv'ry moonlight gleams
Still I wander on in dreams
In a land of love, it seems
Just with you.

**Verse 2**
Longing for you all the while
More and more
Longing for the sunny smile,
I adore;
Birds are singing far and near
Roses blooming ev'rywhere
You, alone, my heart can cheer
You just you.

**Chorus**
Let me call you "Sweetheart"
I'm in love with you
Let me hear you whisper
that you love me too
Keep the love-light glowing
In your eyes so true
Let me call you "Sweetheart"
I'm in love with you.

## How it is sung

Let Me Call You Sweetheart is a Tin Pan Alley popular song, sold as sheet music for voice and piano, with the chorus printed once after the two verses and first and second endings at its close, so it is sung after each verse [^lilly1910][^sager]. Rossiter also published it for mixed and male quartet, orchestra and band, and a male quartet made its best-known early record [^rossiter1925][^byu][^sager][^locblog].

![[4]]

Our arrangement takes the melody and words of the chorus from Friedman's first edition, page 4, every pitch as printed, moved up a semitone from B-flat to B, in 3/4 [^lilly1910]. We sing the chorus once, for 2 to 4 singers, all on the words: soprano and alto, then the bass, then the tenor. The harmony is our own, in barbershop style. From "with you" in the second line the chords step round the circle of fifths, G-sharp 7, C-sharp 7 and F-sharp 7, through "Let me hear you whisper that you", and land on B at "love". A few notes are shortened to leave room to breathe, and the last note is held to the end of its bar.

## Did you know?

- Whitson also wrote stories. Edward J. O'Brien's The Best Short Stories of 1916 named her The Knitter of Liège, from the Southern Woman's Magazine, among his fifty American short stories of the year, calling it the tale of a Belgian woman caught up in the war [^obrien1916][^chapter16].
- In the song's second wave, Harold Rossiter published an answer song, Since You Called Me Sweetheart (copyright 1925), and advertised it inside the covers of Let Me Call You Sweetheart, claiming that every answer song of the past twenty-five years had failed but this one [^rossiter1925][^byu].
- Rossiter's printings of the 1920s add ukulele chord boxes above the voice, a tuning of A, D, F-sharp, B, and the note "When used with piano put Capo on first fret" [^rossiter1925][^byu].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/let-me-call-you-sweetheart/soprano): you carry the tune, a slow waltz that settles on a long held note at the end of each line. It opens by climbing from the D-sharp above middle C, "Let me call you sweet-", then drops a sixth on "heart". Your top note, a high E, comes once, on "I'm" in the last line, and the final "you." holds for two full bars.
- [Alto](/songs/let-me-call-you-sweetheart/alto): you sing close under the tune, often a third below, hopping between the warm notes of each barbershop chord. In the third line you hold one note, E, through "Let me hear you whis-per that you" while the tune moves above; on "I'm in" in the last line you sing C natural twice, a note outside the key, under the tune's high E and D-sharp, so tune it with care.
- [Tenor](/songs/let-me-call-you-sweetheart/tenor): you sing the words in the middle, mostly on F-sharp, then B and C-sharp, a narrow line from low E to C-sharp. "Let me hear you" sits on C-sharp, with a dip to A-sharp on "whis-"; on "too." you drop to your lowest note, E, the seventh of the chord, and you end on F-sharp, held for two bars.
- [Bass](/songs/let-me-call-you-sweetheart/bass): you sing the words on the bass line, holding B and F-sharp for long stretches under the chords: B through "Let me call you sweet-", low F-sharp through "Let me hear you". On the first "love with you.", and again on "eyes so true.", you walk down E, D-sharp, C-sharp; you end on B, held for two bars.

## Questions

### Who originally sang Let Me Call You Sweetheart?

No source we read names the first singer to perform it on stage. Its first record was an Edison Amberol cylinder (no. 637) by the tenor Arthur Clough, issued in March 1911 [^sager][^ers2004]. Its best-known early record came out on Columbia that November, by a male quartet credited as the Columbia Quartette, which was the Peerless Quartet under another name [^sager][^locblog].

### Is Let Me Call You Sweetheart public domain?

Yes. It was published in the United States in 1910, and all works published there before 1 January 1931 are in the public domain [^cce1910][^circ15a][^cornell]. Its writers died long ago too, Leo Friedman in 1927 and Beth Slater Whitson in 1930, more than seventy years past [^variety1927][^loc-friedman][^jwj][^loc-whitson][^tnenc]. The original words and melody are free to sing and print; later arrangements and recordings can have their own copyrights.

### What genre is Let Me Call You Sweetheart?

It is a Tin Pan Alley popular song, a love song in waltz time, written for voice and piano and marked Moderato in 3/4 [^sager][^lilly1910][^loc-work]. Its publisher also issued it for male and mixed quartet, orchestra and band, and its best-known early record, of 1911, is by a male quartet [^rossiter1925][^byu][^sager][^locblog].

### When was Let Me Call You Sweetheart popular?

Twice. Its publisher called it one of the big hits of 1911 [^rossiter1925]. It was then recorded only a few times until 1924, when dance orchestras took it up again for Columbia, Victor and Edison [^sager][^victor1924][^edison1924]. By 1925 Harold Rossiter was advertising it as once again the biggest selling popular song on the market, and printing an answer song to ride the wave [^rossiter1925][^byu].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, Friedman's melody note for note, and everyone sings the words together. The alto sings close under it, the tenor holds the middle of the chords, and the bass sings the bass line. With two singers it is soprano and alto; with three, add the bass.
