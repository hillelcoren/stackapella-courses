Annie Laurie, also known by its first line, Maxwelton Braes Are Bonnie, is a Scottish love song. Its words grew from two old verses, said to be by a Mr Douglas of Fingland, about Anne Laurie, a daughter of the laird of Maxwelton in Dumfriesshire; their earliest known printing is in 1823 [^sharpe][^chambers1862][^fuld]. Alicia Spottiswoode, later Lady John Scott, reworked them, wrote a third verse and set them to a tune of her own in 1834 or 1835 [^warrender1911][^ross1910][^moffat]. It was printed in Edinburgh in 1838 without her name [^ross1910][^fuld]. By the early 1850s American publishers were selling it with Jenny Lind's portrait or name on the cover [^hall][^peters].

## Where it comes from

**The old song.** In 1823 the Edinburgh collector Charles Kirkpatrick Sharpe printed A Ballad Book for his friends, only thirty copies by his own account [^sharpe][^ross1910]. Song XXXVII begins "Maxwelton banks are bonnie, Whare early fa's the dew", in two verses that end "I'd lay down my head and die". Sharpe's note says that Anne Laurie was famous for her beauty and won the heart of Mr Douglas of Fingland, "who is said to have composed the following verses". Unluckily for him, she married Mr Ferguson of Craigdarroch [^sharpe]. In 1825 the poet Allan Cunningham reprinted it from Sharpe in his Songs of Scotland [^cunningham1825][^graham]. No older copy has been found [^fuld][^balladindex], and later editors thought its second verse partly borrowed: Graham from an old John Anderson, My Jo, Moffat from a Percy manuscript song [^graham][^moffat].

**The real Annie Laurie.** Anne Laurie was born in 1682 to Sir Robert Laurie and his wife Jean Riddell [^ross1910][^reddall][^fergusson1895]. In 1709 she married Alexander Fergusson of Craigdarroch, whose family papers kept her marriage contract and will [^fergusson1895][^ross1910]. Maxwelton, her home, still stands by the Cairn Water in Glencairn parish: a listed house, largely 17th-century, with a stone dated 1641 from her grandparents' time [^chambers1862][^hes][^ross1910].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Douglas and Annie were young lovers from rival clans, a Scottish Romeo and Juliet [^fuld][^balladindex]. What the record shows: Sharpe's note, the earliest, says only that Douglas "is said" to have written the verses, and the tune historian James Fuld treats the tale as legend [^sharpe][^fuld].

**Lady John Scott's song.** Alicia Ann Spottiswoode was born on 24 June 1810 at Spottiswoode, her family's house in Berwickshire [^warrender1911][^edinburgh]. She told her friend Lord Napier how the song began: "I made the tune very long ago to an absurd ballad, originally Norwegian, I believe, called 'Kempie Kaye'." Staying with her sister Maggy at Marchmont before her marriage, she came on a book of Allan Cunningham's and "took a fancy to the words of 'Annie Laurie'". She rewrote the peacock verse and made the third verse herself, "only for my own amusement", and wrote it down because her sister and brother-in-law liked it [^warrender1911][^ross1910]. Her grand-niece dates this to 1834 or 1835; the editor Donald Ross says 1835 [^warrender1911][^ross1910]. She later told Ross she had kept the first verse almost as she found it; to Moffat, too, she said the air was hers [^ross1910][^moffat]. On 16 March 1836 she married Lord John Scott, a younger son of the fourth Duke of Buccleuch [^warrender1911][^edinburgh].

**Printed without her name.** The earliest known printing is in the third volume of The Vocal Melodies of Scotland, arranged by Finlay Dun and John Thomson for Paterson & Roy of Edinburgh. Its preface is dated October 1838, and Annie Laurie is No. 27, on page 89, with no author or composer named [^fuld][^ross1910]. Her grand-niece wrote that it appeared without her knowledge, taken, Lady John believed, when her music book was sent away to be rebound [^warrender1911]. Graham's Songs of Scotland, in the late 1840s, called the air "quite modern" and named the composer only as "Lady J. S.", with the rest of her surname dashed out [^graham]. In 1862 Robert Chambers still wrote of "a living lady of rank" [^chambers1862]. Her name appeared with it at last in a set of her songs sold for soldiers' families of the Crimean War: in 1854, Ross says, while her grand-niece places it after the war [^ross1910][^warrender1911].

**The New York printing we read.** Our arrangement is read from a sheet published by William Hall & Son of New York, plate 793; its library dates it about 1848 from the publisher's address [^hall]. Its cover, a portrait of Jenny Lind listing Annie Laurie as No. 7 of eight songs, suggests a little later: Lind's American tour opened in New York in September 1850 [^hall][^nps][^epochs]. Inside are four pages for voice and piano, marked Moderato, with all three verses and no author or composer named [^hall]. A Cincinnati edition of the same notes came in a series whose cover reads "Selection of Scotch Songs as sung by Jenny Lind", with Annie Laurie as No. 4; inside, it is headed "as sung by Catherine Hayes" [^peters].

**A poem from the Crimea.** The song's best-known link with the Crimean War is a poem, Bayard Taylor's The Song of the Camp: on the eve of an attack, soldiers in the trenches facing the Redan ask for a song, and "all sang 'Annie Lawrie'" [^taylor1865]. It is a poet's picture, not a report.

Lady John Scott died at Spottiswoode on 12 March 1900; ten years later Ross wrote that no Scottish song was better known [^warrender1911][^edinburgh][^ross1910].

## What the words mean

The song is a lover's vow. The old song's second verse admired her shape more frankly: "She's backit like a peacock", straight-backed and proud, "jimp about the middle", slim at the waist, with "a rolling eye" [^sharpe]; Lady John Scott replaced it [^warrender1911][^moffat].

1. Maxwelton's "braes", its hill slopes [^dsl-brae], are "bonnie", lovely. The dew "fa's", falls, early there. It was there Annie "gie'd", gave, me her promise, which will "ne'er", never, be forgotten. For bonnie Annie Laurie I would "lay me down and dee", lie down and die.
2. Her brow is white as a "snaw drift", a snowdrift, her throat as graceful as a swan's. Her face is the fairest the sun ever shone on, and her "e'e", her eye, is dark blue.
3. The "fa' o' her fairy feet", her light footfall, is like dew lying on the "gowan", the daisy [^dsl-gowan]. Her voice is low and sweet, like summer winds sighing, and she is all the world to me.

Printings differ in small words: some have "neck" for "throat" [^graham][^moffat], Lady John Scott's own text has "bonniest" for "fairest" [^warrender1911], and an American songbook of 1894 smooths the Scots into "falls", "Gave" and "die" [^reddall].

### The full words

These are the three verses as printed in the William Hall & Son sheet our arrangement is read from, pages 2 to 5, with its spelling and punctuation. It prints "And its" where we sing "And it's". Lady John Scott died in 1900, so all three verses are in the public domain.

**Verse 1**
Maxwelton braes are bonnie,
Where early fa's the dew;
And its there that Annie Laurie,
Gie'd me her promise true.
Gie'd me her promise true,
Which ne'er forgot will be;
And for bonnie Annie Laurie,
I'd lay me down and dee.

**Verse 2**
Her brow is like the snaw drift,
Her throat is like the swan;
Her face it is the fairest,
That e'er the sun shone on,
That e'er the sun shone on,
And dark blue is her e'e
And for bonnie Annie Laurie,
I'd lay me down and dee.

**Verse 3**
Like dew on the gowan lying,
Is the fa' o' her fairy feet;
Like the winds in summer sighing,
Her voice is low and sweet.
Her voice is low and sweet
She's all the world to me
And for bonnie Annie Laurie,
I'd lay me down and dee.

## How it is sung

Annie Laurie began as a song for one voice: Lady John Scott sang it for her family, and publishers sold it for voice and piano [^warrender1911][^hall][^peters]. Each verse repeats its fourth line, as the old song did [^hall][^sharpe].

Our arrangement is our own four parts on Lady John Scott's tune, in C major and 2/4 as the Hall sheet prints it, slow and tender, for 2 to 4 singers: soprano and alto, then the bass, then the tenor. Its chords were checked against Hall's piano part and against Finlay Dun's piano arrangement, in an 1877 St. Louis printing [^hall][^dun1877]. We sing verses 1 and 2. In verse 2, "throat is like the" follows the Peters edition's rhythm, since Hall's bar is a sixteenth too long [^peters]; the grace notes and the piano bars between the verses are left out.

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/annie-laurie/soprano): you carry Lady John Scott's tune. Its first two phrases start low and leap: from middle C on "Maxwelton" you jump an octave to C on "braes", and again from "there that" to "An-nie". The third phrase, "Gie'd me her promise true, Which ne'er forgot will be;", climbs to your top E on "true," and "be;" ("on," and "e'e" in verse 2), and "And for bonnie Annie Laurie" falls from that E. Watch "lay": one syllable that leaps from middle C up an octave, then drops to E for "me". Each verse ends low, on middle C for "dee."
- [Alto](/songs/annie-laurie/alto): you begin on the tune's middle C, then sing mostly a third or a sixth below it. Your lowest note is the A on "the" in verse 2's "like the swan"; the B just above it comes on "the dew;" and "it's" in verse 1 and on "like", "swan;" and "Her" in verse 2. In each "And for bonnie Annie Laurie," you start on your top C and walk down the scale, through F on "Annie", to E on "Lau-", then drop to middle C on "-rie,". Wherever the tune comes to rest on middle C ("true.", "on," and both "dee."), you sing the E above it.
- [Tenor](/songs/annie-laurie/tenor): your part sits in a small space, from the E below middle C up to middle C, and much of it on G. You start on that E for "Maxwelton". You rise to middle C, your top note, as on "Which ne'er for-", "And dark blue" and the start of each "Annie" in "And for bonnie Annie Laurie". Tune the B natural on "and" just before each "dee": it drops a third to G, and the last one is held to the end.
- [Bass](/songs/annie-laurie/bass): you sing the low line. The opening moves from C up to F on "bonnie, Where" and down to low G on "the dew". Under the tune's climb you hold C and dip a half step to B natural on "promise" and "-got will". In each "And for bonnie Annie Laurie," you step down in thirds, C, A, F, to your lowest note on "Annie", then leap back up to C for "Laurie".

## Questions

### Who was Annie Laurie?

She was Anne Laurie of Maxwelton in Dumfriesshire, born in 1682, a daughter of Sir Robert Laurie, first baronet, and Jean Riddell [^sharpe][^ross1910][^fergusson1895]. In 1709 she married Alexander Fergusson of Craigdarroch, not Mr Douglas of Fingland, the suitor said to have written the old song [^fergusson1895][^sharpe].

### When was Annie Laurie written?

In two stages. Robert Chambers guessed the old verses dated from around 1700, in Annie's youth, though no copy before 1823 is known [^graham][^fuld]. Lady John Scott reworked them in 1834 or 1835, setting them to a tune she had made earlier, and the song was printed in 1838 [^warrender1911][^ross1910][^fuld].

### What does "Maxwelton braes are bonnie" mean?

It means "the hill slopes of Maxwelton are lovely". In Scots a brae is the brow of a hill [^dsl-brae] and "bonnie" means beautiful or pretty [^dsl-bonny]. Maxwelton, the Laurie family's house on the Cairn Water in Dumfriesshire, was the real Annie Laurie's home [^chambers1862][^hes][^ross1910].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings Lady John Scott's tune, as the Hall printing gives it [^hall]. The alto starts with it on middle C and then sings mostly a third or a sixth below it. With two singers you have the tune and the alto; with three, add the bass, and the tenor makes four.
