Now Thank We All Our God is a German hymn of thanks, "Nun danket alle Gott", written by Martin Rinkart, a Lutheran pastor in the Saxon town of Eilenburg, around 1630 [^adb-rinckart][^ndb-rinckart]. He called it a little prayer at table, a grace [^julian1907][^adb-rinckart]. He lived through the Thirty Years' War, and in the plague of 1637 he buried thousands of his townspeople; his own wife died in it [^julian1907][^ndb-rinckart]. Johann Crüger, a Berlin church musician, gave it its tune [^ndb-cruger][^adb-cruger]. Catherine Winkworth's English followed in 1858 [^lg1858][^hymnarchive-nun]. Julian's Dictionary of Hymnology called it "the German Te Deum" and found it in every German hymn book [^julian1907]. Bach built a whole cantata on its three verses [^bachipedia][^imslp192].

## Where it comes from

**The words.** Martin Rinkart was born at Eilenburg on 23 April 1586, by his own account, though his other marker for the day, Jubilate Sunday, fell that year on the 24th [^julian1907][^adb-rinckart][^ndb-rinckart]. From 1617 until his death he was archdeacon of his home town, through the whole of the Thirty Years' War [^julian1907][^ndb-rinckart][^hymnarchive-rinkart].

Then came the plague of 1637. By Julian's dictionary, the superintendent left the town, Rinkart was for a time its only clergyman, and he read the burial service over 40 to 50 people a day, about 4,480 in all [^julian1907]; Catherine Winkworth, writing in 1869, put it at more than 4,000 [^hymnarchive-rinkart]. Some 8,000 people died that year [^julian1907][^hymnarchive-rinkart], among them his first wife, Christine [^ndb-rinckart]; Julian dates her death 8 May 1637, from the title of the hymns Rinkart wrote in her memory [^julian1907]. Julian's dictionary credits him with twice saving the town from Swedish troops, in 1637 and 1639 [^julian1907]; Catherine Winkworth told of one such time, when his prayers moved a Swedish general to lower the town's levy [^hymnarchive-rinkart]. He died at Eilenburg on 8 December 1649, a year after the peace [^julian1907][^ndb-rinckart].

Nun danket alle Gott is older than the plague. Both German national biographies date it to around 1630 [^adb-rinckart][^ndb-rinckart], and Rinkart wrote in 1637 that the songs of his Jesu Hertz-Büchlein ("Jesus's little heart book") were finished six or seven years before [^hymnarchive-nun][^fischer-tuempel]. Its first two verses follow a passage of the Apocrypha, Ecclesiasticus (Sirach) 50:22 to 24, and the third is a version of the Gloria Patri, "Glory be to the Father" [^julian1907][^hymnarchive-nun]. What it was first written for is less certain than when. Fischer and Tümpel, from its place in the book, take it as meant first for grace at table, and record that the biographer Büchting dated it 1630 as a song of jubilee for the hundredth anniversary of the Augsburg Confession [^fischer-tuempel]; the Hymnology Archive reports a lost manuscript said to date it 24 June 1630 [^hymnarchive-nun].

Where the words were printed earliest is less certain. The hymn is usually said to have appeared in Rinkart's Jesu Hertz-Büchlein, Leipzig, 1636 [^julian1907][^canterbury], and in 1637 he wrote of "meinem getruckten Hertz-Büchlein", my printed heart book [^fischer-tuempel]. But no copy of that edition is known [^julian1907][^canterbury][^fischer-tuempel]. The oldest copy the reference books know is the second edition, Leipzig, 1663. There the hymn comes among its "Tisch-Gebetlein", graces for three children to say in turn; after the meal, "Hierauff alle zugleich", all say together either Sirach's own words in prose, "or else" ("Oder also") the hymn, printed without verse breaks, "or else" a short "Wir loben Gott" [^fischer-tuempel][^julian1907]. The Hymnology Archive shows two small pages captioned as the 1636 edition, under the same running head and in the same layout, the hymn again run on without verse breaks [^hymnarchive-nun]. But the pages show no date, the Archive names no library, and their wording is the wording Fischer and Tümpel give for 1663 [^fischer-tuempel]. So the earliest known printing that can be dated is in Johann Crüger's hymn book with music, of 1647 or 1648 [^julian1907][^zahn].

**The tune.** Johann Crüger was born on 9 April 1598 at Gross-Breesen near Guben, and from 1622 was cantor of St Nicholas's Church in Berlin until his death in 1662 [^adb-cruger][^hymnarchive-cruger]. His hymn book of 1640 grew, in its second edition, Berlin 1647, into Praxis pietatis melica ("the practice of piety in song") [^ndb-cruger][^hymnarchive-cruger]. Hymnals usually date Nun danket alle Gott and its tune, NUN DANKET (named after the hymn's first words), to that edition of 1647 [^hymnary-tune][^hymnarchive-nun]. The oldest copy that scholars around 1900 could find, a damaged one thought to be of 1648, has it as No. 183, on page 315, with its melody and without the author's name [^zahn][^fischer-tuempel][^julian1907]. The Bavarian State Library's copy of the edition of 1653 shows it as No. 239 among the "Lob- und Dancklieder", songs of praise and thanks, on pages 461 and 462: the melody with a figured bass, then all three verses run on like prose, unsigned [^ppm1653]. The tune is Crüger's by the usual account: Zahn and Julian's dictionary both note his initials on it in the Berlin hymn book of 1653 edited by Christoph Runge [^zahn][^julian1907]. It has also been called an adaptation of a melody by Luca Marenzio or of a motet by Rinkart, and some hymn books name Rinkart as its composer, but Julian found "very little evidence" for either [^julian1907][^eg321].

![[1]]

**The English.** Catherine Winkworth, born in London on 13 September 1827 by her memorial and the national biography (Julian's dictionary says 1829), grew up near Manchester [^dnb-winkworth][^memorials1908][^julian1907]. Her translation appeared in 1858, words only, in the second series of her Lyra Germanica [^hymnarchive-nun][^julian1907]. In the London first edition it opens the section "Praise and Thanksgiving", in three verses, headed "The Chorus of God's Thankful Children", and she credited it "Martin Rinckart. 1636.", her date for the German [^lg1858]. In The Chorale Book for England (1863) she set it to Crüger's tune [^hymnarchive-nun][^julian1907].

![[2]]

**The harmony.** In 1840 Felix Mendelssohn wrote his Lobgesang ("Hymn of Praise"), Op. 52, for Leipzig's festival marking 400 years of printing; it was first heard in St Thomas's Church there that June [^todd][^scholz]. Its eighth movement is the chorale Nun danket alle Gott, sung first by the choir alone and then with the orchestra [^todd][^hymnarchive-nun][^lobgesang1841]. Hymns Ancient and Modern (1861) printed it as Hymn 238, under "Thanksgiving", with harmony by its music editor, W. H. Monk [^ham1861]. Other hymnals took Mendelssohn's harmony: the Episcopal Church's Hymnal of 1916 heads it "Harmonized by Felix Mendelssohn, 1809-1847" [^hymnal1916], and Lift Up Your Hearts (2013) says its harmony is based on the Lobgesang [^liftup].

![[3]]

**Myth and record.** Often told: Rinkart wrote the hymn to celebrate the Peace of Westphalia, which ended the war in 1648. What the record shows: Julian's dictionary says the hymn "has no connection" with the peace, and Fischer and Tümpel agree [^julian1907][^fischer-tuempel]. Julian names a magazine of 1888 that told a detailed tale of its writing in November 1648 as "a good specimen of the way in which stories of hymns are manufactured". It suggests the idea grew because army chaplains were told to preach on the same verses of Ecclesiasticus at the thanksgiving for the peace [^julian1907].

Often told, too: on the night after Frederick the Great's victory at Leuthen, 5 December 1757, his soldiers began to sing this hymn, some 25,000 voices together [^carlyle][^spsg]. The museum of the Prussian palaces, which holds Wilhelm Camphausen's painting of the scene, "Dankchoral von Leuthen" (1864), calls it an anecdote, recorded by the historian Franz Kugler in 1840 [^spsg].

## What the words mean

The hymn has three verses of eight short lines, and Winkworth translated all three in 1858; we sing her first two [^julian1907][^lg1858]. Her 1858 printing has "Hath bless'd us", "Oh may this bounteous God" and "when perplex'd"; we sing them as later hymnals print them, "Hath blessed", "O may" and "perplexed" [^lg1858][^hymnal1916]. Julian called her version "full and very good" [^julian1907].

Verses 1 and 2 follow Ecclesiasticus 50:22 to 24, which blesses God for his mercy from the womb on, and asks for joy of heart and peace [^hymnarchive-nun][^kjv-apoc].

1. Everyone is called to thank God with heart, voice and hands. God has done great things everywhere, and has done good to each of us from the start of life, "von Mutterleib", from the mother's womb, and still does, "noch jtzund", even now.
2. A prayer for the rest of life: a joyful heart, peace, God's grace that keeps on holding, and rescue from every trouble "here and there", meaning in this life and the next.
3. Praise to God as Trinity: the Father, the Son, and "the one equal to both", Rinkart's way of naming the Holy Spirit, "three times one". It closes "as it was in the beginning, is now, and ever shall be", the church's old doxology, the Gloria Patri, sung at the end of psalms [^julian1907][^hymnary-text].

### Rinkart's German

Rinkart's German is given here as Johann Crüger's hymn book printed it in 1653, the earliest printing we have read in facsimile, with its spelling; its running text is set out line by line, with abbreviations written out [^ppm1653]. It matches the 1648 text as Fischer and Tümpel edited it [^fischer-tuempel]. Today's German hymnal ends verse 1 "bis hierher hat getan", up to now, and names the Spirit in verse 3, "und Gott dem Heilgen Geist" [^eg321]. Below each German line is our own plain English.

**Verse 1**
> Nu dancket alle GOtt
Now everyone, thank God
> mit hertzen/ mund und händen/
with heart and mouth and hands,
> Der grosse dinge thut
who does great things
> an uns und allen enden/
for us and in every place,
> Der uns von mutterleib
who from our mother's womb
> und kindesbeinen an
and from our childhood on
> Vnzählich viel zu gut/
has done us good past counting,
> und noch jtzund gethan.
and does so still today.

**Verse 2**
> Der ewigreiche GOtt
May God, rich for ever,
> woll uns bey unserm leben
all through our lives
> Ein jmmerfrölich hertz
give us an ever joyful heart
> und edlen frieden geben/
and noble peace,
> Vnd uns in seiner gnad
and in his grace
> erhalten fort und fort/
keep us on and on,
> Vnd uns aus aller noth
and from every need
> erlösen hier und dort.
set us free, here and hereafter.

**Verse 3**
> Lob/ ehr und preis sey GOTT
Praise, honour and glory be to God,
> dem Vater und dem Sohne/
the Father and the Son,
> Vnd dem/ der beyden gleich
and to him who is equal to both
> im höchsten himmelsthrone/
on the highest throne of heaven:
> Dem dreymal einem GOtt/
to God, three times one,
> als er ursprünglich war/
as he was in the beginning
> Vnd ist und bleiben wird
and is and shall remain,
> jtzund und jmmerdar.
now and for evermore.

### The full words

These are Catherine Winkworth's words. Verses 1 and 2 are as The Hymnal of 1916 prints them, the two verses on its page and the two we sing [^hymnal1916]. Verse 3, the doxology, is from her own printing of 1858, with its spelling and stops [^lg1858].

**Verse 1**
Now thank we all our God,
With heart, and hands, and voices,
Who wondrous things hath done,
In Whom His world rejoices;
Who from our mother's arms
Hath blessed us on our way
With countless gifts of love,
And still is ours to-day.

**Verse 2**
O may this bounteous God
Through all our life be near us!
With ever-joyful hearts
And blessèd peace to cheer us;
And keep us in His grace,
And guide us when perplexed,
And free us from all ills
In this world and the next.

**Verse 3**
All praise and thanks to God
The Father, now be given,
The Son, and Him who reigns
With them in highest heaven,
The One eternal God,
Whom earth and heaven adore,
For thus it was, is now,
And shall be evermore!

## How it is sung

In Germany it became the hymn of national thanksgiving, "used at all national festivals or special occasions of thanksgiving", as Julian's dictionary put it [^julian1907]. In the United States it is now a Thanksgiving hymn [^grindal][^hymnary-text]. Julian's dictionary found Winkworth's English in American hymn books, the Episcopal hymnal of 1871 among them [^julian1907], and by 1916 that church's hymnal printed it under Thanksgiving Day [^hymnal1916].

The tune is NUN DANKET, in the meter 6.7.6.7.6.6.6.6 [^hymnary-text][^liftup]. Most modern hymnals print the tune in even notes throughout, the "isorhythmic" form [^hymnary-tune].

Our arrangement, for 2 to 4 singers, takes Hymn 422 of The Hymnal of 1916: Winkworth's verses 1 and 2, in F major, with Mendelssohn's harmony as that book gives it, adapted for beginners [^hymnal1916]. The page is in 4/2, in minims; we write it in 4/4, with a one-beat pickup. The soprano sings Crüger's tune unchanged, every part sings one note to a syllable but for the tune's two notes on "blessed" and "guide", and the two pauses the page marks, on "voices" and "rejoices", are held for four beats [^hymnal1916].

## Did you know?

- Bach's cantata BWV 192 survives only in its parts: its original score is lost, and the tenor part is missing [^bachipedia][^emmanuel192].
- An American sheet-music printing of 1845 sets Mendelssohn's chorale to "Let all men praise the Lord", from Alfred Novello's translation for the Lobgesang's word-book of 1843, not Winkworth's English, which came 13 years later [^loc1845][^julian1907][^lg1858].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/now-thank-we-all-our-god/soprano): you carry Crüger's tune, mostly by step and one beat to a note. Lines 1 and 3 sit on C and lift to your top D ("all our", "things hath"), and lines 2 and 4 step down to F, held four beats on "voices" and "rejoices". Line 6 gives you the only two notes on one word, A and B natural on "blessed", then D on "on". The last line dips to your low E on "to-" before F on "day".
- [Alto](/songs/now-thank-we-all-our-god/alto): you sing a third under the tune through lines 1 and 3, A and B-flat under its C and D, then drop to a low A held four beats under "voices" and "rejoices". Line 7 has two F-sharps, on "count-" and "of", before G on "love". You end low: A, B-flat, A on "ours to-day".
- [Tenor](/songs/now-thank-we-all-our-god/tenor): in lines 1 and 3 you move against the tune: as it rises from C to D, you step down from C to B-flat. Your top D comes on "Whom". Line 6 is the tricky one, B natural on "on", G-sharp on "our", then A on "way"; solo it in the player. You end on your lowest note, C, for "to-day".
- [Bass](/songs/now-thank-we-all-our-god/bass): your first line leaps from F up to B-flat on "all our" and back. Lines 2 and 4 end on your low F, held four beats for "voices" and "rejoices". Line 5 has a B natural on "from", and line 7 holds F-sharp for "countless", then falls from G to the low G on "love".

## Questions

### Is Now Thank We All Our God a Catholic hymn?

It began as a Lutheran hymn and is now sung by Catholics too. Martin Rinkart was a Lutheran pastor[^adb-rinckart][^ndb-rinckart], but today the hymn is No. 405 in the Gotteslob, the Catholic hymnal of the German-speaking dioceses[^dli405], and American Catholic hymnals print Winkworth's English: Gather Comprehensive (GIA, 2004) as No. 545 and Breaking Bread (OCP, 2019) as No. 200[^gc2][^bb2020].

### When was Now Thank We All Our God written?

Around 1630, by Martin Rinkart, pastor at Eilenburg in Saxony [^adb-rinckart][^ndb-rinckart]. It was probably printed in his Jesu Hertz-Büchlein in 1636, but no copy of that edition is known; the earliest known printing is in Johann Crüger's hymn book, Praxis pietatis melica, Berlin, 1647 or 1648 [^canterbury][^julian1907][^fischer-tuempel]. It was not written for the Peace of Westphalia in 1648, though that story is often told [^julian1907][^adb-rinckart].

### What does "Nun danket alle Gott" mean in English?

It means "Now everyone, thank God", or "Now let all thank God". "Nun" is now, "danket" is the plural command "thank", "alle" is all, and "Gott" is God. The line goes on: with heart, mouth and hands [^eg321]. In Catherine Winkworth's English of 1858 it became "Now thank we all our God", sung to the same tune [^hymnarchive-nun][^lg1858][^julian1907].

### Did Bach set Now Thank We All Our God?

Yes, in at least two cantatas. His chorale cantata Nun danket alle Gott, BWV 192, from 1730, sets all three of Rinkart's verses without change [^bachipedia][^imslp192]. His cantata for the Reformation festival, Gott der Herr ist Sonn und Schild, BWV 79, has Rinkart's first verse, to Crüger's tune, as its third movement [^emmanuel79][^hymnary-tune].

### Is Now Thank We All Our God a funeral hymn?

It was not written as one: Rinkart wrote it, by most accounts, as a grace at table, and hymn books have filed it with praise and thanksgiving at least since Crüger's of 1653 [^julian1907][^adb-rinckart][^ppm1653][^lg1858]. Its link with death is its author's life: in the plague of 1637 he buried more than 4,000 people, and his wife died in it [^julian1907][^ndb-rinckart]. The hymn itself is older, and thanks God for his goodness from birth to this day.
