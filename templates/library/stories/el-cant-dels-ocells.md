El cant dels ocells, "the song of the birds", is a Catalan Christmas carol. Older books spell it El cant dels aucells or Lo cant dels aucells, and in English it is often called Song of the Birds. In it the birds fly to greet the "greater light" that dawns in the most blessed night, from the imperial eagle to the owls [^millet1917][^briz1874]. Its words were in print by 1874 and its tune by 1903, with no author known for either [^briz1874][^capmany1903]. The choirmaster Lluís Millet harmonised it for men's voices [^millet1917][^enccat-millet], and the cellist Pau Casals made it known on the cello, playing it at the United Nations in 1971 [^un-av][^paucasals-un].

## Where it comes from

**The words in print.** In 1874 the collector Francesc Pelay Briz opened the fourth volume of his *Cansons de la terra*, "songs of the land", with "Lo cant dels aucells", a "cansò de Nadal", a Christmas song [^briz1874]. Its fifteen six-line verses are Millet's of 1917, in the same order, with small differences of wording [^briz1874][^millet1917]. Briz's tune, though, is in 6/8 and is not the one we sing. He wrote that he could give many variants, "emperò la lletra no s'ho val", but the words were not worth it: what had real value was the tune, perhaps one of the most beautiful and original [^briz1874].

![[1]]

**The tune we sing.** Aureli Capmany's *Cançoner popular* came out in Barcelona as single sheets at ten cents each, already written about in La Renaixensa in September 1901 and gathered as a first series in 1903 [^capmany1903]. Sheet I, "Lo cant dels aucells", has a drawing signed J. M. Comas of birds around the stable, then the tune we sing, hand-lettered in 2/4 in B-flat minor, its last three lines repeated [^capmany1903]. Capmany's notes say it was sung on Christmas night, and that it had come down so complete because, no doubt, it was kept in print as goigs, single printed sheets of verse, for singing together [^capmany1903].

**How old is it?** Capmany thought it "provablement és del sigle XVII", probably of the 1600s, and no later, because a parody of it greeted the archduke Charles of Austria, hailed in Barcelona as King Charles III, at the start of the 1700s. He printed the parody as his sheet IV, from a goigs sheet: its birds sing as the ships appear before Barcelona, and half its eagle verse is the carol's, with the same faulty rhyme, "imperial" with "volant" [^capmany1903]. We know of no printing of the carol before 1874.

**Millet's harmony.** Lluís Millet (1867 to 1941) founded the Orfeó Català, a Barcelona choral society, with Amadeu Vives in 1891 [^enccat-millet][^palau-orfeo][^loc-millet]. In November 1912 he gave a lecture at the Palau de la Música Catalana on the people's religious song, at the third national congress of sacred music (on the 20th, says the footnote in his book; the Enciclopèdia's list of his works says the 23rd), with singers of the Orfeó giving the examples. Among the Christmas songs came this one, the birds proclaiming the good news, in the harmony for men's voices he had written some years before [^millet1912][^enccat-millet].

**The page.** Its music was printed in Millet's *Pel nostre ideal*, finished in Barcelona on 26 May 1917 [^millet1917]. Exemple XIV, "El cant dels aucells", "Harm. de'n Lluís Millet", is marked Moderat. The four men's staves, Tenors I and II, Baritons and Baixos, carry pp and "(Boca tancada)", closed mouth: the men hum, three bars alone before the solo's first "Al". All fifteen verses follow under the music, and a footnote says Iberia Musical had published the score and parts [^millet1917].

![[2]]

**Myth and record.** Sometimes told: it is Pau Casals's own composition, as one radio almanac puts it [^datebook]. What the record shows: its words were printed in 1874, two years before Casals was born, and its tune by 1903; what Casals made was an arrangement for cello [^briz1874][^capmany1903][^loc-casals][^paucasals-bio].

## What the words mean

After the first verse, in each six-line verse one to four birds sing or speak [^millet1917]. The print keeps old spellings: "aucells" for today's ocells (birds), "lo" and "los" for el and els (the), "ab" for amb (with), "dihent" for dient (saying), "tráurens" for treure'ns (to take us out) and "darnos" for donar-nos (to give us) [^millet1917][^capmany1903].

**Verse 1.** "Despuntar" is what the sun does as it first appears [^dcvb-words]. "Lo major lluminar" echoes the "greater light to rule the day" of Genesis 1:16, the Vulgate's "luminare majus" [^genesis][^dcvb-words]. Here that sun rises in the night, on the "nit més ditxosa", the most blessed night: it is the child, and the little birds go to greet him "ab sa veu melindrosa", with their dainty voices [^millet1917][^dcvb-words][^materials].

**Verse 2.** The imperial eagle flies through the air, singing that Jesus is born to take us out of sin and give us joy [^millet1917].

**The other verses** call the birds one by one, by their old country names [^millet1917][^dcvb-birds]. The "lluhé" is a siskin (today lluer), the merry "Tort" a thrush (tord), and the "estiverola", the great tit, has a name made from estiu, summer: here it sings that it is neither winter nor summer but spring, because a flower is born. Last come the owls, who see the sun come out and withdraw, dazzled: "Mirar no puch", I cannot look [^millet1917][^dcvb-birds]. Other words: "manyach" is a fond word for a gentle little one, "establía" a stable, and "m'admiren" means they amaze me [^dcvb-words][^millet1917].

### The full words

These are the fifteen verses as printed under Millet's music in *Pel nostre ideal* (1917), pages 240 and 241, with the spelling and accents as printed. We sing the first two. The print sets each speech off with a long dash, which we leave out; the meaning beside each line is our own. Bird names follow the dictionaries; where they differ we say so: the cotoliu is a woodlark in the Institut d'Estudis Catalans' dictionary and the Enciclopèdia's natural history, a meadow pipit in the Alcover-Moll dictionary [^diec][^dcvb-birds].

**Verse 1**
> Al veure despuntar
Seeing the dawning
> lo major lluminar
of the greater light
> en la nit més ditxosa,
in the most blessed night,
> los aucellets cantant
the little birds, singing,
> a festejarlo van
go to celebrate him
> ab sa veu melindrosa.
with their dainty voices.

**Verse 2**
> L'Aliga imperial
The imperial eagle
> va pels aires volant
goes flying through the air,
> cantant ab melodía,
singing a melody,
> dihent: Jesús es nat
saying: Jesus is born
> per tráurens de pecat
to take us out of sin
> i darnos alegría.
and to give us joy.

**Verse 3**

The sparrow answers him:
> Esta nit es Nadal

> es nit de gran contento.
a night of great happiness.
> Lo Verdúm i'l Lluhé,
The greenfinch and the siskin
> diuhen cantant també:
say, singing too:
> ¡Oh, que alegría sento!
Oh, what joy I feel!

**Verse 4**
> Cantava'l Passarell:
The linnet sang:
> Oh, qu'hermós y que bell
Oh, how lovely and how fine
> es l'infant de María;
is Mary's child;
> i lo alegre Tort:
and the merry thrush:
> Vensuda n'es la Mort,
Death is defeated,
> ja naix la vida mía.
now my life is born.

**Verse 5**
> Cantava'l Rossinyol:
The nightingale sang:
> Hermós es com un sol,
He is lovely as a sun,
> brillant com una estrella.
shining like a star.
> La Cotxa i lo Bitxach
The redstart and the stonechat
> festejen al manyach
celebrate the gentle little one
> i a sa Mare donzella.
and his maiden mother.

**Verse 6**
> Cantava'l Reyetó
The goldcrest sang
> per gloria del Senyor,
for the glory of the Lord,
> inflant ab bisarría;
puffing itself up with flair;
> lo Canari segueix;
the canary follows;
> la música pareix
the music seems
> del Cel gran melodía.
a great melody from heaven.

**Verse 7**
> Cantava'l Cotolíu:
The woodlark sang:
> Aucells, veníu, veníu,
Birds, come, come,
> a festejà a la Aurora;
to celebrate the Dawn;
> i lo Merlot, xiulant,
and the blackbird, whistling,
> anava festejant
went celebrating
> a la millor Senyora.
the best of ladies.

**Verse 8**
> L'Estiverola diu:
The great tit says:
> No es hivern ni es istiu,
It is not winter, nor summer,
> sino qu'es primavera;
but it is spring;
> perque ha nat una flor
for a flower is born
> que per tot dona olor,
that gives its scent everywhere,
> en lo Cel i la terra.
in heaven and on earth.

**Verse 9**
> Cantava 'l Francolí:
The francolin sang:
> Aucells, ¿qui vol venir
Birds, who will come
> a glosar melodía,
to make a song in praise,
> a veure 'l gran Senyor
to see the great Lord
> ab son gran resplandor
in his great brightness
> dintre d'una establía?
inside a stable?

**Verse 10**
> Cantava la Puput:
The hoopoe sang:
> Esta nit ha vingut
Tonight has come
> lo Rey de cels y terra.
the King of heaven and earth.
> La Tòrtora i Colom
The turtle dove and the dove
> admiren a tothom
amaze everyone,
> cantant sense tristesa.
singing without sadness.

**Verse 11**
> Pigots i Borroners
Woodpeckers and borroners
> se'n pujen pels fruyters,
climb up into the fruit trees,
> cantant ab alegría;
singing with joy;
> la Guatlla i lo Cu-cut
the quail and the cuckoo
> molt de lluny han vingut
have come from very far

to gaze on the Messiah.

**Verse 12**
> Cantava la Perdiu:
The partridge sang:
> Me 'n vaig a fer lo niu
I am off to make my nest
> dins d'aquella establía,
inside that stable,
> pera veure l'infant
to see the child,
> com està tremolant
how he lies trembling
> en brassos de María.
in Mary's arms.

**Verse 13**
> La Garsa, Griva i Gaig,

> tota planta floreix

The chaffinch sang:

The tawny owl and the eagle-owl
> diuhen: Mirar no puch,
say: I cannot look,
> los resplandors m'admiren
the brightness amazes me.

## How it is sung

It is a song for Christmas night [^briz1874][^capmany1903]. Millet said many carols like it were sung in church at the Missa del Gall, the midnight mass, though they were the people's music, not church music [^millet1912]. The Gran Enciclopèdia Catalana calls its melody melancholy, in a minor key, with an unusual range [^enccat-ocells].

Our arrangement is Millet's harmony, printed in 1917, a tone lower, in F minor, at 60 beats a minute, the speed the *Materials* prints [^millet1917][^materials]. The alto, tenor and bass hum "oo" throughout and open alone for three bars; the soprano then sings verse 1 and verse 2, the eagle, each with its last three lines twice.

## Versions and other words

**Variants and a comic tail.** Capmany's notes say he collected two variants [^capmany1903]. Briz wrote that singers tacked on a tail that hinted at a parody, with peacocks and capons, and a partridge eaten "ab la taronja y pebre", with orange and pepper [^briz1874].

**The smugglers.** The *Materials* of the Obra del Cançoner Popular de Catalunya, in a fascicle printed in May 1928 from Francesc Pujol's song samples for a congress in Vienna in March 1927, prints the carol, "noted and harmonised" by Millet, just after a smugglers' song, "Els contrabandistes", also noted by him. It calls the carol one of the finest in its songbook and nothing other than "Les contrebandiers", the smugglers' song, transfigured [^materials]. That is Pujol's own claim.

**The cello.** Pau Casals arranged it for cello [^paucasals-bio][^enccat-casals]. In exile from 1939 at Prada de Conflent, he played it at the start of his concerts, the Enciclopèdia Catalana says, and it came to be seen, in its words, as a national symbol for Catalans [^enccat-ocells][^enccat-casals].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/el-cant-dels-ocells/soprano): you sing every word, from middle C to the F an octave and a fourth above. "Al veu-re des-pun-tar" rises from C through F, G, A-flat and B-flat to a C held three beats; then you leap to your top F on "lo". On "dit-xo-sa" you fall from E natural to D-flat, an augmented second; tune it with the player. Each "me-lin-dro-sa" drops a fifth to the low F.
- [Alto](/songs/el-cant-dels-ocells/alto): you hum "oo", between the G below middle C and the E above middle C. You open the song with the tenor and bass, rocking C, D-flat, E-flat, C, twice. Under the last syllable of "dit-xo-sa" you rise to your top E natural and echo the tune's turn, E, D-flat, C, a bar after the soprano and an octave lower. Under "los au-cel-lets can-tant" you swing between D-flat and E-flat on every beat.
- [Tenor](/songs/el-cant-dels-ocells/tenor): you hum "oo", from the C below middle C to middle C. Under "los au-cel-lets can-tant" you climb F, A natural, B-flat to your top C. Under "-dro-sa" you dip from F to E natural and back; tune that half step.
- [Bass](/songs/el-cant-dels-ocells/bass): you hum "oo", from low F to the F above it. Through the opening and the first two lines you hold one note, the upper F, until the breath before "-nar" of "llu-mi-nar"; then you walk down E-flat, D-flat, C to "dit-xo-sa", and climb from G to C under "fes-te-jar-lo van ab sa veu".

## Questions

### What does El cant dels ocells mean?

It means "the song of the birds". In its words the birds see "the greater light", the newborn Jesus, dawn in the night and fly to greet him [^millet1917][^genesis]. The eagle sings that Jesus is born to take away sin, and in the last verse even the owls are dazzled [^millet1917].

### Does El cant dels ocells have lyrics?

Yes: fifteen six-line verses in Catalan, in print since at least 1874 [^briz1874][^millet1917]. It is often heard without them because Pau Casals played it on the cello, as at the United Nations in 1971 [^un-av][^paucasals-bio]. In our arrangement the soprano sings two of them while the others hum [^millet1917].

### Who composed El cant dels ocells?

No one knows. The words were printed in 1874 and the tune by 1903, both with no author named [^briz1874][^capmany1903]. Lluís Millet harmonised it for men's voices before 1912 and Pau Casals arranged it for cello [^millet1912][^paucasals-bio]. Its words were in print two years before Casals was born [^briz1874][^loc-casals].

### Why did Pau Casals play El cant dels ocells at the United Nations and the White House?

In exile from 1939 he opened his concerts with it, the Enciclopèdia Catalana says, and it came to stand for Catalonia [^enccat-casals][^enccat-ocells]. From 1946 he would not play in countries tolerating Franco's regime, the United States among them [^paucasals-bio][^nara2013], but on 13 November 1961 he played at the White House dinner the Kennedys gave for Puerto Rico's governor, ending with this song [^stategov][^nara2013][^musicweb]. On 24 October 1971 he played it at the United Nations on receiving its Peace Medal, as a song of hope and peace, his foundation says [^paucasals-un][^un-av].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano, alone and with all the words, as the solo voice does in Millet's 1917 print [^millet1917]. The alto, tenor and bass hum "oo" underneath. With two singers you have the tune and the alto; the bass makes three and the tenor four.
