Leave Her, Johnny, also sung as Leave Her, Johnny, Leave Her and Time for Us to Leave Her, is a sea shanty for the end of a voyage [^sharp][^balladindex][^whall]. It was sung as the voyage ended, as the ship came into port or once she was tied up, and the shantyman used it to say what the crew thought of the ship they were about to leave: the low wages, the hard bread, the salt beef, the leaks [^sharp][^colcord]. The crew answered every complaint with the same goodbye, "It's time for us to leave her" [^sharp][^bullen]. No author is known [^balladindex][^sharp]. Our version is the one John Short, an old shantyman of Watchet in Somerset, sang to the folk-song collector Cecil Sharp in 1914 [^sharp][^csp].

## Where it comes from

**Across the Western Ocean.** The master mariner W. B. Whall, whose Ships, Sea Songs and Shanties first came out in 1910 (we read its third edition, of 1913), printed the song as "Time For Us to Leave Her" and believed it began as an older shanty, "Across the Western Ocean" [^whall][^sharp]. In Whall's account that one came from the packet ships that carried thousands of Irish emigrants over the Atlantic, and he guessed its date at about 1850 [^whall]. When its words no longer fitted, he wrote, someone kept its first line, "Oh, the times are hard and the wages low", and new words grew from it [^whall]. Joanna Colcord, who wrote down the shanties she remembered from an American ship of the late 1800s, also traced the song to "Across the Western Ocean", which she counted among the oldest shanties of the Atlantic packet ships. She gives no date for the song itself, but in her account the packet trade with Liverpool grew up soon after the War of 1812 and brought its great wave of emigrants in the early 1840s, which points earlier than Whall's guess [^colcord].

![[2]]

**The last song of the voyage.** Frank T. Bullen, a sailor turned writer, printed the song in 1914 in Songs of Sea Labour [^bullen]. It "sums up all the hatred of a ship that had been accumulating during the voyage", he wrote. To sing it before the last day or so on board was "almost tantamount to mutiny", and even then captain and officers could resent it fiercely; it was a whole service of cursing in itself, and "often more than justified" [^bullen]. Colcord placed it later still, at the very last job: pumping the ship out once she lay tied up at the pier, with the voyage done and nothing left to lose by telling ship and officers what the crew thought of them [^colcord].

**Who sang the shanties.** Shanties were the work songs of merchant ships, never of the Navy [^sharp][^colcord]. Sharp noted that ships in the trade between Britain and America often sailed with mixed crews, one watch Black and one white [^sharp]. Colcord called Black American singers the best shantymen of all, and wrote that a whole class of shanties began with Black stevedores in the Gulf ports, who rammed cotton bales into ships' holds to their rhythm [^colcord][^whall][^bullen]. The Library of Congress's folklorists count African Americans and Afro-Caribbean people as central to the tradition [^winick]. No source ties this song to a Black singer; its known trail runs back through the Atlantic emigrant packets [^whall][^colcord]. Steam ended the shanty's working life: engines and steam winches did the work the crews had sung to [^sharp][^terry].

**John Short of Watchet.** John Short was baptised in Watchet, Somerset, in 1839 [^csp][^halsway][^sharp]. He sailed deep-water as far as North America and the Far East, and was often the shantyman who led the crew's singing [^csp][^halsway][^sharp]. Sharp gave his age as 76, and he was home after more than fifty years in sailing ships, Watchet's town crier [^sharp][^csp]. Sharp began collecting from him in April that year [^csp][^halsway], and admired a voice "rich, resonant and powerful" that could still turn trills and graces [^sharp]. Short gave him 57 shanties, and 46 of the 60 in English Folk-Chanteys are his [^sharp][^csp]. Sharp was frank about himself: "I have never even heard a chantey sung on board ship" [^sharp].

**The page we read.** In Sharp's book the song is No. II, "Leave her Johnny", first version, on page 3, in the section of capstan chanteys [^sharp]. The tune, with one sharp, has the first verse under it and "Solo." and "Chorus." taking turns line by line. Sharp's piano part is marked soft under the solo and loud under the chorus, and verses 2 to 7 follow, each a single solo line except the fourth [^sharp]. In his notes on page 65, Sharp called Short's variant "the usual form of the air", close to the versions already printed by Bullen and by Tozer [^sharp][^bullen]. Tozer's is No. 5 in Sailors' Songs or "Chanties", with words by Frederick J. Davis and music arranged by Ferris Tozer (London: Boosey), which Sharp dates to 1887 in its first edition [^sharp][^mystic]. We have not found a scan, or which edition first held the song, so the earliest printing we have seen is Whall's [^sharp].

### Timeline

- 1913: Whall's third edition, the earliest printing we have seen (the book first came out in 1910), prints "Time For Us to Leave Her", with the chorus "Leave her, bullies, leave her" [^whall][^sharp]
- 1914: Bullen and Arnold's Songs of Sea Labour prints "Leave Her Johnny" among the windlass and capstan shanties [^bullen]
- 1914: John Short sings it to Cecil Sharp at Watchet, and Sharp prints it in English Folk-Chanteys [^sharp][^csp]
- 1924: Colcord's Roll and Go closes its capstan shanties with it, as the last song of the voyage [^colcord]
- 2008: a statue of John Short is put up on Watchet's seafront [^csp][^halsway]

## What the words mean

We sing the first three of the seven verses in Sharp's book, with his words [^sharp]. Each verse is a grumble from the shantyman, usually one line sung twice, with the crew's answer after each line.

"Her" is the ship, and to leave her is to quit her for good, once she is safe in port [^sharp][^colcord]. "Johnny" is the crew being sung to; other singers made it "Leave her, Johnnies" or "Leave her, bullies" [^whall][^folkinfo].

**"O the times are hard and the wages low."** The pay is poor. This is the line Whall says survived from "Across the Western Ocean" [^whall].

**"The bread is hard and the beef is salt."** On a ship, "bread" was the usual name for biscuit, flat cakes of flour and as little water as possible, slowly baked [^smyth]. The beef is salted meat: the food is poor.

**"O, a leaking ship and a harping crew."** The ship lets in water, which meant hours at the pumps, and the men keep harping, going on and on about it [^webster][^colcord].

In the verses we don't sing, "Our mate he is a bully man, He gives us all the best he can" uses "bully" in its slang sense of fine or excellent [^webster]. Then the singer has no money and no clothes, and his mother writes that she will send both.

### The full words

These are the seven verses of John Short's version, as Cecil Sharp printed them in English Folk-Chanteys (1914). Sharp prints the first verse in full under the tune, and each later verse as its solo line only; here the lines and the crew's answers are written out as they are sung, with the fourth verse's two lines taking the two solo places.

**Verse 1**
O the times are hard and the wages low;
Leave her Johnny leave her;
O the times are hard and the wages low,
It's time for us to leave her.

**Verse 2**
The bread is hard and the beef is salt,
Leave her Johnny leave her;
The bread is hard and the beef is salt,
It's time for us to leave her.

**Verse 3**
O, a leaking ship and a harping crew,
Leave her Johnny leave her;
O, a leaking ship and a harping crew,
It's time for us to leave her.

**Verse 4**
Our mate he is a bully man,
Leave her Johnny leave her;
He gives us all the best he can.
It's time for us to leave her.

**Verse 5**
I've got no money, I've got no clothes,
Leave her Johnny leave her;
I've got no money, I've got no clothes,
It's time for us to leave her.

**Verse 6**
O, my old mother she wrote to me:
Leave her Johnny leave her;
O, my old mother she wrote to me:
It's time for us to leave her.

**Verse 7**
I will send you money, I will send you clothes.
Leave her Johnny leave her;
I will send you money, I will send you clothes.
It's time for us to leave her.

## How it is sung

Sharp and Bullen print it as a capstan shanty, one for steady, even work, marching round the capstan or working the pumps, the shantyman singing the line and the crew answering [^sharp][^bullen]. Colcord and Hugill put it at the last pumping in port, and Hugill also at warping the ship in through the dock locks [^colcord][^folkinfo]. Not everyone agrees on the job: Whall called it a hauling song, and the contents of Terry's second Shanty Book, listed at the back of his first, put "Time for us to leave her" among the halliard shanties [^whall][^terry]. The moment is clearer than the job: Sharp, Bullen, Colcord and Hugill tie it to the end of the voyage, and Whall wrote that "Leave her, bullies, leave her" was often the last song sung before getting ashore, though Colcord and Hugill both knew it at the pumps at sea too [^sharp][^bullen][^colcord][^folkinfo][^whall].

Our arrangement sings verses 1 to 3 for 2 to 4 singers, at a steady working pace, in E dorian as Sharp printed it. The soprano is the shantyman and sings each grumble alone; everyone answers in strong, plain chords on the harmony of Sharp's piano part. The tune is his, with breaths marked for beginners [^sharp].

## Versions and other words

- **"Across the Western Ocean"**: the packet-ship shanty Whall and Colcord name as its parent [^whall][^colcord].
- **"Leave her, bullies, leave her"**: Whall's modern form, in which the old man promises the crew their pay tomorrow [^whall].
- **Bullen's version (1914)**: "Leave her Johnny and we'll work no more", marked "Slow, and with expression", from a crew who have had their fill of the pumps ("Of pump or drown we've had full store") [^bullen].
- **Richard Perkins's version (1914)**: Sharp's second version, No. III on page 4, from St Ives, with the same later verses, in the dorian mode; Sharp heard in its first phrase the opening of the Somerset song "I'm Seventeen Come Sunday" [^sharp][^folkinfo].
- **Colcord's versions (1924)**: one leaves the ship moored at the West Street pier after a hard passage from the Liverpool docks to the Brooklyn yard, and another, for the pumps at sea, has the water rising in the hold [^colcord].
- **The rough verses.** Sailors made up verses against the officers, the food and the owners, many too coarse to print, and Colcord's texts keep some of their oaths [^folkinfo][^colcord]. We don't print them.

## Did you know?

- Sharp thought many shanty tunes sound like hymn tunes, and named the second version of "Leave her Johnny" in his book as one; the sailor, he wrote, was a great singer of hymn tunes [^sharp][^folkinfo].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/leave-her-johnny/soprano): you are the shantyman. Sing each grumble alone, then lead the answers with everyone. Your top D comes the second time you sing each grumble, on "times", "bread" and the "leak-" of "leaking"; the C-sharp just before it is the note that makes the tune dorian.
- [Alto](/songs/leave-her-johnny/alto): you rest through each grumble and come in on the answers, mostly on B. You rise to D on "leave her" at the end of each "Leave her, Johnny, leave her", and on "leave" in "It's time for us to leave her".
- [Tenor](/songs/leave-her-johnny/tenor): you come in on the answers, mostly on a low E, and rise to B on the last "her" of each "Leave her, Johnny, leave her".
- [Bass](/songs/leave-her-johnny/bass): you sing the bass of Sharp's chords on the answers, mostly a low G and D, and climb to the G an octave up at the end of each "Leave her, Johnny, leave her".

## Questions

### What does "Leave Her, Johnny" mean?

It means "quit the ship". "Her" is the ship, and the song was sung as the voyage ended, with the crew about to go ashore and leave her for good [^sharp][^colcord]. Each verse is a complaint, about the pay, the food or the leaking hull, and the chorus answers that it is time to go [^sharp].

### Where does "Leave Her, Johnny" come from?

It is a traditional shanty of the merchant sailing ships, sung at the end of a voyage, and no record says exactly where or when it began [^balladindex][^sharp]. W. B. Whall and Joanna Colcord both traced it to an older shanty of the Atlantic packet ships, "Across the Western Ocean": Whall guessed that one's date at about 1850, and Colcord's account of the packet trade points to the 1840s [^whall][^colcord]. The earliest printing we have seen is Whall's. Sharp knew a version in Tozer's Sailors' Songs, a book he dates to 1887, but we have not found a scan [^whall][^sharp].

### Is "Leave Her, Johnny" Irish?

Not that anyone can show; the Irish thread is Whall's alone. Whall believed it grew out of "Across the Western Ocean", which he tied to the packet ships carrying Irish emigrants across the Atlantic about 1850 [^whall]. Colcord also named "Across the Western Ocean" as its parent, but said nothing of the Irish [^colcord]. The version we sing came from an English sailor, John Short of Watchet in Somerset [^sharp][^csp].

### Who wrote "Leave Her, Johnny"?

No one knows; it is a traditional shanty, made and remade by the sailors who sang it [^balladindex][^whall][^colcord]. No one knows when, either. Its parent song goes back to about 1850 by Whall's guess, and earlier by Colcord's account; the song itself is in print in the 1913 edition of Whall's book we read, and perhaps earlier [^whall][^colcord][^sharp]. Our words and tune are John Short's, as Cecil Sharp printed them in 1914 [^sharp].
