Come, Ye Thankful People, Come is a harvest hymn. Henry Alford, vicar of the village of Wymeswold in Leicestershire, printed it in 1844, new in the hymn book he made for his parish, under the heading "After Harvest" [^ph1844][^life1873]. Its first verse thanks God for a harvest gathered in before the winter. The other three turn to Jesus's parable of the wheat and the tares, and to the harvest at the end of time [^ph1844][^kjv]. Hymns Ancient and Modern set it in 1861 to the tune it is still sung to, ST. GEORGE'S WINDSOR, by George Elvey, organist of St George's Chapel, Windsor [^ham1861][^hymnary-tune]. In England it belongs to the harvest festival [^life1873][^hymnarchive]. American Episcopal hymnals printed it for Thanksgiving from at least 1871 [^pe1871][^pe1892].

## Where it comes from

**The words.** Alford was born in London in 1810 and went to Trinity College, Cambridge [^life1873][^dnb-alford]. In 1835 he became vicar of Wymeswold and stayed eighteen years [^dnb-alford][^julian]. His widow wrote that in the spring of 1844 he was preparing "a larger Hymn Book for the use of his parishioners" [^life1873]. It came out that year in London as *Psalms and Hymns, adapted to the Sundays and Holydays throughout the Year*. Its preface is signed at Wymeswold on 18 April 1844 [^ph1844].

The page shows the hymn as No. CXVI (116), under the heading "After Harvest", in four verses of eight lines. The table of contents gives its meter as "D. sevens" and its author as "H. A.", with the asterisk the book uses for hymns "published in this Collection for the first time" [^ph1844].

A harvest thanksgiving in church was then new again. In September 1843 Robert Stephen Hawker, vicar of Morwenstow in Cornwall, called his people to give thanks for the harvest the next month, with Communion bread made of the new corn. His biographers call him "probably the first" or "one of the first" in the Church of England to do so [^baringgould][^byles]. Nothing we have read ties Alford's hymn to Hawker.

**Other hands, and Alford's own.** The first music edition of Hymns Ancient and Modern, the Church of England hymnal whose preface is dated Lent 1861, printed the hymn as No. 223 under "Harvest" [^ham1861][^julian]. Its compilers rewrote much of it. In their preface they thank the Dean of Canterbury, as Alford had been since 1857, for its use, and add in a footnote: "considerable alterations were made by them in this Hymn, without his sanction; and ... he wishes not to be considered responsible for it in its present form" [^ham1861][^dnb-alford][^hymnarchive-alford]. Julian's Dictionary of Hymnology says the compilers added such a note in later editions; the 1861 book already has it [^julian][^ham1861].

Alford answered in the fourth edition of his Poetical Works in 1865. There the hymn is "After Harvest (1844)", with a footnote: it had been "in various collections much disfigured by alterations made without the author's consent", and he was "responsible for this form of it only" [^pw1865][^hymnarchive]. The Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology dates this note to the fifth edition, of 1868; the 1865 printing already has it [^canterbury][^pw1865]. A second footnote records his own change. His 1844 lines, "From his field shall purge away / All that doth offend that day", now read "From His field shall in that day / All offences purge away". He made it, he says, at the request of the Tract Committee of the Christian Knowledge Society, the SPCK, the old wording "possibly bearing an ambiguous meaning" [^pw1865][^ph1844][^canterbury].

In 1867 he revised it once more for The Year of Praise, a hymnal he edited for use in Canterbury Cathedral. Verse 2 now opens "All the world is God's own field" instead of "We ourselves are God's own field", and verse 4 is largely rewritten, opening "Even so, Lord, quickly come", though it keeps three of its 1844 lines, among them "Free from sorrow, free from sin" [^yop1867][^ph1844][^hymnarchive]. He died at the Deanery in Canterbury in January 1871 [^life1873][^hymnarchive-alford].

**The tune.** George Job Elvey was born at Canterbury in 1816 and sang as a boy in the cathedral choir there [^dnb-elvey][^hymnary-elvey]. In 1835, at nineteen, he became organist of St George's Chapel, Windsor, and kept the post until 1882. He was knighted in 1871 and died at Windlesham in Surrey in 1893 [^dnb-elvey][^hymnary-elvey]. The earliest known printing of his tune is in E. H. Thorne's *A Selection of Psalm and Hymn Tunes* of 1858, and it is named after his chapel [^hymnary-tune][^hymnarchive]. Hymns Ancient and Modern thanked "Dr. G. J. Elvey, for the use of the tune" and called it "St. George" in its index of tunes [^ham1861]. Elvey's entry in the Dictionary of National Biography names it among his most popular works [^dnb-elvey].

**In America.** The Episcopal Church's Hymnal of 1871, printed in Philadelphia, has it as No. 289, in a section headed "Thanksgiving and Harvest-Home", with the 1861 text and, above it, a line from Isaiah 9:3 [^pe1871][^kjv]. In that church's Hymnal of 1892 it is No. 193 under "Thanksgiving Day" [^pe1892]. How it first crossed, and who first sang it for Thanksgiving, the sources we read don't say.

## What the words mean

The hymn moves from a farm harvest to God's harvest at the end of the world. Behind it is Jesus's parable in Matthew 13: an enemy sows tares, a weed, among a farmer's wheat; both grow until harvest, when the tares are burned and the wheat is stored. The field, Jesus explains, is the world, the harvest the end of the world, the reapers angels [^kjv].

1. The harvest is in, before the winter storms. "Harvest-home" was the old name for the time of harvest, for the feast when the corn was gathered in, and for the reapers' song at it [^webster1828]; "ere" means before. God has given what we need, so come to church and give thanks.
2. The world, like a farm, is God's field, growing fruit for his praise. Wheat and tares grow side by side, toward joy or sorrow. "First the blade, and then the ear, / Then the full corn" is from another of Jesus's farming parables, in Mark 4: the shoot, then the head of grain, then the ripe grain. "Corn" here is any grain, not maize. The prayer: make us good grain.
3. God will come and bring his own harvest home. On that day he will clear from his field "all offences", Matthew's "all things that offend", meaning everything that leads people into sin. The angels will burn the tares and store the good grain in his "garner", his granary, for ever [^kjv].
4. "Even so, Lord, quickly come" echoes a prayer near the very end of the Bible, in Revelation 22: "Even so, come, Lord Jesus." Gather your people in at the final harvest, free of sorrow and sin, to live with you, and let all the angels sing [^kjv][^yop1867].

### The full words

These are Alford's last revision, as printed in The Year of Praise in 1867 as Hymn 275, "Harvest", with its spelling. Alford printed a colon and a dash after "supplied"; we print the colon [^yop1867]. We sing verse 1 as Hymns Ancient and Modern printed it in 1861, the same words with its own capitals and stops, and verse 4 as given here [^ham1861][^yop1867].

**Verse 1**
Come, ye thankful people, come,
Raise the song of Harvest-home!
All is safely gathered in,
Ere the winter storms begin:
God our Maker doth provide
For our wants to be supplied:
Come to God's own temple, come,
Raise the song of Harvest-home!

**Verse 2**
All the world is God's own field,
Fruit unto His praise to yield;
Wheat and tares together sown,
Unto joy or sorrow grown:
First the blade, and then the ear,
Then the full corn shall appear:
Lord of Harvest, grant that we
Wholesome grain and pure may be.

**Verse 3**
For the Lord our God shall come,
And shall take His Harvest home:
From His field shall in that day
All offences purge away:
Give His angels charge at last
In the fire the tares to cast;
But the fruitful ears to store
In His garner evermore.

**Verse 4**
Even so, Lord, quickly come,
To Thy final Harvest-home;
Gather Thou Thy people in,
Free from sorrow, free from sin;
There for ever purified,
In Thy presence to abide:
Come, with all Thine angels, come,
Raise the glorious Harvest-home!

## How it is sung

In England it is a hymn for the harvest festival. When a figure of Alford was unveiled at Canterbury Cathedral, the Dean, Dr. Payne Smith, spoke of "that Harvest Hymn" as sung "in church throughout England at that season of the year" [^life1873]. A modern annotated anthology of hymns, quoted by Hymnology Archive, calls it, with Elvey's tune, a feature of harvest festivals [^hymnarchive]. In America it is a Thanksgiving hymn [^julian][^pe1892].

The tune is ST. GEORGE'S WINDSOR, in 7.7.7.7 D meter: eight lines of seven syllables [^ham1861]. It has gone by other names: "St. George" in 1861, and "Elvey" in 1867, when Alford kept it for his hymn [^ham1861][^yop1867][^hymnary-tune][^hymnarchive].

Our arrangement sings verse 1 and Alford's 1867 last verse, "Even so, Lord, quickly come", in G and in 4/4, on Elvey's four parts as Hymns Ancient and Modern printed them in 1861 as Hymn 223, made easier for beginners, for 2 to 4 singers [^ham1861][^yop1867]. The soprano sings Elvey's tune unchanged, every part sings one note to a syllable, and each line is one breath [^ham1861].

## Versions and other words

Three texts are Alford's. The 1844 one has "We ourselves are God's own field" and a last verse calling on the "Church triumphant" and "ten thousand angels" [^ph1844]. In 1865 he changed only verse 3 [^pw1865]; in 1867, verses 2 and 4 [^yop1867]. Sources differ on which one is standard. Julian's Dictionary of Hymnology (1907) called the 1865 text the authorized one, and the one usually given in hymnals. The Psalter Hymnal Handbook (1987), quoted on Hymnary.org, traces its own hymnal's text to 1867 instead [^julian]. Some books mix them: the American Episcopal Hymnal of 1892 prints the 1867 text, but with the earlier line "Grant, O harvest Lord, that we", as in 1844 and 1865 [^pe1892][^ph1844][^pw1865].

A fourth text is not his: the 1861 Hymns Ancient and Modern rewrite, which asks "What is earth but God's own field?" and gathers the saints "upon the golden floor" [^ham1861].

Julian's Dictionary of Hymnology and the Psalter Hymnal Handbook, both quoted on Hymnary.org, give the hymn seven verses, and Hymnary's notes have Alford cutting it to four in 1865 [^julian]. The 1844 page has four, and so do the 1865 and 1867 printings [^ph1844][^pw1865][^yop1867][^hymnarchive].

## Did you know?

- In 1861 Hymns Ancient and Modern used Elvey's tune for this hymn and for the Easter hymn "Christ the Lord is risen to-day", and its preface defends the choice: the "joy of Harvest" and that of Easter "are not so unconnected" [^ham1861].
- A figure of Alford stands on the north-west buttress of Canterbury Cathedral's west front, beside Erasmus, carved by Theodore Phyffers [^life1873][^canarch].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/come-ye-thankful-people/soprano): you carry Elvey's tune, and half its lines open the same way, a long B and a short one ("Come, ye", "Raise the", "All is", "For our"). Line 4 climbs B, C-sharp, D to "win-", leaps down to G on "-ter", then steps down to your low D on the last syllable of "begin"; line 6 climbs C, D, E to your top E on the last syllable of "supplied", and line 7 starts up there, on "Come to God's own Temple".
- [Alto](/songs/come-ye-thankful-people/alto): you stay on D for "Come, ye thankful" and rise step by step to G on "come". Watch the sharps: a D-sharp on line 2's "Home!", and a G-sharp on "Come to" in line 7, right after you climb D, E, F natural, G under "to be supplied". You end low, on B under the last "Home!".
- [Tenor](/songs/come-ye-thankful-people/tenor): you climb from G to D across the first line, "Come, ye thankful people, come". Lines 4 and 5 sit mostly on A ("storms begin; God, our Maker"), line 7 jumps from B up to your top E on "to God's", and you end on your low D under the last "Home!".
- [Bass](/songs/come-ye-thankful-people/bass): you sing the bass line, on the chord's root about two thirds of the time, and two lines are scales. Line 5 walks down from D to your low G on "God, our Maker, doth provide", and line 6 walks down again from G, through F natural, to C on "to be supplied". Then a G-sharp on "Come" opens line 7, and you finish on the low G.

## Questions

### What does "harvest home" mean in the hymn?

Harvest-home was the old English name for the time of harvest, for the feast when the corn was gathered in, and for the reapers' song at that feast. To "raise the song of Harvest-home" is to sing that thanks, in church [^webster1828][^yop1867]. In verses 3 and 4 the word turns to the harvest at the end of the world, when God "shall take His Harvest home" [^yop1867][^kjv].

### Which verses does our arrangement sing?

The first verse and Alford's last verse of 1867, "Even so, Lord, quickly come", on Elvey's own four parts from the first Hymns Ancient and Modern of 1861, made easier for beginners [^yop1867][^ham1861]. Verses 2 and 3, on the wheat and the tares, are printed in full above.

### What are the original words?

The original is Alford's text of 1844: four verses, headed "After Harvest", the second beginning "We ourselves are God's own field" [^ph1844]. He revised it in 1865 and 1867, and disowned the rewrite Hymns Ancient and Modern printed in 1861 [^pw1865][^yop1867][^ham1861].
