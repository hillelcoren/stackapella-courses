The Hallelujah chorus, or simply the Hallelujah, is the chorus that closes Part Two of Messiah, George Frideric Handel's oratorio on the life of Christ, sung entirely in words from the Bible [^wordbook1750][^vickers2023]. Its text, chosen by Charles Jennens, joins three verses from the Book of Revelation: "Hallelujah, for the Lord God Omnipotent reigneth", "The kingdom of this world is become the kingdom of our Lord, and of his Christ", and "King of Kings, and Lord of Lords" [^wordbook1750][^kjv]. Handel dated its last page 6 September 1741 [^autograph][^grove1880]. Audiences rise for it, a custom already told of in 1780 and still kept when the Dictionary of National Biography described it in 1890 [^forbes1806][^dnb-handel]. We sing the whole chorus in Handel's four voice parts, in his key, D, for four singers.

## Where it comes from

**The words.** Charles Jennens, born in 1700, was the only surviving son of a family of Gopsall, in Leicestershire, and wrote the words of Handel's oratorios Saul, Messiah and Belshazzar [^dnb-jennens][^vickers2023]. Messiah's words are not new verse but a collection of verses from the Bible, chosen and ordered by Jennens [^vickers2023][^tobin]. On 10 July 1741 he wrote to his friend Edward Holdsworth that Handel "says he will do nothing next Winter", but that he hoped to persuade him to set "another Scripture Collection I have made for him", adding: "The Subject is Messiah" [^vickers2023][^jhcorr]. Jennens's and Holdsworth's letters are now in the Gerald Coke Handel Collection at the Foundling Museum in London [^jhcorr].

**Twenty-four days.** Handel's own score of Messiah, now in the British Library, opens in his hand with "Messiah, an Oratorio. Part the First", and at the foot of its first page is a note, partly struck through, with "22 August" and "1741": the day he began [^autograph][^grove1880]. He finished Part One on 28 August [^grove1880][^vickers2023]. The Hallelujah is the end of Part Two, and its last page, folio 106, is signed off "Septembr 6. 1741", in ink, under the last bar of the choir's final "Hallelujah" [^autograph][^vickers2023]. Grove's dictionary records Part Three as done by 12 September, and the whole score was finished by 14 September, twenty-four days in all [^grove1880][^autograph][^vickers2023].

**Dublin, 1742.** That autumn Handel went to Dublin, arriving on 18 November 1741, and gave concerts in the new Musick Hall in Fishamble Street [^townsend1852][^jhcorr]. On 29 December he wrote to Jennens of "your Oratorio Messiah, which I set to Musick before I left England" [^townsend1852]. Its first performance was at that hall at noon on Tuesday 13 April 1742, for three charities: the relief of prisoners in the city's jails, Mercer's Hospital and the Charitable Infirmary [^townsend1852][^grove1880]. The singers of both Dublin cathedrals took part [^townsend1852][^tobin]. To fit in more people, the stewards asked ladies to come without hoops in their skirts and gentlemen without their swords [^townsend1852][^schoelcher1857]. Faulkner's Dublin Journal reported that "Words are wanting to express the exquisite Delight it afforded to the admiring crowded Audience", that above 700 people were in the room, and that about £400 was raised [^townsend1852][^vickers2023].

**London, 1743.** Messiah reached London at the Covent Garden theatre on 23 March 1743, advertised as "A New Sacred Oratorio" [^grove1880][^vickers2023][^tobin]. For it Jennens saw to an accurate wordbook, with the motto "Majora canamus", "let us sing of greater things", on its title page; later London wordbooks keep it, among them that of 1750, the one we read [^vickers2023][^wordbook1750]. There the Hallelujah is printed simply as "Chorus", the last item before Part the Third [^wordbook1750]. Jennens was never fully pleased with the music. In 1745 he wrote that Handel "has made a fine Entertainment of it, tho' not near so good as he might & ought to have done" [^vickers2023][^tobin].

**Myth and record.** Often told: at the first London performance King George II was so moved by the Hallelujah that he stood up, the audience stood with him, and audiences have stood ever since. What the record shows: the story comes from a letter the Scottish poet James Beattie wrote from Aberdeen on 25 May 1780, thirty-seven years later, passing on what Lord Kinnoull had told him [^forbes1806][^townsend1852]. Beattie wrote that when Messiah "was first performed" the audience, at "For the Lord God Omnipotent reigneth", were so moved "that they all, together with the King, (who happened to be present) started up, and remained standing till the chorus ended", and "hence it became the fashion in England" [^forbes1806]. He named neither the year nor which king, and Messiah's very first performance was in Dublin, not London [^townsend1852][^grove1880]. Writers of the next century placed it at Covent Garden on 23 March 1743, with George II [^dnb-handel]. So the King's part in it is tradition. The standing itself is real: the Dictionary of National Biography called it a custom "which has continued till the present day" in 1890 [^dnb-handel].

**The Foundling Hospital.** In May 1749 Handel offered a concert to raise money for the chapel of the Foundling Hospital in London, a home for abandoned babies, and was made one of its governors [^brownlow1847][^dnb-handel]. On 1 May 1750 he gave Messiah in that chapel, its first performance in a place of worship, and he gave it there every spring to the end of his life [^dnb-handel][^tobin][^grove1880][^burney1785]. Charles Burney counted the takings of these performances under Handel at £6,935, and wrote of the oratorio that "it has fed the hungry, clothed the naked, fostered the orphan" [^burney1785][^dnb-handel]. Handel died in London on 14 April 1759 [^dnb-handel][^vickers2023]. His will left "a fair copy of the score, and all the parts of my oratorio called The Messiah to the Foundling Hospital" [^brownlow1847][^prout1902].

**Mozart and the printed scores.** The first complete printed score of Messiah came out in London in 1767, eight years after Handel's death, from Randall and Abell, the successors of Handel's publisher Walsh [^prout1902][^vickers2023]. In March 1789 Mozart rescored Messiah for Baron Gottfried van Swieten's concerts in Vienna, adding wind parts; it was first heard at Count Johann Esterházy's on 6 March 1789 [^koechel1862][^holschneider][^imslp-k572]. Breitkopf & Härtel of Leipzig printed it in 1803, "nach W. A. Mozart's Bearbeitung", after Mozart's arrangement, with changes by Johann Adam Hiller [^prout1902][^imslp-k572]. In the Hallelujah, Ebenezer Prout complained, Mozart's score had Handel's trumpet parts "cut out altogether" or so changed that their character was destroyed [^prout1902][^holschneider]. Prout's own edition, published by Novello in London in 1902, went back to Handel's manuscript, which a photographic facsimile had made easy to study, and it is the edition we sing from [^prout1902][^grove1880].

## What the words mean

The chorus is the climax of Part Two, which follows Christ's suffering, death, resurrection and ascension, and ends with God's triumph over those who reject him [^vickers2023]. Jennens takes its words from three verses of Revelation, the last book of the Bible, in which John describes his visions of heaven [^kjv].

**Hallelujah.** A Hebrew word, "praise ye Jah", that is, praise the Lord [^webster-halleluiah]. The King James Bible spells it Alleluia in this verse; the wordbook spells it Hallelujah [^kjv][^wordbook1750].

**"For the Lord God Omnipotent reigneth."** In Revelation 19:6 John hears "the voice of a great multitude, and as the voice of many waters, and as the voice of mighty thunderings", crying out these words [^kjv]. "Omnipotent" means almighty, all-powerful [^webster-omnipotent], and "reigneth" is the old form of "reigns".

**"The kingdom of this world is become the kingdom of our Lord, and of his Christ."** From Revelation 11:15, where a seventh angel sounds his trumpet and voices in heaven proclaim it [^kjv]. "Is become" is old English for "has become": the world's rule now belongs to God and to Christ, his anointed one. The King James Bible has the plural, "The kingdoms of this world are become the kingdoms of our Lord"; Jennens's wordbook makes it one kingdom [^kjv][^wordbook1750].

**"And he shall reign for ever and ever."** The rest of the same verse [^kjv].

**"King of Kings, and Lord of Lords."** From Revelation 19:16, a name John sees written "on his vesture and on his thigh": the King above all kings, the Lord above all lords [^kjv].

### The full words

These are the words as the London wordbook of 1750 prints them, with its capitals and the long s made modern [^wordbook1750]. Handel repeats them many times over; the page shows the words as we sing them.

Hallelujah, for the Lord God Omnipotent reigneth.
The Kingdom of this World is become
the Kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ;
and he shall reign for ever and ever.
King of Kings, and Lord of Lords.
Hallelujah.

## How it is sung

In Handel's day Messiah was a concert work, heard in theatres and concert rooms, and from 1750 in the Foundling Hospital chapel each spring [^tobin][^vickers2023]. Today it is just as often a Christmas work: in Boston, the Handel and Haydn Society has sung it "every year since 1854 at Christmastide without interruption", by its own account [^hh]. Wherever Messiah is sung, the Hallelujah ends Part Two, before the soprano's "I know that my Redeemer liveth" opens Part Three [^wordbook1750][^vickers2023].

Handel builds it from short blocks [^prout1902]. All four parts shout "Hallelujah" together; then all sing "For the Lord God Omnipotent reigneth" in unison, on the same notes in their own octaves. Then that line passes from part to part while the others answer "Hallelujah". "The kingdom of this world" is quiet, in long four-part chords. "And He shall reign for ever and ever" enters part by part, bass, tenor, alto, then soprano, and under the long notes of "King of Kings, and Lord of Lords" the other parts call "for ever and ever, Hallelujah". Prout notes that the soft marking at "The kingdom of this world" is tradition, not Handel's: there is no authority for it in the autograph, so he printed it in brackets [^prout1902].

Our arrangement is read from Prout's full score of 1902, No. 44, "Allegro", crotchet = 72, in D [^prout1902]. We sing the whole chorus in Handel's four voice parts, in his key, without the orchestra: its three bars of introduction are left out, so the song starts at the choir's first "Hallelujah". We take it at 60 beats a minute rather than 72, so that Handel's short rests are long enough to breathe, and we have written in a few more breaths for beginners; it lasts about six minutes. We keep Prout's two corrections from the autograph: the places where "jah" and the next "Hal-" share one note, and the tenor's "and He shall reign, and He shall reign" near the end, which every earlier edition had wrong [^prout1902]. It is for four singers only, because the parts take turns with the tunes.

## Versions and other words

Mozart's Messiah of 1789, Der Messias, is sung to German words, and its Hallelujah keeps Handel's voice parts under new wind parts [^holschneider][^prout1902]. Mozart's own manuscript of the arrangement is lost, so how much of the printed score of 1803 is his, and how much Hiller's, cannot be settled [^prout1902][^koechel1862]. In Britain Messiah was long sung with these added parts; Prout wrote his edition to give a corrected text of what Handel wrote, with new accompaniments of his own [^prout1902]. The Dictionary of National Biography says it "had little success" [^dnb-prout].

## Did you know?

- In Prout's score the Hallelujah's opening bars are marked "senza Rip.", for a few of the string players only, and "con Rip.", the whole band, a bar after the choir comes in. Handel wrote these markings into the score he conducted in Dublin [^prout1902][^tobin].
- Handel's will gave the parts of Messiah to the Foundling Hospital, and they survive: the editor of the Halle Handel Edition calls them the only complete set of eighteenth-century parts [^tobin][^brownlow1847].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/hallelujah-chorus/soprano): you sing the top line, most often on D, then A and E. Your one top A comes on "He" as the sopranos first come in with "and He shall reign". Then comes the long climb: "King of Kings, and Lord of Lords" on held notes that rise A, D, E, F-sharp, G, while the others sing "for ever and ever" under you. Your low D comes on "Om-" in the first "Lord God Omnipotent" and on "world" and "come" in "The kingdom of this world is become".
- [Alto](/songs/hallelujah-chorus/alto): you sing the alto line, most often on A, then D and F-sharp. You twice lead "for the Lord God Omnipotent" while the others answer "Hallelujah", and there Handel drops you an octave to your low G on "Om-". Your top D comes on "He" each time the altos enter with "and He shall reign".
- [Tenor](/songs/hallelujah-chorus/tenor): you sing the tenor line, most often on D, then A and F-sharp. Your top A comes on the "lu" of the Hallelujah just before your "for the Lord God Omnipotent", and on "He" as the tenors enter with "and He shall reign". Your low D comes on "Om-" of "Omnipotent" and on the "dom" of "The kingdom of this world".
- [Bass](/songs/hallelujah-chorus/bass): you sing the bass line, most often on D, then A and F-sharp, and you open "and He shall reign", alone, before the others enter one by one. You reach your top D often, 51 times, among them "God" in "Lord God Omnipotent" and "Kings" and "Lords" near the end. Your low G comes only once, on "Om-" when the altos, tenors and basses sing "for the Lord God Omnipotent" together.

## Questions

### Why do people stand for the Hallelujah Chorus?

By custom, and the custom is old: the Dictionary of National Biography described it in 1890 as continuing "till the present day" [^dnb-handel]. The usual explanation, that King George II stood up at the first London performance in 1743, rests on a letter James Beattie wrote in 1780, at second hand from Lord Kinnoull, which names neither the year nor which king [^forbes1806][^townsend1852]. The King's part is tradition; the standing itself is an old custom.

### What Bible verses are in the Hallelujah Chorus?

Three verses from the Book of Revelation in the King James Bible: 19:6, "Alleluia: for the Lord God omnipotent reigneth"; 11:15, "The kingdoms of this world are become the kingdoms of our Lord, and of his Christ; and he shall reign for ever and ever"; and 19:16, "King of Kings, and Lord of Lords" [^kjv]. Charles Jennens chose them, and his wordbook turns "kingdoms" into the singular "Kingdom" [^wordbook1750][^vickers2023].

### When was the Hallelujah Chorus written?

In the late summer of 1741. Handel began Messiah in London on 22 August and dated the last page of the Hallelujah, the end of Part Two, 6 September 1741; the whole oratorio was finished by 14 September [^autograph][^grove1880][^vickers2023]. It was first heard in Dublin on 13 April 1742 [^townsend1852][^grove1880].

### Which part sings the tune?

There is no single tune. The soprano has the long notes of "King of Kings, and Lord of Lords" climbing from A to G, but "for the Lord God Omnipotent" passes from part to part while the others answer "Hallelujah", and "And He shall reign" starts in the bass and climbs through tenor and alto to the soprano [^prout1902]. That is why all four singers are needed.
