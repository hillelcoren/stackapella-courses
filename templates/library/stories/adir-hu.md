Adir Hu is a Hebrew hymn sung at the Passover seder, the home service and festive meal on the first nights of the festival.[^je][^idel29] In the Ashkenazi rite it comes after the formal service is over, among the songs that close the evening.[^ej][^sefaria] Its verses run through the Hebrew alphabet, praising Hashem as mighty, chosen, great, holy and more, and each one ends with the same plea: build Your house, the Temple, soon.[^je][^ej][^sefaria] Unlike most seder songs, it has one tune nearly everywhere. The Jewish Encyclopedia of 1901 noted that while the other seder hymns had many tunes, this one was sung to the same melody, and synagogues borrowed it to mark the festival in their own prayers.[^je][^ej]

## Where it comes from

No one knows who wrote the words. Most sources say Adir Hu came late to the Haggadah, the book of the seder. The Jewish Encyclopedia says it is not found there much before the end of the fifteenth century, and the Union Haggadah of 1923 says that in the fifteenth century "the two anonymous ditties 'Addir Hu' and 'Ki Lo Noeh' were added".[^je][^uh1923] The Encyclopaedia Judaica counts it among the hymns added in the Middle Ages and in print from the sixteenth century.[^ej] A. Z. Idelsohn, the historian of Jewish music, places it more loosely among folk songs for the seder composed "from the tenth century on".[^idel29] It was not only a Passover song. In the prayer book of the Jews of Avignon it was a hymn for all the festivals.[^je][^ej]

Adir Hu has a twin. Ki Lo Na'eh, sung just before it, runs through the alphabet in the same way.[^je][^sefaria] The Union Haggadah's notes say the two were added in the fifteenth century; the Jewish Encyclopedia says Adir Hu was taken up a little later, as a companion to Ki Lo Na'eh.[^uh1923][^je] The Jewish Encyclopedia saw the difference in their aims: Ki Lo Na'eh praises the power that can redeem Israel, while Adir Hu prays to that power to hurry and rebuild.[^je]

A Judeo-German version, "Allmächtiger Gott, nun bau dein' Tempel schiere", "Almighty Hashem, now build Your Temple soon", was once widely sung, and the Jewish Encyclopedia found it still in use in 1901.[^je][^idel29] Like the Hebrew, its verses changed the name each time: Almighty Hashem, Merciful Hashem, and so on.[^je] Idelsohn writes that it was called the Baugesang, the song of the rebuilding of the Temple.[^idel29]

The tune's earliest known printing is in a Haggadah with Latin and German translations, published at Königsberg in 1644 by J. S. Rittangel, a Christian Hebraist who taught Oriental languages there.[^je][^idel29][^brown][^braginsky][^chalmers] His book gives the tunes of songs sung at the close of the seder, and the Jewish Encyclopedia says he printed it with a bass part.[^je][^braginsky][^niv] The 1644 form is plainer than ours. The encyclopedia called it "a droning intonation rather than a set melody", and thought the basses at the table, singing "seconds" under it, soon reached phrases nearer the tune sung in 1901.[^je] Idelsohn traced that change through two more printings, both at Leipzig, in 1677 and 1769. The Jewish Encyclopedia found the 1769 form "almost precisely" the tune sung in North Germany in its own day, 1901.[^idel29][^je]

Where the tune came from is not settled. The Jewish Encyclopedia took it for the work of a Jewish singer of the early seventeenth century, still fairly new in 1644. The Encyclopaedia Judaica says it may be older than 1644.[^je][^ej] Idelsohn called its character "decidedly German", though, he wrote, no identical German tune had yet been found. He compared some of its turns of phrase with German songs, among them a cradle song of Mary of 1544 and a pilgrims' song of 1537.[^idel29] Idelsohn notes that even the old Jewish communities of southern France, whose tunes differ from those of every other community, took up this Ashkenazi tune.[^idel29]

## What the words mean

The title is the first words: adir is mighty, hu is he. "He is mighty."[^sefaria] The full hymn has eight stanzas. The first has one word, for the letter alef, and the other seven have three words each, so the names march from bet to the last letter, tav.[^je][^sefaria] The Union Haggadah, which we sing, prints three verses that pick names from across the hymn, each verse keeping alphabet order.[^uh1923][^sefaria]

**The refrain, in every verse.** Yivne veito bekarov: may He build His house soon. Bimhera, bimhera, beyameinu bekarov: speedily, speedily, in our days, soon. Kel bene, Kel bene, b'ne veitcha bekarov: Hashem, build; Hashem, build; build Your house soon.[^sefaria] His house is the Temple in Jerusalem.[^ej][^je][^idel29] The first line speaks about Hashem, "His house"; the last speaks to Hashem, "Your house".[^sefaria]

**Verse 1.** Adir hu: He is mighty. The book prints it twice, and our tune sings it a third time to fill the bars the other verses fill with three names.[^uh1923]

**Verse 2.** Bachur hu, gadol hu, yachid hu: He is chosen, He is great, He is one. The first two open the hymn's second stanza; yachid, "one" or "only", comes from the fourth.[^sefaria][^uh1923]

**Verse 3.** Tzadik hu, kadosh hu, rachum hu: He is righteous, He is holy, He is compassionate. These come from the hymn's last two stanzas.[^sefaria][^uh1923]

Where the Hebrew has El, one of Hashem's names, we sing Kel, the substituted name customary when singing outside prayer. Many Jews change Hashem's names this way when saying them outside prayer, out of reverence and to avoid taking the name in vain.[^ohr]

## How it is sung

Adir Hu is above all a song of the Ashkenazi seder. In the traditional order it comes after the Hallel psalms and the closing words of the service: first Ki Lo Na'eh, then Adir Hu, and later the counting song Echad Mi Yodea and Chad Gadya.[^sefaria][^ej] The Union Haggadah of the Reform movement keeps the songs after the fourth cup of wine but puts Adir Hu before Ki Lo Na'eh.[^uh1923] The Jewish Encyclopedia thought the tune changed little once it took this shape, because a family at the table, children included, could sing it without the hard turns of synagogue chant.[^je]

Our arrangement is read from page 81 of the Union Haggadah, revised edition, published by the Central Conference of American Rabbis in 1923. There it is headed "Chorus", marked Maestoso and "Traditional", in C major and 4/4, for voice and piano.[^uh1923] We sing it at a stately pace, with four parts of our own; the piano part was used only to check the chords. The book spells the Hebrew in the Ashkenazi way, "yivneh veso b'korov"; we respell it in modern Israeli pronunciation, syllable for syllable as the notes carry it, so the print's "b'korov" becomes "bekarov".[^uh1923] We sing its three verses for 2 to 4 singers: soprano and alto, then the bass, then the tenor. Two short breaths are written in for beginners.

## Versions and other words

Rabbi Gustav Gottheil wrote "God of Might, God of Right, Thee we give all glory" to the tune. It is not a translation: its verses praise Hashem for the freedom story told each year at Passover. The Union Hymnal printed it in 1897, and the Union Haggadah prints it on the page before Adir Hu.[^uh1897][^uh1923][^hymnary]

## Did you know?

- The Jewish Encyclopedia called the chanting of the responses of Psalm 118 to the Adir Hu tune an old custom, but not as old as 1644: Rittangel wrote that those verses then had a melody of their own. The Union Haggadah of 1923 still sets Psalm 118's opening verses to a variation of the tune, and the Encyclopaedia Judaica adds the Kaddish over the Torah scroll and the Priestly Blessing to the prayers sung to it.[^je][^uh1923][^ej]
- The three earliest printings of the tune that Idelsohn lists were all made by Christians. Rittangel was a Christian Hebraist at Königsberg, though his religion at birth is uncertain. Idelsohn calls the other two converts: Christiani, once the cantor at Bruchsal, who the Jewish Encyclopedia says later returned to Judaism, and Gottfried Selig, whose weekly Der Jude explained Jewish customs to German readers.[^idel29][^brown][^niv][^jechristiani][^kestenbaum]

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/adir-hu/soprano): the tune, most often on G, then C. Each verse opens low, on E, for the names, then climbs: the top E comes on the second "bimhera," and on "veitcha" in the last line, which settles back on C.
- [Alto](/songs/adir-hu/alto): a third or a sixth under the tune most of the time, mostly on E. Its top A comes only on "bimhera," and its low B only on the "bekarov." after "beyameinu", in every verse.
- [Tenor](/songs/adir-hu/tenor): the middle of the chord on just five notes, mostly on G. It rises to C on the third "adir hu,", on "yachid hu," and "rachum hu,", on every second "bimhera," and every "beyameinu", and dips to F on the first "bekarov," and the one after "beyameinu".
- [Bass](/songs/adir-hu/bass): mostly the roots of the chords, on a low C most of the time, holding it under the names that open each verse. It reaches its top G only on "veito" and drops to its low F on the last "bekarov." of every verse.

## Questions

### What does Adir Hu mean?

"He is mighty." Adir means mighty and hu means he. The rest of the hymn names Hashem the same way, chosen, great, holy and more, in the order of the Hebrew alphabet, and every verse asks: may He build His house, the Temple, soon, speedily, in our days.[^sefaria][^je][^ej]

### Is there an English version of Adir Hu?

There is an English hymn to its tune, though it is not a translation. Rabbi Gustav Gottheil's "God of Might, God of Right" was printed in the Union Hymnal of 1897 and the Union Haggadah. It praises Hashem for the Passover story of freedom rather than asking for the Temple.[^uh1897][^uh1923][^hymnary]

### When is Adir Hu sung at the Passover seder?

Near the end, after the meal, the Hallel psalms and the words that close the formal service. In the traditional Ashkenazi order it follows Ki Lo Na'eh and comes before Echad Mi Yodea and Chad Gadya. The Encyclopaedia Judaica places it in the Ashkenazi rite, and the Union Haggadah sings it after the fourth cup of wine.[^sefaria][^ej][^uh1923]

### Why does this version sing "Kel" and not "El"?

El is one of Hashem's names. Many Jews alter Hashem's names when saying them outside prayer, out of reverence and to avoid using the name in vain, so El becomes Kel. Our Jewish songs, sung outside prayer, use the altered forms; the meaning is the same.[^ohr]

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune throughout, as the Union Haggadah prints it. The alto moves with it, mostly a third or a sixth below. With two singers it is soprano and alto; with three the bass joins on the roots of the chords; with four the tenor fills the middle.[^uh1923]
