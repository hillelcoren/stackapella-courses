Away in a Manger is a children's Christmas carol about the baby Jesus asleep in the hay. Its words are anonymous and American. Hymnology Archive reports eight of its lines in a newspaper story of 1868, and its first two verses were in print by 1882 under the heading "Luther's Cradle Song" [^hymnarchive][^cynosure1882]. Martin Luther had nothing to do with it [^hill][^hymnarchive]. It has two main tunes: James R. Murray's of 1887, the most printed setting Hill found, and William J. Kirkpatrick's of 1895, which the Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology says Britain always sings [^hill][^hymnarchive][^canterbury]. By 1945 the scholar Richard S. Hill had counted forty-one settings of these few lines [^hill][^hymnarchive].

## Where it comes from

**The words.** Hymnology Archive reports that on 24 December 1868 the Protestant Churchman printed "A Christmas Story. Founded on Fact", signed "S.H.M.", and its image of the story shows eight lines of the carol in quotation marks, credited to no one [^hymnarchive].

Hymnology Archive reproduces both verses from The Congregationalist of 4 January 1882 [^hymnarchive]. On 2 March 1882 The Christian Cynosure, a Chicago weekly, printed them in its children's corner as "Luther's Cradle Song", with a note that Luther wrote the hymn for his children and German mothers still sang it [^cynosure1882]. In 1883 a New York songbook for primary classes, Little Pilgrim Songs, put it into a Christmas service as a recitation "by a very little child" or a song to the air of "Home, Sweet Home", with the note that Luther wrote it "for his own children" [^pilgrim1883].

![[1]]

The earliest known printing with music is in Little Children's Book for Schools and Families, an Evangelical Lutheran book published in Philadelphia in 1885 [^lcb1885][^hill]. It stands among the nursery hymns, set to ST. KILDA by J. E. Clark, with no author named and no word of Luther [^lcb1885][^hymnarchive].

The third verse, "Be near me, Lord Jesus", has its earliest known printing in Gabriel's Vineyard Songs, compiled by Charles H. Gabriel and published in Louisville in 1892. Gabriel credited the hymn to Luther and gave the new verse no author of its own [^gabriel1892][^hymnarchive][^canterbury]. In 1937 Robert G. McCutchan's Our Hymnody printed Bishop William F. Anderson's account that between 1904 and 1908 he asked John T. McFarland for a third verse for a Children's Day programme, and McFarland wrote it [^hymnarchive][^hill]. Hill pointed out that the verse was in print in 1892, so the story cannot stand as told [^hill][^hymnarchive]. Who wrote it is not known.

**The tunes.** James R. Murray, born in Andover, Massachusetts, in 1841, worked in Cincinnati for the music publisher John Church Company [^hill][^phh-tunes]. In 1887 he printed the carol in his Dainty Songs for Little Lads and Lasses as "Luther's Cradle Hymn", with the same note about German mothers, and signed the music only "J. R. M." [^murray1887][^phh-tunes]. Later books credited the tune to an unidentified "Carl Mueller", hence its name MUELLER; Hill found no evidence for him and gave it to Murray [^hill][^hymnarchive].

William J. Kirkpatrick (1838 to 1921) was a Philadelphia church musician, music director at Grace Methodist Episcopal Church, and a compiler of gospel songbooks [^hymnary-kirkpatrick][^hymnarchive-kirkpatrick]. In 1895 he and John R. Sweney wrote the music for Around the World with Christmas, a Christmas exercise for Sunday schools [^hill][^canterbury]. There, under "The German Fatherland", is "Luther's Cradle Hymn", with Kirkpatrick's music and all three verses [^kirk1895]. He printed it again in Joy and Praise, a Sunday-school book he edited with J. H. Fillmore in 1908, marked "Solo or Duet": the page our arrangement reads [^jp1908].

![[2]]

Hill found Kirkpatrick's setting the first to carry the words beyond the United States [^hill]. In 1905 the Sunday School Union in London printed it in Carey Bonner's Sunday School Hymnary, for "Children in Unison. Softly. Not too quickly.", as "Translated from Martin Luther" [^bonner1905].

![[3]]

**Myth and record.** Often told: Luther wrote this carol for his children, and German mothers still sing it. What the record shows: the earliest known Luther claim is in American papers of 1882 [^hymnarchive][^cynosure1882]. The carol is in none of Luther's hymn collections, and no German form of it is known from before the English one [^hymnarchive][^hill]. The hymn Luther is said to have written for his children's Christmas Eve is "Vom Himmel hoch, da komm ich her", in an account John Julian quotes [^julian][^hill]. An American book of 1872, Luther at Home, said that "Luther's carol for Christmas, written for his own child Hans, is still sung" in Dresden, without naming it [^stork1872]; the Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology suggests Murray took that carol to be this one [^canterbury]. Hill guessed the poem was first printed for Luther's 400th birthday in 1883, and Hymnology Archive ties the Luther label to that year, but both were in print by early 1882 [^hill][^hymnarchive][^cynosure1882].

## What the words mean

We sing the first, as Kirkpatrick printed it in Joy and Praise [^jp1908].

**Verse 1, the one we sing.** A manger is a trough that farm animals eat from. Luke's Gospel says Mary laid her newborn son in one "because there was no room for them in the inn" [^kjv]. A crib is a baby's bed: he had no proper cot, so he sleeps on the hay. The eight lines of 1868 already end "asleep on the hay" [^hymnarchive]. The printings of 1882 to 1885 have "no crib for His bed", "in the sky" and "asleep in the hay" [^cynosure1882][^lcb1885]. "For a bed" and "on the hay" are in Murray's book of 1887, and "the bright sky" in Kirkpatrick's of 1895 [^murray1887][^kirk1895].

**Verse 2.** "Lowing" is mooing. The baby wakes but does not cry. The singer then speaks to Jesus: "Thee" is the old familiar word for "you", kept in prayer, and "nigh" means near. In 1882 the singer asks him to stay by "my crib", "watching my lullaby"; Murray has "to watch lullaby"; Kirkpatrick wrote "till morning is nigh" [^cynosure1882][^murray1887][^kirk1895].

**Verse 3.** The Psalter Hymnal Handbook calls it a bedtime prayer, after two verses with a lullaby's feel [^hymnary-text]. The singer asks Jesus to stay close, bless all children and take them to heaven. Gabriel in 1892 and Kirkpatrick in 1908 say "take us to heaven"; the London book of 1905 says "fit us for heaven", make us ready for it [^gabriel1892][^jp1908][^bonner1905].

### The full words

These are the three verses as Kirkpatrick printed them in Joy and Praise in 1908, No. 174, the page our arrangement reads, the dash after "lay" written as a comma [^jp1908]. The first two were in print by 1882; the third was added later, with its earliest known printing in 1892, and nobody knows who wrote it [^cynosure1882][^gabriel1892][^hymnarchive].

**Verse 1**
Away in a manger, no crib for a bed,
The little Lord Jesus laid down His sweet head.
The stars in the bright sky looked down where He lay,
The little Lord Jesus asleep on the hay.

**Verse 2**
The cattle are lowing, the baby awakes,
But little Lord Jesus no crying He makes.
I love Thee, Lord Jesus! look down from the sky,
And stay by my cradle till morning is nigh.

**Verse 3**
Be near me, Lord Jesus, I ask Thee to stay
Close by me forever, and love me, I pray.
Bless all the dear children in Thy tender care,
And take us to heaven to live with Thee there.

## How it is sung

From the start it was a carol for small children: a recitation in 1883, a nursery hymn in 1885, a song for the primary school in 1895 [^pilgrim1883][^lcb1885][^kirk1895]. The Psalter Hymnal Handbook suggests Christmas Eve [^hymnary-text]. Its meter is 11.11.11.11 [^lcb1885][^bonner1905].

Kirkpatrick's tune is CRADLE SONG and Murray's is MUELLER, though books also call either one AWAY IN A MANGER [^phh-tunes][^canterbury][^iph2004]. The Anglican Church of Canada's Common Praise (1998) and The Irish Presbyterian Hymnbook (2004) print Kirkpatrick's [^cp1998][^iph2004]. American books vary: The United Methodist Hymnal (1989) uses Murray's, the Episcopal Hymnal 1982 uses Kirkpatrick's, and the Christian Reformed Church's Psalter Hymnal prints both [^umh1989][^eh1982][^phh-tunes].

Our arrangement reads Kirkpatrick's page in Joy and Praise, No. 174, printed in F and sung here a whole tone higher, in G [^jp1908]. The soprano sings his tune and the alto his second voice, with eleven notes changed so it moves by step. The bass sings the lowest note of his accompaniment, with two notes changed, and the tenor part is ours, on his harmony. We sing the first verse for 2 to 4 singers, slow and gentle.

## Versions and other words

The earliest printings suggested "Home, Sweet Home" as a tune [^pilgrim1883][^hymnarchive]. Then came ST. KILDA in 1885, Murray's in 1887, J. B. Herbert's in 1891, which by the late 1930s rivalled Murray's, one in 6/8 in Gabriel's book of 1892 and Kirkpatrick's in 1895 [^lcb1885][^murray1887][^hill][^gabriel1892][^kirk1895]. The words were also often fitted to an older tune: J. E. Spilman's "Flow Gently, Sweet Afton", first published in 1838, which Hill found printed with them more often than any tune but Murray's [^hill]; Charles L. Hutchins included it in Carols Old and Carols New (Boston, 1916) [^hutchins1916].

## Did you know?

- Kirkpatrick's own songbook of 1908 still credits the words to Martin Luther [^jp1908].
- Sources disagree on where Kirkpatrick was born. George C. Stebbins's account of 1924, quoted by Hymnology Archive, says Ireland; Hymnary.org's biography says Duncannon, Pennsylvania [^hymnarchive-kirkpatrick][^hymnary-kirkpatrick].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/away-in-a-manger/soprano): you carry Kirkpatrick's tune, a gentle lullaby in three time. The first and third lines are the same tune, each climbing to your top E, on "a" in "no crib for a bed" and on "He" in "looked down where He lay".
- [Alto](/songs/away-in-a-manger/alto): you sing Kirkpatrick's second voice, mostly a sixth or a third under the tune, mostly on B, D and C. Two moments need care: the C-sharp on "down His sweet" that falls to C on "head", and the F-natural on "down where" before G on "He". Solo them in the player.
- [Tenor](/songs/away-in-a-manger/tenor): you fill in the middle of the chord, mostly on G. The whole first line sits on G until "bed", where you step down to F-sharp. Your top note, a C, comes on "lay" at the end of the third line and stays for "The little" that starts the last.
- [Bass](/songs/away-in-a-manger/bass): you sing the lowest notes of Kirkpatrick's accompaniment, mostly on G and D. Your highest note, an E, is on "a" in "no crib for a bed", under the tune's top E. Your lowest, a low F-sharp, is on "Lord" in the last line.

## Questions

### Did Martin Luther write Away in a Manger?

No. The words are anonymous and American, and no German form of the carol is known from before the English [^hymnarchive][^hill]. By 1882 American papers were calling it "Luther's Cradle Song", with a note that he wrote it for his children [^cynosure1882][^hymnarchive]. In 1945 Richard S. Hill showed that Luther had nothing to do with its words or its tunes [^hill]. The Christmas hymn Luther is said to have written for his children is "Vom Himmel hoch" [^julian][^hill].

### Who wrote the music for Away in a Manger?

Two musicians working in the United States wrote the tunes most sung today. James R. Murray published his, now called MUELLER, in 1887; Hill found it the most printed of all, and the Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology calls it the American tune [^murray1887][^hill][^canterbury]. William J. Kirkpatrick published his, CRADLE SONG, in 1895; Canterbury says Britain always sings it, and we sing it too [^kirk1895][^canterbury].

### What Bible verse is Away in a Manger based on?

Luke 2:7, where Mary lays her firstborn son in a manger "because there was no room for them in the inn" [^kjv]. The angel tells the shepherds they will find the baby "lying in a manger" (Luke 2:12) [^kjv]. The lowing cattle and the baby who does not cry are the carol's own picture, not the Gospel's.

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings Kirkpatrick's tune, from a low D up to the E a ninth above. With two singers you have the tune and the alto, which is Kirkpatrick's own second voice, as he printed it "Solo or Duet" [^jp1908]. With three, add the bass; the tenor makes four.
