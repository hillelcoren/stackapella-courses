Shoshanat Yaakov is a short Hebrew song for Purim. Once the Book of Esther has been read aloud from its scroll and the blessing after it said, these lines follow.[^baer][^kitzur] They are the last lines of an old poem, Asher heni, with one more line from an old custom, and they gather the whole story into a few words: the Jews rejoiced when they saw Mordecai in royal blue, Hashem was their rescue then and is their hope in every generation, and Harbona, the king's servant who pointed out Haman's gallows, is remembered for good.[^vitry][^yer][^je-harbona] Its first line borrows its words of joy from the Book of Esther itself.[^esther][^vitry] Our tune is the one Abraham Baer, cantor of the Gothenburg synagogue in Sweden, printed in his handbook for cantors.[^baer][^hammarlund]

## Where it comes from

**The story behind it.** The Book of Esther tells of the Jews of the Persian empire under King Ahasuerus. Haman, the king's chief minister, was enraged that Mordecai the Jew would not bow to him, and he plotted to destroy every Jew in the kingdom on a day chosen by lot, pur.[^esther][^je-purim] Esther, the cousin Mordecai had raised as his daughter, had become queen without making her people known. She went to the king unbidden, at the risk of her life, and at a banquet named Haman as the enemy who had sold her people to be destroyed.[^esther] Harbona, one of the king's chamberlains, then told the king of the gallows Haman had built for Mordecai, and Haman was hanged on it.[^esther][^je-harbona] A new decree let the Jews stand and defend their lives, and Mordecai went out from the king in royal robes of blue and white while the city of Shushan cried out and was glad.[^esther] Purim, named for the lots, is kept on the fourteenth of Adar, and in Shushan and the old walled cities on the fifteenth, with the reading of the book, feasting, and gifts to friends and to the poor.[^esther][^je-purim]

**The words.** The song's last line is not part of the poem but a custom the Talmud records. The Jerusalem Talmud and the minor tractate Soferim say that after the reading Rav required the words "Cursed be Haman, cursed be his sons", and that Rabbi Pinchas required "Harbona, remembered for good".[^yer][^soferim] Soferim also blesses Mordecai, Esther and all Israel.[^soferim]

The poem itself, Asher heni, is a poem of praise said after the blessing that follows the reading.[^sa][^baer] Its lines run through the Hebrew alphabet: "Shoshanat" stands at shin and "Teshuatam" at tav, the last two letters.[^vitry] It is in the Mahzor Vitry, the prayer book of the school of Rashi compiled in northern France by Simhah of Vitry, who died by 1105, though its manuscripts carry later additions too.[^ej-vitry][^je-simhah]

**Myth and record.** Often told: the poem was made by the Men of the Great Assembly, the sages whom tradition places at the start of the Second Temple period and the Talmud credits with fixing the days of Purim.[^je-great][^meg] What the record shows: that is the Mahzor Vitry's heading for it, "a poem from the foundation of the Men of the Great Assembly", a traditional attribution; the book names no poet.[^vitry][^je-great]

**The tune.** Abraham Baer was born in 1834 and in 1857 became cantor of the synagogue in Gothenburg, Sweden, where he served until his death in 1894.[^je-baer][^hammarlund] His Baal T'fillah, or Der practische Vorbeter, "the practical prayer leader", gathers about 1,500 tunes for the cantor, German, Polish and Sephardi.[^je-baer][^hammarlund] Baer dated his first preface in Gothenburg in September 1877, and in his second he says he gave this work of fifteen years to the public in 1877, the year Hammarlund gives too; the Jewish Encyclopedia gives 1871. A second, enlarged edition followed in 1883.[^baer][^hammarlund][^je-baer] Our song is No. 197 on page 59 of the third edition, printed in Frankfurt am Main after 1883.[^baer]

Baer names no maker for it. His first preface, of 1877, explains his marks: "N. W.", new tune; "A. W.", old tune, for the work of single cantors of earlier times that is known only locally; and no mark at all for the very old tunes, known by their typical character and how widely they have spread.[^baer] The Esther verses and the blessing printed above it on page 59 carry "A. W.", the older cantors' mark; this song carries none, so by his own key it is a very old, widely sung tune.[^baer] His second preface, of 1883, says that all the Polish melodies, some of the Ashkenazi ones marked "A. W.", and all the pieces marked "N. W." are his own compositions. It also lists by number the pieces he wrote in the old style, ending "and other similar passages"; No. 197 is not among them.[^baer] The marks are a cantor's judgment, not a record: who made the tune, and when, is not known.

## What the words mean

We sing the closing lines of Asher heni, then the line about Harbona.[^vitry][^baer] The Mahzor Vitry's copy ends a little differently, with "those who trust in You" and a last line calling Hashem the shield of the righteous, and has no line about Harbona.[^vitry]

**"Shoshanat Yaakov tzahala vesamecha."** The rose of Jacob cried out with joy and was glad. Shoshana is a lily or a rose; Simeon Singer's English prayer book made it "the lily of Jacob".[^singer] Jacob is the people of Israel, named for the patriarch. "Tzahala vesamecha" are the words the Book of Esther uses for the city of Shushan when Mordecai came out in royal robes.[^esther]

**"Birotam yachad techelet Mordechai."** When together they saw Mordecai's blue: techelet is the blue of the royal robe in the same verse.[^esther]

**"Teshuatam hayita lanetzach, v'tikvatam bechol dor vador."** You were their salvation forever, and their hope in every generation.

**"L'hodia shekol kovecha lo yevoshu, velo yikalmu lanetzach kol hachosim bach."** To make known that all who hope in You will not be put to shame, and all who take shelter in You will never be disgraced.

**"Vegam Charvona zachur latov."** And Harbona, too, is remembered for good. Zachur latov is the phrase Rabbi Pinchas asked for.[^yer][^soferim]

These lines need none of the substituted names customary when singing outside prayer: they speak to Hashem only as "You", in "kovecha" and "bach".

### The full words

These are the poem's last lines and the lines that follow them, as the Siddur Sefard on Sefaria prints them, in the public-domain Hebrew of Torat Emet; the meanings are our own.[^sefard] Singer's English has no line on all the wicked and all the righteous.[^singer]

> שׁוֹשַׁנַּת יַעֲקֹב צָהֲלָה וְשָׂמֵחָה
*Shoshanat Yaakov tzahala vesamecha*
The rose of Jacob cried out with joy and was glad
> בִּרְאוֹתָם יַחַד תְּכֵלֶת מָרְדְּכָי:
*birotam yachad techelet Mordechai.*
when together they saw Mordecai's blue.
> תְּשׁוּעָתָם הָיִיתָ לָנֶצַח.
*Teshuatam hayita lanetzach,*
You were their salvation forever,
> וְתִקְוָתָם בְּכָל דּוֹר וָדוֹר:
*v'tikvatam bechol dor vador,*
and their hope in every generation,
> לְהוֹדִיעַ שֶׁכָּל קֹוֶיךָ לֹא יֵבֹשׁוּ.
*l'hodia shekol kovecha lo yevoshu,*
to make known that all who hope in You will not be put to shame,
> וְלֹא יִכָּלְמוּ לָנֶצַח כָּל הַחוֹסִים בָּךְ:
*velo yikalmu lanetzach kol hachosim bach.*
and all who take shelter in You will never be disgraced.

> אָרוּר הָמָן אֲשֶׁר בִּקֵשׁ לְאַבְּדִי.
*Arur Haman asher bikesh l'abdi,*
Cursed be Haman, who sought to destroy me;
> בָּרוּךְ מָרְדְּכַי הַיְּהוּדִי.
*baruch Mordechai hayehudi.*
blessed be Mordecai the Jew.
> אֲרוּרָה זֶרֶשׁ אֵשֶׁת מַפְחִידִי.
*Arura Zeresh eshet mafchidi,*
Cursed be Zeresh, the wife of the one who terrified me;
> בְּרוּכָה אֶסְתֵּר בַּעֲדִי.
*brucha Ester baadi.*
blessed be Esther, who pleaded for me.
> אֲרוּרִים כָּל הָרְשָׁעִים.
*Arurim kol hareshaim,*
Cursed be all the wicked;
> בְּרוּכִים כָּל הַצַּדִּיקִים.
*bruchim kol hatzadikim.*
blessed be all the righteous.
> וְגַם חַרְבוֹנָה זָכוּר לַטּוֹב:
*Vegam Charvona zachur latov.*
And Harbona, too, is remembered for good.

## How it is sung

The Talmud says the scroll of Esther is read at night and again by day, and it gives the blessing said after the reading, in places where that is the custom.[^meg][^je-purim][^kitzur] In the Ashkenazi rite the whole of Asher heni follows the blessing at night. Moses Isserles's gloss on the Shulchan Aruch notes the custom of saying it at night but not by day, and Singer's prayer book leaves the paragraph out after the morning reading.[^sa][^singer] Shlomo Ganzfried's Kitzur Shulchan Aruch has only Shoshanat Yaakov said after the morning reading.[^kitzur]

Baer's page sets the scene: the scroll is rolled up, the blessing is sung, Asher heni is said quietly (skipped in the morning, his note adds), and then comes this song, for the cantor alone, Andantino, in B-flat major.[^baer] Above the blessing he prints the four verses of Esther that the congregation sings aloud during the reading, each marked "Chor, dann Solo": the congregation, then the cantor.[^baer][^je-purim] For a moment, on "lo yikalmu lanetzach, kol hachosim bach", the print splits into two voices, the second a third above the tune. The song ends with a double bar and a quiet last line, marked "Leise weiter", go on softly: "W'gam charwonoh sochur latow".[^baer]

Our arrangement is read from that page, in B-flat as printed, for 2 to 4 singers, with the words in modern Israeli pronunciation. The soprano sings Baer's tune, taking the lower of his two voices, and the alto, bass and tenor are our own. The whole song is sung twice, then the soft last line once.[^baer]

## Versions and other words

**The whole poem.** Asher heni retells the story along the alphabet: Haman's pride and plot, Mordecai's mourning, Esther's plea and Haman's fall, before it reaches the rose of Jacob.[^vitry][^singer]

**The curses and blessings.** The Shulchan Aruch gives the fuller form: cursed be Haman and blessed be Mordecai, cursed be Zeresh, Haman's wife, and blessed be Esther, and Harbona remembered for good.[^sa][^soferim] The Mahzor Vitry stretches the pattern into a whole alphabet, which it says rests on tractate Soferim.[^vitry]

## Did you know?

- The four verses read aloud during the reading are Esther 2:5, 8:15, 8:16 and 10:3, and 8:15 is the verse this song borrows its blue robe and glad city from. [^je-purim][^ej-purim][^esther]
- The rabbis did not agree about Harbona. The Talmud has Rabbi Eleazar say he had been part of Haman's plot and changed sides when it failed; Esther Rabbah says it was the prophet Elijah who appeared in Harbona's likeness. [^meg][^er][^je-harbona]

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/shoshanat-yaakov/soprano): the tune, most often on F, then G. The line beginning "l'hodia" opens with its biggest leap, a sixth from F up to D, and climbs to the top E-flat on "lo" and "yevoshu". It also dips below the usual soprano range, to low B-flat on "Yaakov" and "Teshuatam", just as Baer printed it.
- [Alto](/songs/shoshanat-yaakov/alto): mostly a third or a sixth under the tune, most often on D, then E-flat, up to its top B-flat only on "velo". In the soft last line it holds D through "Vegam Charvona" while the tune moves above it, meeting the tune on "char-".
- [Tenor](/songs/shoshanat-yaakov/tenor): the middle of the chord, most often on B-flat, then G, up to its top E-flat on "yevoshu", "velo" and "yikalmu". The first time through it stays on its low D from "Shoshanat" to the start of "vesamecha"; the second time it starts on F and moves. Solo it in the player to hear the change.
- [Bass](/songs/shoshanat-yaakov/bass): the roots of the chords nearly all the time, most often on B-flat, then C. It reaches its top B-flat only on "velo" and drops to its low F on "bechol", "vador" and "hachosim". In the last line it rocks between F and B-flat on "zachur latov".

## Questions

### What does "Shoshanat Yaakov" mean?

"The rose of Jacob", or "the lily of Jacob": the Jewish people, named for the patriarch Jacob. The line says the rose of Jacob cried out and was glad on seeing Mordecai in royal blue, words from Esther 8:15, where the city of Shushan rejoices as he goes out from the king in royal robes.[^esther][^singer]

### Is it Shoshanat Yaakov or Shoshanas Yaakov?

Both: they are the same Hebrew words said two ways. In Ashkenazi Hebrew the final letter, tav, is said "s", so Shoshanas Yaakov, and Baer printed it as "Schoschannas jaakow". Modern Israeli Hebrew says "t", so Shoshanat Yaakov. We sing the modern Israeli form, syllable for syllable as Baer's notes carry the words.[^baer]

### Who wrote Shoshanat Yaakov?

No one knows. Its lines close Asher heni, an alphabet poem in the Mahzor Vitry, the prayer book of Rashi's school compiled by Simhah of Vitry, who had died by 1105, though its manuscripts carry later additions too. The book heads the poem "a poem from the foundation of the Men of the Great Assembly", a traditional attribution, and names no poet. The last line, for Harbona, comes from a custom the Talmud records, and who made Baer's tune is not known either.[^vitry][^ej-vitry][^je-simhah][^je-great][^yer][^soferim][^baer]

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune throughout, as Baer printed it for the cantor; where his print splits into two voices for a moment, it takes the lower one. With two singers it is soprano and alto, with three the bass joins, and with four the tenor fills the middle of each chord.[^baer]
