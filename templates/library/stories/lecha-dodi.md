Lecha Dodi (also spelled Lekha Dodi or L'cha Dodi), "Come, my beloved", is the Hebrew hymn that welcomes the Sabbath on Friday evening. It belongs to Kabbalat Shabbat, "receiving the Sabbath", the short service sung as the Sabbath comes in, and as its last verse begins many in the congregation turn around and bow.[^ej][^abrahams] Solomon ha-Levi Alkabetz wrote it in Safed in the sixteenth century, and it was one of the last Hebrew poems to become a fixed part of the prayer book.[^je][^ej] Since then it has been sung in Jewish communities all over the world.[^schechter][^ejalk] In 1929 A. Z. Idelsohn wrote that it had received about two thousand musical settings.[^idel29][^ej] Ours is an old Ashkenazi one that was kept for the weeks between Passover and Shavuot.[^je][^idel29]

## Where it comes from

The idea is older than the poem. The Talmud tells of two rabbis who dressed for the Sabbath on Friday evening to greet it. Rabbi Hanina would stand at nightfall and say, come, let us go out to greet the Sabbath queen; Rabbi Yannai would say, "Come, O Bride; Come, O Bride".[^shabbat][^abrahams] Israel Abrahams noted in 1922 that the picture of the Sabbath as a bride was much older than Alkabetz, and that the refrain was not original to him either.[^abrahams]

In sixteenth-century Safed the greeting became a ritual. The town was then the gathering place of Jewish mystics, the kabbalists.[^jealk][^schechter] The Encyclopaedia Judaica tells how they walked out into the fields on Friday afternoons, meditating and singing, to meet the Sabbath as a queen.[^ej] Idelsohn retells a story about Isaac Luria, who later led the Safed mystics: one Sabbath eve he went out of the city with his disciples to receive the Sabbath, singing.[^idel29] The psalms said before Lecha Dodi were added by the same Safed mystics.[^abrahams][^idel29]

![[1]]

Alkabetz was born about 1505.[^ejalk][^idel29][^huji] Before he settled in Safed he lived in the Ottoman cities of Salonica and Adrianople.[^jealk][^ejalk][^idel29] He was a kabbalist and poet, the teacher and brother-in-law of the mystic Moses Cordovero, and a contemporary and friend of Joseph Caro, the author of the law code the Shulchan Aruch.[^jealk][^ejalk][^schechter] The sources differ on his death. The Jewish Encyclopedia of 1901 gives no year: he was still living in 1561, it says, and a book finished in 1575 still spoke of him as alive. Idelsohn gives 1580, and the Encyclopaedia Judaica and the Hebrew University's Jewish Music Research Centre give 1584.[^jealk][^idel29][^ejalk][^huji]

The poem has a refrain and nine verses. The first letters of the first eight spell his name in Hebrew, Shlomo HaLevi.[^je][^ej][^ashkenaz] Its words lean on the Bible, above all on Isaiah's prophecies that Israel will be restored.[^je][^abrahams] The scholar Solomon Schechter called it "perhaps one of the finest pieces of religious poetry in existence".[^abrahams][^schechter] Its earliest known printing is in a Sephardi prayer book printed in Venice in 1583/4.[^ej][^posen] That printing names in its heading the tune to sing it to, that of Judah Halevi's poem "Shuvi nafshi limenuchayechi"; Halevi had died about four centuries before Alkabetz.[^ej][^je][^jehalevi] The Jewish Encyclopedia took the heading as a sign that the Sephardi melody is older than the poem.[^je] No source from Alkabetz's own time says what tune it was first sung to.[^ej]

![[2]]

**The tune.** At certain seasons, the Jewish Encyclopedia wrote in 1904, many northern congregations set aside their newer settings of Lecha Dodi for two simpler old melodies. The better known was kept for the weeks of the Omer, between Passover and Shavuot.[^je] Idelsohn calls it the "Sefira Lechoh-dodi": sefira is the counting of those seven weeks, which the Bible commands.[^idel29][^lev] Francis L. Cohen printed the tune in that 1904 article, with English words. Cohen (1862 to 1934) was an English rabbi and the Jewish Encyclopedia's music editor, and later chief minister of the Great Synagogue in Sydney.[^je][^jecohen][^ejcohen][^apple]

**Myth and record.** Often told: the tune comes from Mozart's opera *The Marriage of Figaro*. What the record shows: the Jewish Encyclopedia reports that some had heard in its phrases the English political song "Lilliburlero" and the cavatina near the start of *Figaro*, but it heard German folk song of the late seventeenth century all through the melody.[^je] Idelsohn agreed that it resembles that aria, No. 3 in *Figaro*, and found it also very like a German folk song of Paderborn, dated 1765. He thought an older German folk song lay behind both Mozart's aria and the cantors' tune, and that the cantors could not have taken it from Mozart, because manuscripts show the Lecha Dodi tune was well known long before the opera.[^idel29]

## What the words mean

The page shows the words we sing, in modern Israeli pronunciation. Idelsohn, in 1929, spelled the title the Ashkenazi way, Lechoh dodi.[^idel29] Of the nine verses we sing the first and the last. The verses between turn from the Sabbath to Jerusalem: the city is told to rise from its ruins, shake off the dust and wake, in the words of Isaiah, and the hope for redemption grows verse by verse.[^je][^abrahams][^ashkenaz]

We sing it with the substituted names customary when singing outside prayer, Kel and Adoshem: it is the custom not to say Hashem's name in full outside prayer, and a practice song is outside prayer.

**The refrain.** Come, my beloved, to meet the bride; let us welcome the Sabbath's presence. "Lecha dodi" is a phrase from the Song of Songs, "Come, my beloved, let us go forth into the field".[^songofsongs] Kala is "bride". "Penei Shabat" is, word for word, the face of the Sabbath.

**Verse 1.** "Keep" and "Remember" were said in one utterance; Hashem, the One, made us hear them. Hashem is one and His name is one, for renown, for glory and for praise. The Sabbath commandment begins "Remember" in Exodus and "Observe" in Deuteronomy; the Talmud teaches that both were spoken in a single utterance.[^shevuot][^abrahams] "Hashem is one and His name is one" comes from Zechariah, and the last line echoes Deuteronomy's "in praise, and in name, and in glory".[^zech][^deut]

**Verse 9.** Come in peace, crown of her husband, with song and gladness, among the faithful of the treasured people. Come, O bride; come, O bride! "Ateret ba'la" echoes Proverbs: a good wife is the crown of her husband.[^prov] Segula, "treasure", is Hashem's word for Israel in Exodus: "ye shall be Mine own treasure from among all peoples".[^exod] "Bo'i chala" is Rabbi Yannai's greeting from the Talmud; chala is kala, "bride", its first sound softened after the vowel before it.[^shabbat] Ashkenazi prayer books have besimcha, "with joy"; the prayer books of Nusach Sefard, the Hasidic rite, have berina, "with song", and add besimcha on a festival.[^ashkenaz][^singer][^sefard] We sing berina, the Nusach Sefard Sabbath word.

### The full words

The refrain and nine verses as the Siddur Ashkenaz on Sefaria prints them, from the Metsudah siddur of 1981; the meanings are our own.[^ashkenaz] The refrain returns after every verse. The last verse has besimcha where we sing berina, and calls the bride a third time, Shabat malketa, "Sabbath queen". Singer's London prayer book of 1904 calls her only twice, as we sing it; Abrahams noted that this ending differs between prayer books.[^singer][^abrahams] The transliteration gives Hashem's names in their substituted forms, Kel, Adoshem and Elokayich.

*penei Shabat nekabela.*
let us welcome the Sabbath's presence.

*Hitnaari meafar kumi,*
Shake off the dust, rise,

*livshi bigdei tifartech ami.*

*venivneta ir al tilah.*
and the city will be rebuilt on its hill.

*Vehayu limshisa shosayich,*

*venismecha venagila.*
we will be glad and rejoice.

Come in peace, crown of her husband,

*gam besimcha uvetzahala,*

> תּוֹךְ אֱמוּנֵי עַם סְגֻלָּה.
*toch emunei am segula.*
among the faithful of the treasured people.

*Boi chala, boi chala, boi chala Shabat malketa!*
Come, O bride; come, O bride; come, O bride, Sabbath queen!

## How it is sung

In the Ashkenazi rite of Friday evening, six psalms come first, 95 to 99 and 29; Lecha Dodi follows them, and then Psalm 92, "A Psalm, a Song for the Sabbath Day".[^abrahams][^idel29] As the last verse begins, it is the custom to turn around to face the entrance of the synagogue and bow.[^ej] Abrahams saw in this turning a survival of the old procession out to receive the Sabbath.[^abrahams]

In Ashkenazi synagogues of 1904, the Jewish Encyclopedia wrote, the cantor sang the verses at length and the congregation answered each with the refrain. Sephardi congregations all sang it together to an old Moorish melody, using the refrain only as an introduction.[^je]

Our arrangement is read from Cohen's 1904 printing, "Lekah Dodi (Melody for 'Omer Weeks)": a melody alone, in G major and 3/4 time, as we sing it. It carries English words, a rendering of the hymn's second stanza ("How lovely is the Day of Rest") and a refrain ("Then come, my friend, to meet the Bride").[^jescan][^je] The Hebrew is fitted to the tune by us, and the four parts are our own. We sing the refrain, as the hymn begins, then verse 1, the refrain, verse 9 and the refrain to close.

## Versions and other words

**Other tunes.** The Jewish Encyclopedia prints two more beside ours: the old Sephardi melody, and the tune northern congregations kept for the Three Weeks before the fast of the Ninth of Av. It adds that the composer Benedetto Marcello used a form of that Three Weeks tune in his setting of Psalm 19, where it is called an air of the German Jews.[^je] The Jewish Encyclopedia reports that Birnbaum found its source in a Polish folk song, and that by 1904 the tune of the lament Eli Ziyyon more often took its place; the Encyclopaedia Judaica gives the Three Weeks melody as based on Eli Ziyyon.[^je][^ej]

## Did you know?

- Alkabetz wrote that one Shavuot night, as he and Joseph Caro sat up studying Torah, a heavenly messenger, the maggid, appeared to Caro. The Jewish Encyclopedia of 1901 counted the story among the legends that grew up around him. [^ejalk][^jealk][^schechter]
- Because the welcoming of the Sabbath begins before the Sabbath itself, instruments could play for Lecha Dodi. In Prague, in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, an organ and musicians accompanied it on Friday evenings. [^ej][^idel29]
- The German poets Herder and Heine both translated Lecha Dodi into German. [^schechter][^je]

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/lecha-dodi/soprano): the tune. The refrain starts high, reaching its top E on every "dodi", then drops to its low D on "penei" before climbing again. Verse 1 starts on that low D on "Shamor", then holds G for six syllables in a row, rises to B on "Kel" in its second line, and climbs to D only at its very end, on "velithila.".
- [Alto](/songs/lecha-dodi/alto): under the tune, mostly a third or a sixth below it, most often on E. It opens every refrain on its top B on "Lecha", and climbs back to that B on the last word of each verse, "velithila." and "chala!".
- [Tenor](/songs/lecha-dodi/tenor): the middle of the chord, most often on G, then C. It starts each refrain on its top D on "Lecha", and only on the very last "nekabela." does it drop to its low D, an octave down.
- [Bass](/songs/lecha-dodi/bass): the bass line, most often on C, then D. It comes home to its low G at the end of every line of both verses, and on every "Lecha", "likrat", "Shabat" and "nekabela.".

## Questions

### What does Lecha Dodi mean?

"Come, my beloved", the opening words of the refrain, which go on: to meet the bride, let us welcome the Sabbath. The words come from the Song of Songs, and the bride is the Sabbath, as in the Talmud, where Rabbi Yannai greeted the Sabbath with "Come, O Bride".[^songofsongs][^shabbat][^abrahams]

### Who wrote Lecha Dodi?

Solomon ha-Levi Alkabetz, a poet and kabbalist of Safed, in the sixteenth century. He signed it: the first letters of the first eight verses spell Shlomo HaLevi. He was born about 1505; the sources differ on his death: Idelsohn gives 1580, the Encyclopaedia Judaica 1584.[^je][^ej][^ejalk][^idel29]

### Who is the "beloved" in Lecha Dodi?

Readings differ. Dodi is "my beloved", the word the Song of Songs uses in "Come, my beloved, let us go forth into the field". In the reading of the kabbalists, the mystics of Alkabetz's Safed, the beloved is Hashem, called to go out and meet the bride: the Sabbath as the Shekhinah, His presence. Francis L. Cohen's English of 1904 sings it more simply: "Then come, my friend, to meet the Bride".[^songofsongs][^jealk][^schechter][^schwartz][^miller][^je]

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano, throughout: the Omer-weeks melody Francis L. Cohen printed in 1904. With two singers it is soprano and alto, the alto mostly a third or sixth below; with three the bass joins, and with four the tenor fills the middle of each chord.[^je]
