With Cat-like Tread, whose second tune begins Come, Friends, Who Plough the Sea, is the pirates' chorus from Act II of The Pirates of Penzance, the comic opera by W. S. Gilbert (words) and Arthur Sullivan (music) premiered in New York on 31 December 1879 [^hitchcock-lib][^nyt1880][^doyly]. Pirates creep into a moonlit chapel to rob a house, claiming a fly's footstep would be heard, while the hidden police answer, "Tarantara!" [^chappell-score][^hitchcock-lib]. The joke is printed in the libretto itself: beside the pirates' words about silence, Gilbert's stage direction reads "Very loud" [^hitchcock-lib][^stoddart-lib]. In 1917 Leo Feist published its "Come, friends" tune as the chorus of Hail! Hail! The Gang's All Here [^feist1917][^ballad-index].

## Where it comes from

**A premiere in New York.** W. S. Gilbert (1836 to 1911) wrote the words and Arthur Sullivan (1842 to 1900) the music [^loc-gilbert][^londonremembers][^loc-sullivan][^gresham]. Their previous opera, H.M.S. Pinafore, had been staged all over America in versions they had not authorized [^gs-intro][^doyly], so they opened the next one in New York themselves. The Pirates of Penzance had its premiere at the Fifth Avenue Theatre on Wednesday, 31 December 1879 [^hitchcock-lib][^nyt1880][^era-ny]. The afternoon before, one of D'Oyly Carte's touring companies gave a single performance at the Royal Bijou Theatre in Paignton, Devon, to secure the opera's British copyright [^gs-casts][^glasgow-paignton][^doyly]. Reports written that week date it to Tuesday, 30 December: the Glasgow Herald's London correspondent, writing on the Wednesday, says "yesterday afternoon", and The Era's New York correspondent calls it "the day previous" [^glasgow-paignton][^era-ny]. The Morning Post, reviewing the London opening in April, put it in "some obscure west country village" on the same day as New York [^morningpost]. London first saw the opera at the Opera Comique on 3 April 1880, with Sullivan conducting [^glasgow-london][^era-london].

**Authorized editions.** The American vocal score, published in Philadelphia by J. M. Stoddart and dated 1880, calls itself the "Authorized Copyright Edition" [^stoddart-score]. Its title page carries a boxed notice signed by both men: Stoddart alone may publish the opera in the United States, no one should put "unauthorized editions" on the market, and the people of both continents, they write, want to agree on "an international copyright" [^stoddart-score].

**The scene.** Act II is set in a ruined chapel by moonlight on Major-General Stanley's estate [^chappell-score][^hitchcock-lib]. The General escaped the pirates by pretending to be an orphan, and the pirates, who never rob an orphan, have learned that he lied [^tribune1880]. They come at night seeking "a penalty fifty-fold, For General Stanley's story" [^chappell-score][^gs-libretto]. The police, sent to arrest them, hear them coming and decide that "our obvious course is now to hide": Chappell's score gives the line to their Sergeant, the early libretti to the whole police chorus [^chappell-score][^hitchcock-lib][^stoddart-lib]. In the first American libretti (Hitchcock's in New York, undated, and Stoddart's, copyright 1880) the police hide in one aisle while the pirates climb in at a ruined window and come down the stage on tiptoe [^hitchcock-lib][^stoddart-lib]. Then the pirates sing, "(Very loud.) With cat-like tread", and the hidden police answer, "(Pianissimo.) Tarantara!" [^hitchcock-lib][^stoddart-lib].

**Sullivan's march.** Sullivan set it as a march, Allegro marziale, in D, with the pirates marked forte and the piano playing three soft chords in most bars and a crashing fortissimo one on the fourth [^stoddart-score][^chappell-score]. Samuel, the pirates' lieutenant, hands out the burglars' tools in a solo; then both choruses join, and the reprise grows from soft to fortissimo [^chappell-score][^stoddart-score][^gs-libretto]. A correspondent at the New York opening saw the joke: the burglars "chorus vociferously" about creeping up unawares [^era-ny].

**The score we read.** Our arrangement is read from a later London printing of Chappell's vocal score, held by the University of Toronto and catalogued as about 1920 [^chappell-score]. Chappell's plate number, 23731, is at the foot of every page, and the title page cites the Copyright Act of 1911 and sends anyone who wants to stage the opera to Rupert D'Oyly Carte at the Savoy Hotel [^chappell-score]. With Cat-like Tread is Act II, No. 12, a "Solo (Samuel and Chorus of Pirates)", as in Stoddart's 1880 score [^chappell-score][^stoddart-score]. At letter E, page 152, pirates and police sing together, fortissimo, and someone has pencilled "Ladies" at the top of page 149, where the second "Come, friends" begins [^chappell-score]. The score spells "piracee" and "burglaree" to rhyme with "sea"; the early libretti print "piracy" and "burglary" [^chappell-score][^hitchcock-lib].

## What the words mean

1. **"With cat-like tread."** To tread is to walk, "with a stately or a cautious step" [^webster-tread]: the pirates move as softly as a cat. "Upon our prey we steal" means they creep up on their victims; "In silence dread" is "in dreadful silence", turned round for the rhyme; and "Our cautious way we feel" means they feel their way in the dark.
2. **"A fly's foot-fall would be distinctly heard."** So quiet, they claim, you could hear a fly land.
3. **"Come, friends, who plough the sea."** To plough the sea is to sail it, cutting a furrow through the water [^webster-plow]. "Truce to navigation" means a rest from sailing, a truce being a pause in a fight [^webster-truce], and "Take another station" means take up a new post. "Let's vary piracee With a little burglaree" means let's try some house-breaking for a change.
4. **"Tarantara."** The police's sung trumpet call; a report of the New York opening has them performing "a very funny trumpet-call upon their clubs" [^era-ny].
5. **Samuel's tools.** A centre-bit is a hand tool for boring round holes; a life preserver here is not a lifebelt but a club, in the Archive glossary's words "a stick or bludgeon loaded with lead, intended for self-defence" [^gs-glossary][^horniman]. Silent matches were made to light without a sound [^gs-glossary]. A dark lantern has a single opening that can be closed to hide the light [^webster-lantern], and a skeleton key has most of its bit filed away so that it opens many locks [^webster-skeleton].

### The full words

These are the words of Act II, No. 12, as printed in Chappell's vocal score, pages 147 to 152, with the score's dashes left out or made commas [^chappell-score]. In the score most of these lines are sung more than once, and the police's "Tarantara" and "ra, ra" run under the pirates; the words are given here once, in the order they first come. Gilbert died in 1911 and the opera was printed in 1880, so all of it is in the public domain.

**Pirates**
With cat-like tread,
Upon our prey we steal;
In silence dread
Our cautious way we feel!
No sound at all,
We never speak a word;
A fly's foot-fall
Would be distinctly heard.

**Police**
Tarantara, tarantara!

**Pirates**
So stealthily the Pirate creeps,
While all the household soundly sleeps.

**Pirates**
Come, friends, who plough the sea,
Truce to navigation,
Take another station;
Let's vary piracee
With a little burglaree!

**Samuel**
Here's your crowbar, and your centre-bit,
Your life preserver, you may want to hit!
Your silent matches, your dark lantern seize!
Take your file and your skeletonic keys!

**Pirates**
With cat-like tread
Upon our prey we steal;
In silence dread
Our cautious way we feel.

## How it is sung

On stage it is sung by the men's chorus of pirates, with Samuel's solo in the middle and the police answering from hiding; on the page before, the police's "Tarantara" is marked "repeat this, and dim till next Chorus" as they slip out of sight [^chappell-score][^gs-libretto]. It leads straight into the next number, where, as a New York critic put it in 1880, both choruses call for silence "at the top of their lungs" while the General wanders in with a candle [^tribune1880].

Our arrangement sings the end of the reprise, from the last "Come, friends" on page 151 to the end of the voices on page 152, which Sullivan wrote in D for the two men's choruses [^chappell-score]. We sing it in A: the soprano and alto are the pirates, the tenor and bass the police. Sullivan's pirates sing "Come, friends" in unison and his police sing "ra" on every beat [^chappell-score]; our alto line under "Come, friends" is new, our police sing every other beat, and from letter E the parts are revoiced so that two or three singers keep each chord's third. Two singers take the pirates, a third adds the bass, a fourth the tenor. Sing Sullivan's dynamics: quiet at "Come, friends", growing through "Let's vary piracee", full voice from "With cat-like tread" [^chappell-score].

## Versions and other words

**Hail! Hail! The Gang's All Here.** In 1917 Leo Feist of New York, with Herman Darewski in London, published Hail! Hail! The Gang's All Here (What the Deuce Do We Care), with words by "D. A. Esrom" and music by "Theodore Morse and Arthur Sullivan" [^feist1917]. Esrom is Morse spelled backwards, a pen name of the songwriting couple Theodore and Theodora Morse [^ballad-index][^feist1917]. The verse is new; the chorus is Sullivan's "Come, friends, who plough the sea" tune, recast as a 6/8 march in G [^feist1917][^ballad-index]. The Traditional Ballad Index gives the song an earlier date, 1908, from James J. Fuld's Book of World-Famous Music [^ballad-index].

**Early libretti and the score.** The early American libretti print lines that neither vocal score sets: before "So stealthily the pirate creeps" the pirates laugh "Ha! ha! Ho! ho!", and after it they imitate snoring, "Gurr! gurr!" [^hitchcock-lib][^stoddart-lib][^stoddart-score][^chappell-score].

## Did you know?

- In the early American libretti and Stoddart's score it is the Pirate King who comes on "laden with burglarious tools and pistols", though it is Samuel who hands them out in song; the Gilbert and Sullivan Archive's text gives the tools to Samuel [^hitchcock-lib][^stoddart-lib][^stoddart-score][^gs-libretto].
- At the New York premiere the Pirate King was called Richard and the Sergeant of Police Edward; the later Chappell score we read calls them only the Pirate King and the Sergeant of Police [^hitchcock-lib][^gs-casts][^stoddart-score][^chappell-score].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/with-cat-like-tread/soprano): you carry the pirates' tune. "Come, friends" falls from C-sharp to a low E on "sea"; "Truce to navigation" and "Take another station" share one bouncing dotted rhythm, so keep the short notes light. "With a little burglaree!" leaps up through the A chord to your top E on "bur-". "With cat-like tread" sits on one B. At the end, "way" and "we" each hold that top E for a full bar before "feel" falls to A.
- [Alto](/songs/with-cat-like-tread/alto): you sing the pirates' words under the tune. You begin on E, and on "Truce", "navigation", "Take" and "station" you drop to your low G-sharp. "With cat-like tread" holds one D. You rise to A on "Upon", "prey", "steal" and "cautious". Under the soprano's long top E you move from C-sharp on "way" to D on "we", the seventh of the E7 chord, and back to C-sharp on "feel".
- [Tenor](/songs/with-cat-like-tread/tenor): you are a policeman, and you and the bass start the song together, on "Ta-ran-ta-" on E. Then you sing "ra" on the strong beats, mostly on E and F-sharp, under the pirates' march. Your only A comes on the long "ra" under the sopranos' top E; hold it a bar, step down to G-sharp, and finish on E.
- [Bass](/songs/with-cat-like-tread/bass): a policeman too, on Sullivan's bass line. After the pickup on E, your "ra"s sit on low A, then B, then A; at the end of "Come, friends" you climb D to D-sharp to lead into E. Twice your "Ta-ran-ta-ra" climbs B, C-sharp, D, E under the pirates. You hold E for two bars under the top E, then drop to low A for the last "ra!".

## Questions

### What does cat-like tread mean?

It means stepping as softly as a cat [^webster-tread]. The pirates claim to creep so quietly that a fly's footfall would be heard [^chappell-score]. That is the joke: Gilbert's libretto marks the chorus "Very loud", and Sullivan set it as a march with crashing chords [^hitchcock-lib][^chappell-score].

### Is Hail, Hail, the Gang's All Here from The Pirates of Penzance?

Its chorus tune is. Hail! Hail! The Gang's All Here, published by Leo Feist in 1917, credits its music to Theodore Morse and Arthur Sullivan: its chorus is Sullivan's "Come, friends, who plough the sea", recast in 6/8 [^feist1917][^ballad-index]. The verse and words are new, credited to "D. A. Esrom", a pen name of Theodore and Theodora Morse [^feist1917][^ballad-index].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano. In Sullivan's score the tune belongs to the pirates, and in our arrangement the soprano and alto are the pirates, so the soprano carries "Come, friends, who plough the sea" and "With cat-like tread" [^chappell-score]. The alto sings the same words underneath, and the tenor and bass, as the police, sing only "Tarantara" and "ra".
