Deck the Halls is the carol with more "fa la la" than words. Its tune is Welsh: a harp air called Nos Galan, New Year's Eve, printed by the harper John Parry in 1781 and by Edward Jones in 1784 [^parry1781][^jones1784][^tta]. In Wales it carried love verses and a "fal lal" chorus [^jones1784][^jones1794]. Its English words, "Deck the hall with boughs of holly", are Thomas Oliphant's, printed in London in 1862 beside Welsh words by the poet Talhaiarn [^thomas1862][^hullah1866]. Oliphant's first verse called for filling the mead-cup and draining the barrel. By 1877 an American school journal printed "Don we now our gay apparel" in its place, and that is the version later songbooks carried [^psj1877][^mccaskey1881][^golden1923].

## Where it comes from

**The tune.** In 1781 John Parry, harper to the Williams Wynn family at Wynnstay, printed a tune called "Nôs Calan" as No. 7 in British Harmony, his collection of "Antient Welsh Airs", in G with three variations [^parry1781][^dwb-parry]. One researcher traces it further back, to an untitled air in Parry's collection of 1761 and a fiddler's manuscript of about 1752 [^handyfinch1]. Three years later Edward Jones, a harper from Llandderfel in Merionethshire, printed it as "Nôs Galan" in Musical and Poetical Relicks of the Welsh Bards, a book dedicated to the Prince of Wales [^jones1784][^dwb-jones]. Nos Galan is New Year's Eve: Jones's second edition, of 1794, gives the English title "New year's Night", and the 1862 printing gives "New Year's Eve" [^jones1794][^thomas1862].

Jones prints it in F, marked Allegro, with variations and one Welsh stanza under the tune [^jones1784]. The stanza is a love verse, beginning with how warm a slender girl's bosom is, and the short answering bars after the lines are marked "Chorus", with "fal lal &c" under them [^jones1784]. Jones explains that such stanzas, penillion, were the people's own poetry, kept by memory, and that a singer could fit one to many tunes, holding the melody while the harp played variations [^jones1784]. His 1794 edition adds English words, a love verse of soft bosoms and groves in blossom, and a "dadlea" in the chorus [^jones1794].

**The words.** In 1862 the harpist John Thomas, known as Pencerdd Gwalia, published a book of Welsh Melodies with Welsh and English Poetry, arranged for voice with harp or piano [^thomas1862][^imslp-thomas][^handyfinch3]. Nos Galan is No. 15 in its second volume, with Welsh words by Talhaiarn, the poet John Jones (1810 to 1869), and English words by Thomas Oliphant [^thomas1862][^hullah1866][^dwb-talhaiarn][^imslp-thomas]. Oliphant, born in Perthshire in 1799, died in London in 1873. He was the long-serving secretary of the Madrigal Society, and he had spent years fitting English words to Italian madrigals [^dnb-oliphant][^grove-oliphant][^grandemusica]. His Nos Galan begins "Deck the hall with boughs of holly", each line answered by a "Fa la la" [^thomas1862].

In 1866 John Hullah reprinted Oliphant's words in The Song Book, under the title "Deck the Hall with Boughs of Holly", tune "New Year's Eve", from Thomas's Welsh Melodies [^hullah1866]. In his preface he wrote that nothing like Thomas's work, Welsh tunes "adorned by the addition of English words", had been attempted before [^hullah1866].

**The American revision.** In December 1877 the Pennsylvania School Journal printed the carol in four parts as a "Welsh Air", with Oliphant's drinking lines gone [^psj1877]. The same page was reprinted in J. P. McCaskey's Franklin Square Song Collection in 1881 [^mccaskey1881]. The same words, lightly changed again, are in later school songbooks such as The Golden Book of Favorite Songs, the 1923 book our arrangement is read from [^golden1923].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Mozart used the tune in a piece for violin and piano. What the record shows: the story seldom names a work, and the one researcher known to have checked, the writer of the Handy Finch history of the carol, heard the tune in none of Mozart's sonatas for violin and piano [^handyfinch1]. Often told, too: the tune is centuries older than any book. Jones's title page says his tunes were preserved "from remote antiquity", and Parry's calls his airs the remains of those sung by the bards [^jones1784][^parry1781]. What the record shows: every written copy so far found is from the eighteenth century [^tta][^handyfinch1]. The tune may be older, but no copy shows it. And often told: Oliphant translated the Welsh. Hullah's book says "translated from Talhaiarn" [^hullah1866]. But Talhaiarn's first lines praise New Year's night by the fire with a cheerful family, while Oliphant's are about holly and Christmas, and the Handy Finch history, comparing the two texts, finds that the English is not a translation [^thomas1862][^handyfinch3].

## What the words mean

Oliphant wrote three verses: one for decorating and drinking, one for the bowl and the harp, one for the turning year [^thomas1862][^hullah1866]. We sing the first, in the words of the 1923 Golden Book [^golden1923]. That book still heads the song "Deck The Hall", Oliphant's singular, but its first line already reads "Deck the halls" [^golden1923][^hullah1866].

**Verse 1, the one we sing.** To "deck" the halls is to decorate them, and "boughs of holly" are cut branches of holly. "'Tis the season to be jolly" means it is the time of year to be merry. "Don we now our gay apparel" means let us put on our bright, festive clothes: "gay" here is the older sense, merry or bright and showy [^dictionary]. It is the American line; Oliphant wrote "Fill the mead-cup, drain the barrel", mead being a drink made from honey [^thomas1862][^psj1877][^dictionary]. To "troll" a song is to sing it out in a full, rolling voice, or to sing it in turn, as in a round [^dictionary]. Oliphant spelled it "Troul" [^hullah1866]. His "ancient Christmas carol" became the "ancient Yule-tide carol" in our book; Yuletide is the Christmas season [^hullah1866][^golden1923][^dictionary].

**Verse 2.** Oliphant had "See the flowing bowl before us"; the American books put "the blazing Yule", the Yule log burning, in its place [^hullah1866][^psj1877][^handyfinch3]. "Strike the harp and join the chorus" recalls the Welsh scene Jones described: a harp, and singers coming in [^jones1784]. Oliphant's singer promises to sing "of beauty's treasure"; the American books made it Christmas treasure, and the Golden Book Yule-tide treasure [^hullah1866][^psj1877][^golden1923].

**Verse 3.** Here the song turns to New Year's Eve: the old year passes, and the lads and lasses hail the new [^hullah1866]. Oliphant's company go "laughing, quaffing", drinking deep; the American books have them sing joyously together instead, heedless of the wind and weather [^hullah1866][^psj1877].

**The "fa la la".** It means nothing; it is a refrain for everyone to sing. The Welsh printings of 1784 and 1794 already have "fal lal" in the chorus bars, and 1794 adds "dadlea" [^jones1784][^jones1794].

## How it is sung

It is sung at Christmas, and its last verse belongs to New Year's Eve, the night its Welsh name means [^hullah1866][^jones1794]. In Jones's Wales it was a harp air for verses: a singer came in over the harp, and the chorus bars answered [^jones1784]. Hullah's 1866 page says "Repeat in Chorus" [^hullah1866].

Our arrangement reads the Golden Book's four parts on page 66, printed in F and sung here a whole tone lower, in E-flat [^golden1923]. We sing the first verse for 2 to 4 singers, bright and quick, everyone on the words. Eleven notes are changed so the duet keeps its thirds, the alto holds a G under the rising "fa la la" and no inner part leaps awkwardly, and breaths are marked for beginners.

## Did you know?

- Jones's 1794 page has a footnote on the title: the Druids, he says, began their festivals on the night before, citing Caesar, and feasting and mirth are still universal in Wales on the eve of the new year [^jones1794].
- Jones dedicated his 1784 book to the Prince of Wales, writing that many of its tunes had "resounded, in the day of festival, through the Halls" of the prince's forerunners [^jones1784].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/deck-the-halls/soprano): you carry the Welsh tune. "Deck the halls with boughs" steps down the scale from B-flat to E-flat, and the "fa la la" that ends the first, second and last lines dips to a low D before it lands. In the rising "fa la la" in the middle you climb to your top E-flat on the last "la"; aim for it.
- [Alto](/songs/deck-the-halls/alto): you sing the words a third under the tune for most of the verse, mostly on E-flat and D. In the rising "fa la la" you hold G while the tune climbs, then follow it down a sixth below. You end the first and last lines on a low G.
- [Tenor](/songs/deck-the-halls/tenor): you hold the middle of the chord, mostly on B-flat. At the end of the first and last lines you walk down A-flat, G, F to a low E-flat. In the middle "fa la la", as the tune comes back down, one note is an A-natural; listen for it in the player.
- [Bass](/songs/deck-the-halls/bass): you sing the roots, mostly E-flat and B-flat, the home chord and the one that leads back to it. In the "fa la la" that ends the first, second and last lines you drop for one quick note to a low A-flat, your lowest.

## Questions

### Who wrote Deck the Halls?

No one knows who made the tune. It is a Welsh harp air called Nos Galan, New Year's Eve, printed by John Parry in 1781 and Edward Jones in 1784 [^parry1781][^jones1784]. The English words are Thomas Oliphant's, printed in 1862 in John Thomas's Welsh Melodies [^thomas1862][^hullah1866]. The words most people sing, with "Don we now our gay apparel", are an American revision of his, in print by 1877; the reviser is not named [^psj1877].

### What are the original words of Deck the Halls?

Oliphant's 1862 words were a drinking song for the holidays. His first verse has "Fill the mead-cup, drain the barrel", his second "See the flowing bowl before us", and his third has the company "laughing, quaffing" as the old year passes [^thomas1862][^hullah1866]. Before Oliphant, the Welsh tune carried love verses: Jones printed a Welsh one in 1784 and an English one in 1794 [^jones1784][^jones1794].

### What does "Don we now our gay apparel" mean?

It means "let us now put on our festive clothes". To don is to put on, apparel is clothing, and gay is the older sense of the word: merry, or bright and showy [^dictionary]. The line is an American replacement, in print by 1877, for Oliphant's "Fill the mead-cup, drain the barrel" [^psj1877][^hullah1866].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune in our arrangement. With two singers you have the tune and the alto, a third below it for most of the verse; with three, add the bass on the roots; the tenor makes four. The tune's highest note, a top E-flat, comes on the last "la" of the rising "fa la la" in the middle.
