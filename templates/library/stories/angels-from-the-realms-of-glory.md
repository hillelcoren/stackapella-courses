Angels from the Realms of Glory is a Christmas hymn by James Montgomery, a poet who edited a newspaper in Sheffield, in the north of England. He first printed it in his own paper, the Iris, on Christmas Eve 1816, under the title The Nativity [^iris1816][^julian][^canterbury]. Its five verses call on five groups in turn, the angels, the shepherds, the wise men, the faithful at prayer and the sinners, and every verse ends with the same call: "Come and worship, Worship Christ the new-born King" [^iris1816][^cp1825][^ha]. In the United States and Canada it is usually sung to Henry Smart's tune REGENT SQUARE, printed in London in 1866; in Britain, often to IRIS, the French tune of Angels We Have Heard on High [^ha][^hymnary][^methodist].

## Where it comes from

**The poet and his paper.** James Montgomery was born at Irvine, in Ayrshire, Scotland, on 4 November 1771, the son of a Moravian minister, and was sent to the Moravian school at Fulneck, near Leeds [^dnb-montgomery][^hymnary-montgomery][^methodist]. In 1792 he went to Sheffield to work for Joseph Gales, who owned the Sheffield Register [^dnb-montgomery][^methodist]. When Gales fled the country in 1794 to escape prosecution, the paper carried on under Montgomery with a new name, the Iris [^methodist][^hymnary-montgomery]. Hymnology Archive dates his editorship from 1794; the Dictionary of National Biography says he became its owner in 1795 [^ha][^dnb-montgomery]. He was twice jailed in York Castle for what he printed, once for a song celebrating the fall of the Bastille and once for an account of a riot in Sheffield, and he gave the paper up in 1825 [^dnb-montgomery][^methodist][^hymnary-montgomery].

**The Nativity, 1816.** On Christmas Eve 1816 the Iris printed "The Nativity", five verses of six lines, signed only "J. M." [^iris1816][^julian][^canterbury]. A photograph of the page shows it at the head of a column, just above the paper's "Country News", with "Sheffield." at its foot beside the initials [^iris1816]. It is set in the long s of the time ("Worſhip Chriſt the new born King"). Four of the five groups are hailed with an exclamation mark, "Angels!", "Shepherds!", "Sages!", "Saints!", but the sinners get only a comma [^iris1816].

**Into the hymn books.** The hymn's earliest known appearance in a hymn book is Thomas Cotterill's A Selection of Psalms and Hymns, in its eighth edition, published in Sheffield by J. Montgomery in 1819, where it is Hymn 214, headed "Christmas", in the words of 1816 [^cotterill1819][^julian][^ha]. In 1825 Montgomery gathered his own hymns into the "Original Hymns" part of The Christian Psalmist, printed in Glasgow, and this one became No. 487, with a new title from the angel's words to the shepherds in Luke's Gospel: "Good tidings of great joy to all people" [^cp1825][^julian][^kjv]. In the book's opening essay he set out his rule: "a hymn must have a beginning, middle, and end", with "a manifest gradation in the thoughts" [^cp1825][^ha]. This hymn moves that way, from heaven to the first witnesses of the birth and on to the singers themselves.

**What he changed.** Side by side, the 1816 and 1825 printings differ in four words: "flock" became "flocks", "Waiting long with hope and fear" became "Watching long in hope and fear", and justice "repeals" the sentence became "revokes" it [^iris1816][^cp1825]. Julian and the Hymnology Archive each count three changes, not quite the same three [^julian][^ha]. Montgomery kept the 1825 words in his Original Hymns of 1853, and Julian calls that the authorised text [^julian][^ha].

**Smart's REGENT SQUARE.** Henry Smart was born in London on 26 October 1813 [^grove][^canterbury-smart]. He left the law for music and played the organ at Blackburn, in Lancashire, then in London at St Luke's, Old Street, from 1844 to 1864, and at St Pancras [^grove][^psh-tune][^ha-smart]. In 1866 the Presbyterian Church in England brought out a new book, Psalms and Hymns for Divine Worship, published in London by James Nisbet [^ph1866][^ha]. On its page 58 is a tune headed "Regent Square", marked "8,7,4." for verses of the same shape as Montgomery's, and printed with two hymns: Edward Denny's "Bright with all his crowns of glory" and Horatius Bonar's "Glory be to God the Father" [^ph1866][^ha]. The book's index of tunes calls it an "Original Melody, by Henry Smart" [^ph1866]. The tune is named for Regent Square Church in London, a Presbyterian church whose minister, James Hamilton, had worked for a new hymn book for the Presbyterian churches [^ha][^psh-tune][^dnb-hamilton]. The copy we read is dated 1866 on its title page, and the Synod's order adopting the book is dated 19 April 1866, but some hymnal handbooks give the year as 1867 [^ph1866][^ha][^psh-tune]. Smart died in London on 6 July 1879 [^grove][^canterbury-smart][^ha-smart].

**Words and tune together.** By 1871 they shared a page in New York, in The Baptist Praise Book: page 108 prints REGENT SQUARE over another Christmas hymn, with Montgomery's five verses beside it as No. 271 [^bpb1871][^ha].

**The French tune.** In 1928 The Oxford Book of Carols printed the hymn as No. 119, "Christmas: Epiphany", to a "French tune", the tune of the carol Les anges dans nos campagnes, with a harmony signed "M. S.", Martin Shaw, one of its editors [^obc1928][^ha]. Hymnology Archive traces this pairing to that book [^ha][^ha-angels]. The tune is now often called IRIS, after the newspaper in which the words first appeared [^hymnary][^methodist][^ha-angels]. It is the same tune as [Angels We Have Heard on High](/songs/angels-we-have-heard-on-high), whose own story tells where it comes from.

**Myth and record.** Often told: Montgomery based his hymn on the French carol [^psh]. What the record shows: the idea seems to go back to the 1928 Oxford Book of Carols, whose note says only that the hymn is close to a translation of the French carol's opening verses, the carol it took its tune from [^obc1928]. The French carol's earliest known printing with its tune is from Paris in 1842, twenty-six years after the Iris printing [^imslp1842][^ha-angels]. No source we read shows that Montgomery knew the carol in any form.

## What the words mean

Each verse speaks to one group as if they could hear, and calls them to worship [^ha][^cp1825].

**Verse 1.** The angels are told to fly out from heaven, the "realms of glory", over the whole earth. They "sang creation's story": Hymnology Archive reads this as the angels' song when the world was made, in the Book of Job, "when the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy" [^ha][^kjv]. Now they are to announce the birth of the Messiah.

**Verse 2.** The shepherds of Luke's Gospel, "abiding in the field, keeping watch over their flock by night", in the King James Bible's words, which Montgomery's line follows closely [^kjv][^iris1816]. "God with man is now residing" is the name Emmanuel, which Matthew's Gospel explains as "God with us" [^kjv][^ha]. The "infant light" is the newborn child, pictured as light shining in the dark [^ha].

**Verse 3.** The sages are the wise men from the east in Matthew's Gospel, who say "we have seen his star in the east, and are come to worship him" [^kjv][^ha]. They are told to leave their "contemplations", their studies, for something brighter. "The great Desire of nations" is a phrase from the prophet Haggai, "the desire of all nations shall come", which the hymn applies to Christ [^kjv][^ha]. "Natal" means to do with birth, so the "natal star" is the star of his birth [^webster-natal].

**Verse 4.** The saints are the faithful, bowed at the altar, waiting in hope and fear. They are promised that "the Lord, whom ye seek, shall suddenly come to his temple", the words of the prophet Malachi [^kjv][^ha]. Hymnology Archive sees in them Simeon and Anna, who waited in the Temple and greeted the baby Jesus there [^ha][^kjv].

**Verse 5.** Sinners "wrung with true repentance" are twisted with sorrow for what they have done; to wring once meant to press with pain [^webster-wring]. They stand condemned, but justice cancels the sentence and mercy tells them to break their chains. The Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology hears in it Psalm 85: "Mercy and truth are met together; righteousness and peace have kissed each other" [^canterbury][^kjv].

**The refrain.** "Come and worship, Worship Christ the new-born King" closes every verse, the word "worship" said twice in a row, so each group in turn is sent to the child [^cp1825][^ha].

### The full words

These are the five verses as Montgomery printed them in The Christian Psalmist (Glasgow, 1825), No. 487, the text he kept in 1853 [^cp1825][^julian]. Spelling is as printed. The 1825 print has a long dash after "Mercy calls you" in the last verse, shown here as a comma.

**Verse 1**
Angels, from the realms of glory,
Wing your flight o'er all the earth,
Ye who sang creation's story,
Now proclaim Messiah's birth;
Come and worship,
Worship Christ the new-born King.

**Verse 2**
Shepherds, in the field abiding,
Watching o'er your flocks by night,
God with man is now residing,
Yonder shines the infant-light;
Come and worship,
Worship Christ the new-born King.

**Verse 3**
Sages, leave your contemplations,
Brighter visions beam afar;
Seek the great Desire of nations;
Ye have seen his natal star;
Come and worship,
Worship Christ the new-born King.

**Verse 4**
Saints, before the altar bending,
Watching long in hope and fear,
Suddenly the Lord descending
In his temple shall appear;
Come and worship,
Worship Christ the new-born King.

**Verse 5**
Sinners, wrung with true repentance,
Doom'd for guilt to endless pains,
Justice now revokes the sentence,
Mercy calls you, break your chains;
Come and worship,
Worship Christ the new-born King.

## How it is sung

It is sung at Christmas, and because of its third verse, the wise men, at Epiphany too [^hymnary][^obc1928]. Its meter is 8.7.8.7.4.7: four long lines, then the short "Come and worship" and the last line [^ha][^ph1866]. Hymnary.org lists it as 8.7.8.7.8.7 [^hymnary].

In the United States and Canada it is usually REGENT SQUARE; in England, most often IRIS, also called GLORIA [^ha][^hymnary][^methodist]. In the Oxford Book of Carols of 1928 the French tune stretches "Come" over three bars of quick falling notes, and the refrain is sung twice, soft, then loud [^obc1928].

Our arrangement is read from Smart's four parts as Charles L. Hutchins printed them in The Church Hymnal (Boston, 1894), No. 60, headed "Christmas", "Regent Square" and "H. Smart", in C major and 4/4 [^hutchins1894]. Hutchins prints four of the five verses and, under the first, sets "Come and worship" twice to fill Smart's fifth line [^hutchins1894]. We sing it a tone lower, in B-flat, with Smart's tune and chords, the lower parts revoiced in places for beginners, one note to each syllable. We sing verses 1 to 3, the angels, the shepherds and the sages, in Hutchins's words, each with "Come and worship" twice [^hutchins1894]. It is for two to four singers: two take soprano, on the tune, and alto, a third adds the bass, and the tenor makes four.

## Versions and other words

- **A different last verse:** in 1825 The Christmas Box printed Montgomery's hymn beside "The Babe of Bethlehem", an anonymous carol in the same meter. One of its verses, "Though an infant now we view him", is sometimes sung in place of Montgomery's fifth, as in the Oxford Book of Carols of 1928 [^obc1928][^ha].
- **A doxology:** the Salisbury Hymn Book of 1857 has a verse of praise to the Trinity, "Saints and angels join in praising" [^julian][^ha]. The sources disagree on how it was used: Julian lists it among the doxologies sung in place of the sinners' verse, while the Hymnology Archive says that outside the Salisbury book it was usually sung as well as that verse, not instead of it [^julian][^ha].
- **Other words to REGENT SQUARE:** Edward Denny's "Bright with all his crowns of glory" and Horatius Bonar's "Glory be to God the Father", the two hymns it was first printed with in 1866 [^ph1866][^ha].

## Did you know?

- Henry Smart was losing his sight as REGENT SQUARE went to print. Grove's dictionary says he became too blind to write soon after 1864 and dictated his later music, and the Psalter Hymnal Handbook says he was completely blind at fifty-two [^grove][^psh-tune][^ha-smart].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/angels-from-the-realms-of-glory/soprano): Smart's tune, from low D to top E-flat, most often on B-flat, then F. Its low D comes three times a verse: the first and third lines start on F, dip to it ("gels", "who") and leap up to the D above on "realms" and "a-" of "creation's", and the second line ends on it ("earth"). The top E-flat comes once a verse, on "Wor-" of "Worship Christ", and the tune comes home from there to B-flat on "King". One syllable a verse takes two notes: "Mes-" in "Messiah's", "the" in "the infant-light" and "His" in "His natal star".
- [Alto](/songs/angels-from-the-realms-of-glory/alto): under the tune, one note to each syllable, most often on D, then F. In the third line, "sto-ry, Now pro-" in verse 1, it rocks between G and F-sharp: tune those half steps. Its low A comes on the last word before each refrain ("birth", "light", "star"), and right after it the refrain opens on its top A, on "Come and".
- [Tenor](/songs/angels-from-the-realms-of-glory/tenor): the middle of the chord, on B-flat more than half the time. Its top D comes four times in a row in the third line ("-ry, Now pro-claim", "-ing, Yonder shines", "-tions, Ye have seen"); keep it light. Its low F comes on "gels from" ("herds in", "ges, leave"), on the last word before the refrain and on "ship" in "Worship Christ".
- [Bass](/songs/angels-from-the-realms-of-glory/bass): the bass line, most often on B-flat, then D and F. The third line opens on its top B-flat ("Ye", "God", "Seek"), an octave above the note that ends the line before. Its low F comes three times a verse: on "the" in "all the earth" ("by", "a-"), on the last word before the refrain, and on "born" in "the new-born King".

## Questions

### Who wrote the music for Angels from the Realms of Glory?

It depends on where it is sung. In North America the tune is REGENT SQUARE by Henry Smart (1813 to 1879), an English organist, first printed in a London Presbyterian hymn book of 1866 [^ph1866][^ha][^psh-tune]. In Britain it is often sung to IRIS, the anonymous French tune of Les anges dans nos campagnes, first known from Paris in 1842 [^imslp1842][^ha-angels][^methodist]. Montgomery printed his words without any tune [^iris1816][^cp1825].

### Is Angels from the Realms of Glory the same tune as Angels We Have Heard on High?

Sometimes. They are two different songs, but in Britain Montgomery's words are often sung to the French tune of Angels We Have Heard on High, called IRIS when it carries them, a pairing that goes back to the Oxford Book of Carols of 1928 [^ha][^hymnary][^obc1928]. In North America the hymn is usually sung to Henry Smart's REGENT SQUARE instead [^ha][^hymnary].

### Why do some hymnals leave out the last verse?

Because of its hard opening, sinners "doom'd for guilt to endless pains", which many editors have found too severe [^canterbury][^ha]. Julian notes books that put a doxology in its place, such as one from the Salisbury Hymn Book of 1857, and the Oxford Book of Carols of 1928 ends with a verse from the anonymous carol "The Babe of Bethlehem" instead [^julian][^obc1928][^ha]. Those who keep it say it completes Montgomery's plan, from the angels to the sinners, and echoes Psalm 85's meeting of mercy and justice [^ha][^canterbury].
