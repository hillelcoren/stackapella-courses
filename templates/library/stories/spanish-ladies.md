Spanish Ladies, also sung as "Farewell and Adieu", or "Farewell to You, Ye Fine Spanish Ladies" in an old printing, is a sailors' song about leaving Spain and running home up the English Channel [^chappell][^whall]. Its chorus counts the way in: "From Ushant to Scilly is thirty-five leagues" [^sharp]. Its oldest known copy is a page in a Massachusetts captain's logbook, dated 1769 [^pease][^balladindex]. In 1906 an admiral wrote that the seamen of the Channel Fleet would always hear it with pleasure [^bridge]. Cecil Sharp printed it in 1909 from the singing of Captain Lewis of Minehead, the version we sing [^sharp].

## Where it comes from

**A song in a captain's book.** Peter Pease of Dartmouth, Massachusetts, kept one book through voyages between 1769 and 1771, whaling in Davis Strait and trading to Philadelphia and London, in the sloop Nelle and four other vessels [^pease]. Near the back, among a love song and a New Year's wish of the same years, is a page headed "A Boald Seamans Song in the year 1769": a bold seaman's song [^pease]. It opens "A Dew to you Ladies of Lisborn, A Dew to you Ladies of Spain", saying adieu to Lisbon as well as Spain. Then come the chorus, "we will Rant and we will Roar", soundings "from Ushant to Silley", and the run up the Channel past "the Deadman" and "the Ramhead of Plymouth" to an anchorage in the Downs [^pease]. At the foot, under Pease's name, the page says the song "was Rote By mr" Zadoh (or Zadok) Macksfield, as the name reads, "of Dartmouth in the County of Bristol marner 1769" [^pease]. Whether he made the song or only wrote it out, the page doesn't say.

**In the navy.** Its words follow a "grand fleet" home to anchor in the Downs. Grand Fleet, wrote the master mariner W. B. Whall, was the old name for the Channel Fleet, and the Downs the roadstead off Kent where homeward ships waited and squadrons met in wartime [^whall][^smyth]. In his Naval Sketch-Book of 1834, the naval officer William Glascock had two old seamen grumble that crews now sang soft, sentimental songs, not "the staves as we used to sing in the war", among them "From Ushant to Scilly is thirty-five leagues" [^glascock][^firth]. In 1840 Captain Frederick Marryat gave it to a fiddling Greenwich pensioner in his novel Poor Jack, writing that it had been very popular among seamen and was now almost forgotten [^marryat][^chappell].

**The tune in print.** That same year William Chappell printed the tune in his Collection of National English Airs as No. 204, marked "Slow", in a minor key [^chappell1840]. In 1859 he wrote that Lord Vernon had sent it to him, that copies of the words had come from several people, one from an old seaman in Devon, and he printed two versions of the first verse, the chorus and four more verses [^chappell].

![[1]]

**Sharp and Captain Lewis.** Cecil Sharp noted down sailors' chanties in Somerset, chiefly at Bridgwater and Minehead, and took Spanish Ladies from Captain Lewis of Minehead; he printed it in 1909 in Folk Songs from Somerset, Fifth Series, as No. 124, "Spanish Ladies. Sea Chanty" [^sharp]. Lieutenant W. Kettlewell of the Royal Navy, who had made a special study of the song, revised Captain Lewis's words for him [^sharp]. Sharp wrote that he knew of no earlier printing of the tune in its "purely diatonic form", the old minor without the raised seventh that the tune has in Chappell's printings, where the harmony is G. A. Macfarren's [^sharp][^chappell1840][^chappell].

![[2]]

**Myth and record.** Often told: the oldest text is in the 1796 log of HMS Nellie. What the record shows: the page is headed "in the year 1769", in the book of a Massachusetts master whose vessels included the sloop Nelle, a merchant ship, not a Royal Navy one [^pease][^balladindex]. The 1796 and the "HMS" look like slips in the retelling: no source we have read gives either.

## What the words mean

**Verse 1.** The ship has orders to sail for England, and the sailors hope to be back soon. Some old copies are bleaker: "perhaps we may never more see you again" [^chappell][^masefield].

**Chorus.** The sailors will rant and roar, loud and merry, all the way home; Robert Bell noted in 1857 that "rant and roar" was the old reading and "range and rove" a newer one [^bell][^chappell]. To strike soundings is to reach bottom with the deep-sea lead for the first time, coming in from sea, at about 80 to 100 fathoms (a fathom is six feet) [^smyth]. Ushant, the island off France, and the Scilly islands, off Land's End, mark the two sides of the Channel's mouth [^pilot]. A league is three nautical miles, so thirty-five leagues is about 105 [^smyth].

![[3]]

**The older verses.** We sing verse 1 and the chorus. The rest, as Chappell printed it, brings the ship home.

- *We hove our ship to.* To heave to is to set the sails against each other so the ship stops, here to sound; then they filled the main topsail to drive on, and bore away up the Channel [^smyth].
- *The first land we made.* The Deadman is the sailors' name for the Dodman, and Fairly for Fairlight near Hastings [^whall]. Whall put the Dodman "near Plymouth"; the Admiralty's Channel Pilot of 1863 places it more exactly, nearly midway between the Lizard and Rame Head at Plymouth's mouth, and calls Fairlight Down often the first landfall of ships homeward bound [^whall][^pilot].
- *The signal was made.* The fleet anchors in the Downs. The shank-painter holds the anchor against the ship's side, so clearing it readies the anchor to drop; clew garnets haul the big sails up to the yard; tacks and sheets hold a sail's lower corners [^smyth].
- *Let every man toss off a full bumper.* A bumper is a glass filled to the brim [^webster].

### The full words

Verse 1 and the chorus below are as Cecil Sharp printed them in 1909, the words we sing; every line of them is also in printings older than Sharp's, with small differences [^bell][^marryat][^bridge][^chappell]. Lieutenant W. Kettlewell revised Captain Lewis's words for Sharp, and Sharp's verses 2 to 5, on heaving to for soundings, the headlands, anchoring in the Downs and the toast, differ from every older printing we have read; we could not confirm when Kettlewell died, so those four verses are named here, not given [^sharp]. The four verses after the chorus are instead the older words William Chappell printed in 1859, gathered from several copies, with his spelling [^chappell]; sing the chorus after each. All of these words are traditional and were in print by 1859, so they are in the public domain [^chappell][^marryat].

**Verse 1**
Farewell and adieu to you Spanish ladies,
Farewell and adieu to you ladies of Spain;
For we've received orders for to sail for old England,
But we hope in a short time to see you again.

**Chorus**
We will rant and we'll roar like true British sailors,
We'll rant and we'll roar all on the salt seas,
Until we strike soundings in the channel of old England:
From Ushant to Scilly is thirty-five leagues.

**Verse 2**
We hove our ship to, with the wind at sou'west, boys,
We hove our ship to, for to strike soundings clear,
Then fill'd the main topsail, and bore right away, boys,
And straight up the Channel our course we did steer.

**Verse 3**
The first land we made, it is called the Deadman,
Next Ram Head, off Plymouth, Start, Portland, and Wight;
We sailed by Beachy, by Fairly, and Dungeness,
And then bore away for the South Foreland Light.

**Verse 4**
Then the signal was made for the grand fleet to anchor,
All in the Downs that night for to sleep;
Now stand by your stoppers, see clear your shank painters,
Hawl up your clew garnets, stick out tacks and sheets.

**Verse 5**
Now let ev'ry man toss off a full bumper,
Now let ev'ry man take off his full bowl,
For we will be jolly, and drown melancholy,
With a health to each jovial and true-hearted soul.

## How it is sung

- **At the capstan.** Sharp called it a capstan chanty, for weighing anchor. Captain Lewis sang it to him slowly, as a song; round the capstan, Sharp notes, it went much faster. Lieutenant Kettlewell told him the end of the chorus used to be met with a shout of "Heave and pawl": heave until the pawl, an iron catch that stops the capstan running back, can drop into place [^sharp][^smyth].
- **Off watch.** Yet Sharp also wrote that to the sailors of his day it was "a ballad pure and simple", a forecastle favourite [^sharp]. Admiral Sir Cyprian Bridge wrote in 1906 that navy sailors called such songs fore-bitters, after the timber frame near the foremast; Spanish Ladies, he said, had not quite lost its vogue, though it was probably sung now more by midshipmen than seamen [^bridge]. Whall put it among his songs, not his shanties, to be sung slowly in unison [^whall].

So it is best called a forebitter, a song for listening, which some crews also sang at the capstan. A Library of Congress folklorist writes that naval sailors did not sing work shanties, thought bad for discipline; the shanties grew up on merchant ships, where Black and Afro-Caribbean sailors were central, often the most valued singers on board [^winick]. Sharp saw the chanty passing as steam took over the work [^sharp].

Our arrangement is read from Sharp's page, at his printed pitch in A minor, in 3/4 time, for 2 to 4 singers: everyone sings verse 1 and then the chorus twice, a little slower than Captain Lewis sang it. The soprano sings Sharp's voice part note for note, and the alto, tenor and bass sing the words on the chords of his piano part, the bass from his left hand. Under the voice's last note Sharp's piano has A minor; we end on A major, the chord of his piano postlude.

- In Moby-Dick (1851), the midnight watch on the Pequod sings its first lines in chorus, until a Nantucket sailor tells them not to be sentimental [^melville].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/spanish-ladies/soprano): you carry Sharp's tune in its old minor form, mostly on A and C. It climbs to your top E on "sail" and "But" in the verse, and on "channel" and "From" in each chorus, then drops to a low D on "to" in "short time to see you again" and on "is" in "Scilly is thirty five leagues".
- [Alto](/songs/spanish-ladies/alto): you sing the words with the tune all the way, mostly on C and E. Your top A comes only on "strike" in "Until we strike soundings", and on the very last "leagues" your C-sharp turns the final chord major.
- [Tenor](/songs/spanish-ladies/tenor): you sing in the middle of the chord, mostly on E and G, staying on E for most of "Farewell and adieu to you Spanish ladies". Your top D is on "Until" in each chorus.
- [Bass](/songs/spanish-ladies/bass): you sing Sharp's left hand, mostly on A and C. You reach your top A just once, on "hope", and drop to a low F on "old" in every "old England", on "like" in "like true British sailors" and on "roar" in "we'll roar all on the salt seas".

## Questions

### Why does Quint sing "Spanish Ladies" in Jaws?

The film never says, but the song suits him. In the 1975 film Jaws, Quint, played by Robert Shaw (died 1978), sings and hums it as he hunts the shark [^jaws][^screenrant][^loc][^irishtimes]. Quint, a fisherman, tells of serving on the USS Indianapolis in the war [^afi]. Spanish Ladies had been a sailors' song for two centuries: sung in navy forecastles, copied into a Massachusetts whaling master's book in 1769, and sung by Melville's fictional whalemen [^bridge][^pease][^melville].

### Who wrote "Spanish Ladies"?

No one knows: it is traditional. Its oldest known copy, dated 1769 in Peter Pease's logbook, says the song was written by a Dartmouth, Massachusetts, mariner whose name reads Zadoh or Zadok Macksfield, which may mean only that he wrote it out [^pease]. Its words follow a navy fleet home up the Channel to the Downs, and the tune was in print in London by 1840 [^whall][^bridge][^chappell1840][^chappell].

### Is "Spanish Ladies" a sea shanty?

Not really: it is a forebitter, sung for pleasure off watch, though some crews worked the capstan to it. Cecil Sharp printed it as a capstan chanty but said sailors of his day sang it as a ballad; W. B. Whall calls it a song sung slowly in unison, and Admiral Sir Cyprian Bridge counts it among the old songs of the forecastle [^sharp][^whall][^bridge]. Naval sailors, who sang it often, did not sing work shanties [^winick].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, Cecil Sharp's voice part note for note, in its old minor form. Everyone sings the words together: the alto, tenor and bass sing the chords of Sharp's 1909 piano part, and the alto's C-sharp turns the last chord major [^sharp].
