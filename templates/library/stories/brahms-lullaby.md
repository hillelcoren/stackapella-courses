Brahms's Lullaby is the Wiegenlied, "cradle song", Op. 49 No. 4, for voice and piano by Johannes Brahms (1833 to 1897) [^baker][^forty]. It begins "Guten Abend, gut' Nacht"; in English it is the Cradle Song, or "Lullaby and Good Night", a line from Natalia Macfarren's English of the 1870s that Arthur Westbrook kept in his of 1901 [^simrock-en][^forty]. Brahms wrote it in the summer of 1868 as a gift for his friend Bertha Faber in Vienna, on the birth of her second son, and hid in its piano part a Viennese song she had sung to him years before [^kalbeck2][^friedlaender][^buja]. Its first verse is older: a children's rhyme printed in 1808 [^wunderhorn][^friedlaender]. By 1922 Max Friedlaender could call it the most popular and best known of all his songs [^friedlaender].

## Where it comes from

**The singer from Vienna.** In 1859 Brahms was in Hamburg, leading a choir of young women [^kalbeck1][^predota]. Among them was Bertha Porubszky, a pastor's daughter from Vienna, who was staying in Hamburg with her aunt, Auguste Brandt [^kalbeck1][^predota][^buja]. Through her, the biographer Max Kalbeck says, Brahms first heard Viennese songs [^kalbeck1]. One was Alexander Baumann's dialect song that begins "Du moanst wohl, du moanst wohl, die Lieb laßt si zwinga?": "You think, do you, that love can be forced?" [^kalbeck1][^friedlaender]. Its answer is "'s ist anderst", "it's not like that", and the song is often called "S'is anderscht" [^kalbeck1][^buja]. Bertha went home and married Arthur Faber [^predota][^buja].

**The lullaby.** In the summer of 1868, in Bonn, Brahms heard that the Fabers had a second son, Hans, and wrote them a cradle song [^bi][^omi][^kalbeck2]. He sent it with a letter dated 15 July 1868: Bertha would see, he told Arthur, that he had made it "yesterday", just for her little one [^omi][^kalbeck2][^friedlaender]. He added a joke she would understand: while she sings Hans to sleep, her husband sings to her and murmurs a love song [^kalbeck2][^friedlaender]. The love song was Baumann's. Its waltz tune lies almost note for note in the piano part, while the voice sings a new melody against it [^kalbeck1][^friedlaender]. Brahms pointed it out to his Bonn friend Hermann Deiters: the accompaniment hinted at a waltz the lady knew [^kalbeck2][^friedlaender]. He wrote on the copy "Arthur und Bertha Faber zu allzeit fröhlichem Gebrauch": for Arthur and Bertha Faber, to use happily at any time [^kalbeck1][^friedlaender].

![[1]]

**Into print.** Brahms had not meant to publish it, he told Bertha, but his publisher, Fritz Simrock, saw it and held on to it [^kalbeck2][^friedlaender]. It came out in Berlin as No. 4 of the Fünf Lieder, Op. 49 [^first-ed][^bi]. The Brahms-Institut in Lübeck dates the set to November 1868, but by Kalbeck's account Brahms had already sent Bertha the printed book with a letter of 25 October [^bi][^kalbeck2]. Over the song the page says only "An B. F. in Wien", to B. F. in Vienna, and Brahms wrote to her hoping she would not mind those familiar initials [^first-ed][^kalbeck2][^friedlaender]. In the same letter, as Kalbeck prints it, he joked about whether Hanslick, of all people, would sniff out the Austrian tune he had smuggled in [^kalbeck2][^friedlaender].

![[2]]

**The words.** The first edition's single verse is a German children's rhyme [^first-ed], which Achim von Arnim and Clemens Brentano printed in 1808 as "Gute Nacht, mein Kind!", "Good night, my child!", among the children's songs closing Des Knaben Wunderhorn, their collection of old German songs [^wunderhorn][^friedlaender]. The cover of Brahms's set says the song came "aus Simrock's Kinderbuch", from Simrock's children's book, and a Boston edition of 1903 went further and printed "Karl Simrock (1802-1876)" over it as the author [^first-ed][^forty]. But Friedlaender showed in 1922 that Brahms's words follow the Wunderhorn [^wunderhorn][^friedlaender].

**The second verse.** The song spread fast, and Simrock, Friedlaender writes, proposed a second verse by Georg Scherer [^friedlaender]. Scherer had printed it in 1849 in his children's book Alte und neue Kinderlieder, under the Wunderhorn verse and its old title, "Gute Nacht, mein Kind!", ending "Droben im Paradies, schlaf' nun selig und süß": up in paradise, now sleep happy and sweet [^scherer][^friedlaender]. Its last two lines did not fit Brahms's rhythm, so Brahms reworked them into the lines now sung, "Schlaf nun selig und süß, schau im Traum 's Paradies" [^friedlaender][^bi]. He was never happy with them: he let them be printed in 1873, he wrote, only because he knew nothing better [^friedlaender]. The earliest printing with the verse that we have seen is Simrock's German and English issue of about 1874, which gives both verses on its text page while the music carries only the first [^friedlaender][^simrock-en].

**The English.** The opening is older than Westbrook. "Lullaby and good night; With roses bedight" begins Natalia Macfarren's English in a German and English issue that Simrock printed with Stanley Lucas, Weber & Co. of London, which the Bavarian State Library dates to about 1874; her second verse already has "Bright angels" who "will guard thee from harms" [^simrock-en]. The English we sing, credited to "Arthur Westbrook", was printed by Oliver Ditson of Boston, under a copyright of 1901, in Forty Songs by Brahms, edited by James Huneker in 1903 [^forty]. Westbrook kept her first lines and rewrote the rest [^simrock-en][^forty]. The Library of Congress, citing a reference book of pen names, lists that name as one used by Charles Fonteyn Manney (1872 to 1951), an editor at Ditson [^lc-westbrook][^lc-manney][^baker][^imslp-manney]. The IMSLP score library instead names a separate man, Arthur Edward Westbrook, who died in 1952 [^imslp-westbrook].

**Myth and record.** Often told: the lullaby's melody is an Austrian folk song Bertha used to sing. What the record shows: the voice sings Brahms's own tune; Bertha's song is in the piano part [^kalbeck1][^friedlaender][^buja].

### Timeline

- 1808: The rhyme "Gute Nacht, mein Kind!" is printed among the children's songs of Des Knaben Wunderhorn, in Heidelberg. [^wunderhorn][^friedlaender]
- 1849: Georg Scherer prints the verse that later becomes the second. [^scherer][^friedlaender]
- 1859: In Hamburg, Brahms hears Bertha Porubszky sing Baumann's Viennese song. [^kalbeck1][^friedlaender]
- 1868: In Bonn, Brahms writes the Wiegenlied and sends it to Arthur and Bertha Faber with a letter dated 15 July. [^omi][^kalbeck2]
- 1868: Simrock publishes it in Berlin as Op. 49 No. 4, with one verse. [^first-ed][^bi]
- 1874: About this year, by the Bavarian State Library's dating, Simrock prints the song with Scherer's second verse and Natalia Macfarren's English, "Lullaby and good night". [^simrock-en][^friedlaender]
- 1901: Oliver Ditson in Boston copyrights the song with Arthur Westbrook's English, which keeps Macfarren's opening, "Lullaby and good night". [^forty][^simrock-en]

## What the words mean

We sing Arthur Westbrook's two English verses as Ditson printed them in 1903, over Brahms's German [^forty]. They are a free version: the first verse follows the German closely; the second keeps its angels and drops its Christmas picture [^forty].

**Verse 1.** "With roses bedight": decked with roses; "bedight" is an old word for adorned [^webster], and echoes the German "bedacht". "If God will, thou shalt wake when the morning doth break": God willing, you will wake when morning comes. It keeps the German verse's ending, "Morgen früh, wenn Gott will", tomorrow morning, if God wills [^wunderhorn][^first-ed].

**Verse 2.** The angels keep watch, and "dream-land's sweet charm", the magic of dreams, keeps the night gentle [^forty].

### Brahms's German

The first verse is read from the first edition, which has "gut' Nacht" where the Wunderhorn has "gute Nacht", and "wenn Gott will" where it has "wenns Gott will"; both mean "if God wills" [^wunderhorn][^first-ed]. "Bedacht" means roofed or covered over, so the child sleeps under roses [^friedlaender]. "Näglein" are carnations; the old word also means cloves [^friedlaender][^dwb]. The second verse, Scherer's with Brahms's last lines, is as the 1903 Ditson book prints it [^forty][^friedlaender]. Its "Christkindleins Baum" is the tree of the Christ Child, the Christkind who brings children their Christmas presents [^dwds-christkind]. Under each line is our own English.

**Verse 1**
> Guten Abend, gut' Nacht,
Good evening, good night,
> mit Rosen bedacht,
roofed over with roses,
> mit Näglein besteckt
with carnations tucked all round:
> schlupf' unter die Deck':
slip under the covers.
> Morgen früh, wenn Gott will,
Tomorrow morning, God willing,
> wirst du wieder geweckt.
you will be woken again.

**Verse 2**
> Guten Abend, gut' Nacht,
Good evening, good night,
> von Eng'lein bewacht,
watched over by little angels,
> die zeigen im Traum
who show you, in a dream,
> dir Christkindleins Baum:
the Christ Child's tree.
> Schlaf' nun selig und süss,
Now sleep, happy and sweet,
> schau' im Traum's Paradies.
and see paradise in your dream.

### The full words

Arthur Westbrook's two verses as Oliver Ditson printed them in Forty Songs (Boston, 1903), pp. 63 to 65, the printing our arrangement reads its words from; the page carries a Ditson copyright of 1901 [^forty]. Brahms's setting sings each verse's last two lines twice; here they are given once.

**Verse 1**
Lullaby and good night!
With roses bedight,
Creep into thy bed,
There pillow thy head.
If God will, thou shalt wake
when the morning doth break.

**Verse 2**
Lullaby and good night;
Those blue eyes close tight;
Bright angels are near,
So sleep without fear.
They will guard thee from harm
With fair dream-land's sweet charm.

## How it is sung

It is a Lied, an art song for voice and piano, and for half a century before 1922, Friedlaender wrote, mothers had sung their children to sleep with it [^first-ed][^friedlaender]. Brahms marked it "Zart bewegt", gently moving, and set it in E-flat major, in 3/4 time, beginning on an upbeat [^first-ed]. Most lullabies drift downward, Friedlaender noticed, but this one climbs from its first notes [^friedlaender]. By 1888 Deiters called it very popular, and Brahms teased Simrock about its many arrangements: why not an edition in a minor key, for naughty or sickly children? [^deiters][^friedlaender]

![[3]]

Our arrangement is read from the first edition, in Brahms's key of E-flat, with Westbrook's English and its underlay from the 1903 Ditson book, which printed the song in C [^first-ed][^forty]. We sing two verses, as printings from the 1870s on give them (the first edition has one verse, with a repeat sign to sing it again), and leave out his two grace notes [^first-ed][^simrock-en]. It is for 2 to 4 singers: the soprano has the tune, the alto the same words a third or a sixth below, and the tenor and bass soft "oo" chords from Brahms's left hand.

## Did you know?

- A wish much like the lullaby's opening was written down at the end of the 1400s, among model phrases for love letters: "Ich wünschen dir ein gute nacht, von rosen ein dach", I wish you a good night, with a roof of roses [^mone][^muellenhoff]. By 1845 a Low German form of the rhyme, "Goden Abent, gode Nacht! Mit Rosen bedacht", was known in Holstein [^muellenhoff][^friedlaender].
- Brahms wrote his first manuscript of the lullaby on the back of a page of "Ruhe, Süßliebchen", one of his Magelone songs [^bi][^friedlaender]. Another copy in his hand is in the Glinka National Museum of Musical Culture in Moscow; a facsimile edition describes it as the copy he sent the Fabers [^bi][^omi].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/brahms-lullaby/soprano): Brahms's rocking tune, every line one easy breath. Each "If God will" and "They will guard" leaps a full octave, from the low E-flat to the high one, so take the low "If God" lightly and let the voice float up; the high E-flat comes back on "roses", "head", "blue" and "fear".
- [Alto](/songs/brahms-lullaby/alto): starts each verse on the same G as the tune, then sings the words mostly a third or a sixth below it. Through "Creep into thy bed" and "Bright angels are near" it rests on one D. It drops to its low G on each "If God", "They will", "wake" and "harm", and on each verse's last word, "break" and "charm".
- [Tenor](/songs/brahms-lullaby/tenor): soft "oo" chords from Brahms's piano part on just four notes, F, G, A-flat and B-flat. For the first four lines of each verse it holds B-flat; under "If God will" and "They will guard" it moves gently between G and A-flat, with an F under "morning" and "dream-land's".
- [Bass](/songs/brahms-lullaby/bass): low "oo"s on just three notes, B-flat, E-flat and A-flat, almost always the root of the chord. It rests on each line's pickup and comes in on the downbeat, reaching its low A-flat under "will" and "guard".

## Questions

### Who wrote the melody of Brahms's Lullaby?

Johannes Brahms did, in July 1868 [^kalbeck2][^omi]. What he borrowed lies almost note for note in the piano part, under his own melody: the tune of Alexander Baumann's Viennese song, which Bertha Faber had sung to him in Hamburg [^kalbeck1][^friedlaender]. The words are not his either: the first verse is an old German rhyme, the second Georg Scherer's [^wunderhorn][^scherer].

### Is "Lullaby and Good Night" the same song?

Yes. "Lullaby and good night" is the first line of Natalia Macfarren's English, printed with Brahms's song by Simrock in the 1870s, and Arthur Westbrook kept it in the English that Oliver Ditson of Boston copyrighted in 1901 and printed under Brahms's German in 1903 [^simrock-en][^forty]. Westbrook's two verses are the words we sing [^forty]. The German original, Brahms's Wiegenlied, begins "Guten Abend, gut' Nacht", "Good evening, good night" [^first-ed].

### Why is it "gute Nacht" and not "guten Nacht"?

Because "Nacht", night, is feminine in German, and "Abend", evening, is masculine [^dwds-nacht][^dwds-abend]. Both are short for a wish, "I wish you a good evening, a good night", where a masculine noun takes "guten" and a feminine one takes "gute": "(einen) guten Abend", "(eine) gute Nacht" [^dwds-abend][^dwds-nacht].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings Brahms's tune in our arrangement. With two singers you have the tune and the alto; with three, add the bass's low "oo"s. The tune spans one octave, from E-flat to E-flat, so it suits most voices, but each "If God will" leaps the whole octave at once.
