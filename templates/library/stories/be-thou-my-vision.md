Be Thou My Vision is a prayer from medieval Ireland, made into an English hymn in the 20th century. Its Irish poem, "Rop tú mo baile", asks God to be everything to the one who prays: sight, thought, wisdom, father, shield and treasure [^eriu1905][^celt]. Nobody knows who wrote it [^celt][^hymnarchive]. Mary Byrne printed it with a plain English translation in 1905, and Eleanor Hull turned that into rhyming couplets in 1912 [^eriu1905][^hull1912]. The earliest known pairing of the words with their tune, SLANE, an Irish folk melody, is in the Church of Ireland's hymnal of 1919 [^hymnarchive][^methodist]. The Presbyterian churches of Scotland, Ireland, England and Wales printed it in their shared hymnal of 1927 [^rch1927][^moffatt1927].

## Where it comes from

**The poem.** The Irish text survives in three late manuscripts. The best copy is in the Royal Irish Academy in Dublin, manuscript 23 N 10, pages 95 and 96, written in the 1500s [^eriu1905][^isos][^hymnarchive]. One of its scribes dated his work 1575 [^isos]. A second copy in the same library, 23 E 16, is in Byrne's words "only a careless transcript" of the first [^eriu1905][^hymnarchive]. A third, once owned by the collector Thomas Phillipps, is in the National Library of Ireland; Monica Nevin edited it in 1940 [^hymnarchive][^celt]. Scholars judge the language to be early Middle Irish, written somewhere between about 900 and 1100 [^celt][^hymnarchive][^canterbury]. Byrne's text runs to sixteen two-line stanzas, and ten of them open "Rob tu" or "Rob tusa", "be thou" [^eriu1905][^isos].

![[1]]

**The translation and the verse.** Mary Byrne (1880 to 1931) was a Dublin scholar of early Irish [^canterbury-byrne][^hymnarchive]. In 1905 she printed the Irish text, with a line-by-line English prose translation, under the title "A Prayer" in Ériu, the journal of the School of Irish Learning in Dublin [^eriu1905][^hymnarchive]. Her first line already reads "Be thou my vision, O Lord of my heart". Eleanor Hull (1860 to 1935) was born in England to an Irish family and spent her life on Irish literature and history; she was a founder of the Irish Texts Society [^canterbury-hull][^dub-hull]. In 1912 she put Byrne's prose into twelve rhyming couplets for her anthology The Poem-Book of the Gael. It sits among the book's early Christian poems, and her notes credit the Irish text and the literal translation to Byrne [^hull1912][^hymnarchive].

![[2]]

**The tune.** No composer is known. The earliest known printing is No. 323 in Old Irish Folk Music and Songs (1909), edited by P. W. Joyce for the Royal Society of Antiquaries of Ireland [^joyce1909][^hymnarchive]. Joyce printed it as "With my love on the road": two lines of music in 3/4 time, with no words and no note on where he found it, in the part of the book drawn from his own collection [^joyce1909]. His preface says he grew up in Glenosheen, in the Ballyhoura Mountains of County Limerick, where music and song "were in the air of the valley". From 1853 he wrote down every air he could remember, then went among the people noting more [^joyce1909].

![[3]]

**Words and tune together.** The earliest known pairing is in the Church of Ireland's Church Hymnal of 1919, which set Hull's words, with changes, to Joyce's tune and called it SLANE [^hymnarchive][^methodist][^hymnary-ich3]. In 1927 the revised Church Hymnary printed it as No. 477, its chief music editor the Welsh composer David Evans [^rch1927][^moffatt1927]. Sources disagree on the first pairing: the Psalter Hymnal Handbook says SLANE became a hymn tune through Evans's arrangement in that 1927 book [^hymnary-slane].

**Myth and record: the author.** Often told: the poem was written by Saint Dallán Forgaill [^hymnarchive][^hymnary-text], an Irish poet of the late 500s [^hymnarchive][^dnb-dallan]; many hymnals print "Irish, 8th century" [^hymnary-text]. What the record shows: no copy is older than the 1400s or 1500s [^hymnarchive][^isos], and its language points to about 900 to 1100 [^hymnarchive][^celt]. Neither Byrne nor Hull named any author [^eriu1905][^hull1912]. The Dictionary of National Biography (1888) gives Dallán only "fl. 600", active about 600, with his death year unknown, and of the three poems it credits to him, this prayer is not one [^dnb-dallan].

**Myth and record: the hill.** The tune is named after the Hill of Slane in County Meath, across the plain of Brega from Tara [^hymnarchive][^hymnary-slane][^bury1905][^muirchu]. Often told: there Patrick lit an Easter fire against the order of King Lóegaire that no fire should burn before the king's own [^hymnary-slane][^bury1905][^moffatt1927]. What the record shows: the story is first told in Lives of Patrick written in the late 600s, two centuries after him, at length by the churchman Muirchú and briefly by Tírechán [^bury1905][^dnb-muirchu]. Muirchú places the fire at the burial place of the men of Fíacc, on the plain of Brega, and names Slane only as the town where a convert's relics were later honoured [^muirchu]. By 1905 the historian J. B. Bury found it "evident that this was a tradition at Slane". He called the tale a legend, though Patrick may really have spent his first Easter there [^bury1905]. The tune has no known link with Patrick: in 1909 it was "With my love on the road" [^joyce1909][^hymnarchive].

## What the words mean

The words we sing are Hull's, as she printed them in 1912. Behind them is Byrne's prose, which follows the sixteen Irish stanzas. Hull's twelve couplets follow twelve of them, one moved out of order [^eriu1905][^hull1912]. Among the stanzas she let go are prayers that God guard every possession and hear the cry of a "miserable wretch" [^eriu1905].

1. Be my vision, Lord. Nothing else matters to me except that you are. Be my best thought day and night, and your presence my light, awake or asleep. "Naught" means nothing.
2. Be my wisdom and my true word. I am with you and you with me, a father and his son, you living in me.
3. Be my shield and sword in battle, my dignity and delight, my shelter and high tower. Raise me up to heaven.
4. I care nothing for riches or praise. You are my inheritance, first in my heart, my treasure. Byrne's prose has "estate" for inheritance: property, what a person owns [^eriu1905].
5. King of the seven heavens, give me your love in my heart and your light in my soul, and let them never leave. "Dole" here is an old word for a share handed out.
6. After the victory, let me reach heaven's joys. Heart of my heart, whatever happens, still be my vision, ruler of all.

The poem ends where it began, on "vision": in the Irish, the last line closes on "rob tu mo boile", the words that open the first [^eriu1905].

### The Irish poem

The sixteen Irish stanzas as Mary Byrne printed them in Ériu in 1905 from the Royal Irish Academy manuscript 23 N 10, each line followed by our own plain meaning, which follows her translation and keeps her doubts where she marked a reading uncertain [^eriu1905]. Her italics, which show letters she supplied for the scribe's shortened words, are set as plain letters, and her footnotes are left out. Byrne died in 1931, so her edition is in the public domain.

> Rob tu mo bhoile, a Comdi cride.
Be my vision, O Lord of my heart.
> Ní ní nech aile, acht ri secht nime.
No one else is anything to me but the King of the seven heavens.

> Rob tu mo scrutain i llo 'san-aidche.
Be my thought by day and by night;
> Rop tu atcheur im cotlad caidhce.
may it be you I see always in my sleep.

> Rob tu mo labhradh, rob tu mo tuicsi,
Be my speech, be my understanding;
> Rob tusa damsa, rob misi duitsi.
be with me, and let me be with you.

> Rob tusa m' athair, rob me do mac-sa.
Be my father, and let me be your son;
> Rob tusa lemsa, rob misi latsa.
be mine, and let me be yours.

> Rob tu mo cathsciath, rob tu mo cloidem.
Be my battle-shield, be my sword;
> Rob tusa m' ordan, rob tusa m' airer.
be my dignity, be my delight.

> Rob tu mo didiu rob tu mo daingen.
Be my shelter, be my stronghold;
> Rob tu romtogba a n-aontaig aingel.
raise me up to the company of the angels.

> Rob tu gach maithus dom curp, dom anmain.
Be every good thing to my body and my soul;
> Rob tu mo flaithus a nim occus i talmain.
be my kingdom in heaven and on earth.

> Rob tusa t' oinar sainṡerc mo cridhe.
Be you alone the first love of my heart;
> Ni rob nech aile, a airdrig nime.
let there be no one else, O high King of heaven.

> Co talla orm rondul it lama
Until I can pass into your hands (Byrne marks the sense as uncertain),
> Mo cuid, mo cuisle ar med do grada.
my treasure, my beloved, through the greatness of your love.

> Rob tusa at' aonar m'[ḟ]erann uais ammra
Be you alone my noble and wonderful estate;
> Ni cuinngim doine na maine marba.
I do not seek people, or lifeless wealth.

> Rob tusa dinsir cech selb, cech saoghal;
Be the constant guardian of every possession and every life (again uncertain);
> Mar marb ar mbrenadh art fegad t' aonar.
for our corrupt desires die at the mere sight of you.

> Do serc am anmuin, do grad im craidhe
Your love in my soul, your love in my heart:
> Tabuir dam amlaidh, a ri secht nime.
give me this, O King of the seven heavens.

> A ri secht nime, tabair dam amlaidh
O King of the seven heavens, give me this:
> Do gradh im cride, do serc im anmain.
your love in my heart and in my soul.

> Ac righ na n-uile ris iar mbuaid leire
With the King of all, with Him (Byrne marks this as uncertain), after the victory won by devotion,
> Robeo i flaith nime, a gile grene.
may I be in the kingdom of heaven, O brightness of the sun.

> Athair inmain, cluin, cluin mo nuallsa.
Beloved Father, hear, hear my lament;
> Mithig monuarán lasin truagan truaghsa.
it is time for the cry of woe of this poor wretch.

> A cride mo cridhe, cipead dommaire,
Heart of my heart, whatever happens to me,
> A flaith na n-uile, rob tu mo boile.
O ruler of all, be my vision.

### The full words

Eleanor Hull's twelve couplets as she printed them in The Poem-Book of the Gael in 1912, pages 119 and 120, the text our song is checked against [^hull1912]. Hymnals print fewer and change words: the 1927 Church Hymnary has five four-line verses, without the two "King of the seven heavens" couplets [^rch1927]. Hull died in 1935, so her verse is in the public domain.

Be Thou my Vision, O Lord of my heart,
Naught is all else to me, save that Thou art.

Thou my best thought by day and by night,
Waking or sleeping, Thy presence my light.

Be Thou my Wisdom, Thou my true Word;
I ever with Thee, Thou with me, Lord.

Thou my great Father, I thy dear son;
Thou in me dwelling, I with Thee one.

Be Thou my battle-shield, sword for the fight,
Be Thou my dignity, Thou my delight.

Thou my soul's shelter, Thou my high tower;
Raise Thou me heavenward, Power of my power.

Riches I heed not or man's empty praise,
Thou mine inheritance now and always.

Thou, and Thou only, first in my heart,
High King of Heaven, my treasure Thou art.

King of the seven heavens, grant me for dole,
Thy love in my heart, Thy light in my soul.

Thy light from my soul, Thy love from my heart,
King of the seven heavens, may they never depart.

With the High King of heaven, after victory won,
May I reach heaven's joys, O Bright heaven's Sun!

Heart of my own heart, whatever befall,
Still be my Vision, O Ruler of all.

## How it is sung

It is a hymn of devotion and trust, printed in the hymnals of many churches and not tied to one season [^hymnary-text][^hymnary-slane]. Its tune is SLANE, and its meter is 10.10.10.10: four lines of ten syllables [^rch1927][^hymnary-slane].

Its Irish poem was written centuries before the Reformation, and Protestant churches brought it into hymnals first: the Church of Ireland in 1919 and the Presbyterian Church Hymnary in 1927 [^hymnary-ich3][^rch1927]. Catholic hymnals print it too, among them The Pius X Hymnal of 1953 [^hymnary-btmv-catholic]. It is in the public domain as Hull wrote it: the poem is medieval, Byrne died in 1931 and Hull in 1935, and SLANE is a traditional tune printed in 1909 [^canterbury-byrne][^dub-hull][^joyce1909]. Later hymnal versions and arrangements can carry their own rights.

Our arrangement is one verse, Hull's first two couplets, for 2 to 4 singers in F major and 3/4 time. Joyce printed the tune in 3/4 too, with one flat in the key signature [^joyce1909]. The harmony is our own, not the 1919 hymnal's.

## Versions and other words

Hymnals changed Hull's words almost from the start. Besides dropping two couplets, the 1927 Church Hymnary sang "Naught be all else to me", "by day or by night" and "I Thy true son" where Hull has "Naught is all else to me", "by day and by night" and "I thy dear son" [^rch1927][^hull1912]. We sing Hull's own words.

The tune carries other words too. Jan Struther (1901 to 1953) wrote "Lord of all hopefulness" for it, a hymn for the hours of the day, in the enlarged Songs of Praise of 1931 [^canterbury-hopeful][^hymnary-hopeful].

## Did you know?

- The 1927 handbook to the Church Hymnary tells the story of Patrick's fire at Slane in full, but not under this hymn. It tells it under "I bind unto myself to-day", the hymn known as Saint Patrick's Breastplate [^moffatt1927].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/be-thou-my-vision/soprano): you carry the tune, and it runs wide. It dips to low C on "-sion, O", then climbs to its high F on "best" in the third line, "Thou my best thought". Take that top gently, or move the key.
- [Alto](/songs/be-thou-my-vision/alto): you sing the words mostly a third or sixth under the tune, moving mostly by step. On "my best thought by" you rock between B-flat and A at the top of your part. At the very end you skip down a third, from C to your lowest note, low A, on "light".
- [Tenor](/songs/be-thou-my-vision/tenor): you sing the middle of the chords, often resting on F, and never leap more than a fourth. You hold your top note, C, through "my heart, Naught is", then come back to F.
- [Bass](/songs/be-thou-my-vision/bass): you walk the bass line, by step about half the time. Under "Naught is all else to" you step down C, B-flat, A, G to a low F, and you end on that low F under "light".

## Questions

### Who wrote Be Thou My Vision originally?

No one knows. The Irish poem is anonymous, and scholars date its language to about 900 to 1100 [^celt][^hymnarchive]. Hymnal notes often credit Saint Dallán Forgaill, a poet of the late 500s, but its oldest copies are from the 1400s or 1500s, and neither of its first editors named an author [^hymnarchive][^dnb-dallan][^eriu1905][^hull1912]. The English words are Mary Byrne's prose translation of 1905, put into verse by Eleanor Hull in 1912 [^eriu1905][^hull1912].

### Who wrote the music for Be Thou My Vision?

No composer is known. SLANE is a traditional Irish folk tune. Its earliest known printing is in P. W. Joyce's Old Irish Folk Music and Songs (1909), titled "With my love on the road", with no words [^joyce1909][^hymnarchive]. Its earliest known pairing with these words is in the Church of Ireland's hymnal of 1919, which named it SLANE after the hill in County Meath linked with the legend of Saint Patrick's Easter fire [^hymnarchive][^methodist][^hymnary-slane].

### What are the original lyrics of Be Thou My Vision?

The original is an Irish poem of sixteen stanzas, from about 900 to 1100, beginning "Rop tú mo baile" [^eriu1905][^celt]. Its first English form is Mary Byrne's line-by-line prose of 1905, which starts "Be thou my vision, O Lord of my heart" [^eriu1905]. Eleanor Hull made it into twelve rhyming couplets in 1912, the words hymnals sing. Most hymnals print fewer verses and change a few words [^hull1912][^rch1927][^hymnarchive].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune in our arrangement, from a low C up to a high F, the widest range in the song. With two singers you have the tune and the alto. A third singer adds the bass, and the tenor makes four.

### What Bible verse is Be Thou My Vision based on?

None: it is an Irish prayer, not a setting of one passage, but its pictures come from Scripture. Hull's "my high tower" is the King James wording of Psalm 18:2, "my buckler, and the horn of my salvation, and my high tower", and her battle-shield and sword recall the armour of God in Ephesians 6, "the shield of faith" and "the sword of the Spirit" [^hymnarchive][^bible]. Hymnary.org's index also links it to Proverbs 29:18, "Where there is no vision, the people perish" [^hymnary-text][^bible].
