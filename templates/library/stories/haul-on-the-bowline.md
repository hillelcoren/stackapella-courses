Haul on the Bowline, also printed as "Haul the Bowline" and "We'll Haul the Bowline", is a sea shanty: a work song sailors sang to pull together on a rope [^onceaweek][^alden][^terry]. The shantyman sings a line, the crew answers "Haul on the bowline, the bowline haul", and everyone pulls at once on the last word, often shouting it [^onceaweek][^smith][^bullen][^terry]. Its chorus was in print by 1868, and writers on shanties have called it one of the oldest of them all, though that claim rests on no early record [^onceaweek][^whall][^colcord]. The version we sing is the one John Short, an old shantyman of Watchet in Somerset, sang to Cecil Sharp in 1914 [^sharpscan][^csp].

## Where it comes from

**The earliest printing we found.** On 1 August 1868 the London weekly *Once a Week* printed an unsigned article, "On Shanties" [^onceaweek]. For heavy work or few hands, the writer says, a hauling song of longer meter is used, and he gives its words: "Haul the bowline, the fore and main-top bowline, / Haul the bowline, the bowline haul", then "Kitty you're my darling". The last word, "haul", told the men when to pull [^onceaweek]. The Traditional Ballad Index also dates the song from this article [^balladindex].

![[1]]

**The earliest tune.** In July 1882 William L. Alden printed "the Bowline" with its tune in *Harper's New Monthly Magazine* in New York [^alden]. He set the scene on a Liverpool packet ship thirty years before: the topsail yard will not rise until someone calls for "the Bowline" and the men haul on its last syllable. He judged it, like Haul Away, Joe beside it, purely nautical and "undoubtedly very old" [^alden]. Laura Alexandrine Smith printed the same lines with a tune in 1888 [^smith].

![[2]]

**John Short and the page we read.** John Short was baptised in Watchet in April 1839 and died in 1933 [^csp][^halsway]. He spent more than fifty years in sailing ships, mostly as a chanteyman, the solo singer who led the work, and was by then the town crier [^sharp][^csp]. Sharp began visiting him in April 1914, and 46 of the 60 shanties in his *English Folk-Chanteys*, published that year, are Short's [^sharp][^csp][^halsway]. Haul on the Bowline is No. XXXVII, among the pulling chanteys, on pages 42 and 43, in C major with a piano part [^sharpscan]. It opens "Solo. (Introduction.)": the chanteyman sings the whole chorus alone, as Sharp says chanteymen commonly did, to name the chantey and remind the crew of the tune [^sharpscan][^sharp]. Two verses and the full chorus follow. An arrowhead over the last "haul" of each chorus marks the pull, with none over the introduction, when nobody is pulling yet [^sharpscan]. His note on page 71 points to other printings by Tozer, Bullen, Bradford and Fagge, and Smith [^sharpscan].

![[3]]

**Where shanties came from.** Black sailors and dockworkers were central to the shanty: a Library of Congress folklorist writes that the most valued singer on board was often a Black sailor, and Joanna Colcord traced a whole class of shanties to Black stevedores stowing cotton in the Gulf ports [^winick][^colcord]. Steam ended the shanty's working life: Alden heard the steam winch where the pulling song had been, and Sharp wrote that steam had dealt the chantey its death-blow [^alden][^sharp].

**Myth and record.** Often told: it is the oldest shanty of all, sung under Henry VIII. What the record shows: the earliest source we found for that date is John Masefield's *A Sailor's Garland* (1906), which gives no evidence for it, and Colcord repeated it [^masefield][^colcord]. W. B. Whall, who thought it possibly one of the oldest because the bowline was once a ship's most important rope, pointed to the sailors' cries in *The Complaynt of Scotland*, which he and Sharp dated about 1450 [^whall][^sharp]. That book is a century younger: its 1872 editor dated it to 1549, and an 1897 naval history to 1548 [^murray][^clowes]. In it, sailors hauling out the mainsail bowline answer one man's cry with "boulena, boulena", a work cry, not this chorus [^murray][^clowes]. The order is old; the song, as far as print shows, begins in 1868.

### Timeline

- About 1549: *The Complaynt of Scotland* records sailors crying "boulena" as they haul the bowline, a work cry, not this shanty [^murray][^clowes]

## What the words mean

**The bowline.** Here it is a rope, not the knot. *The Sailor's Word-Book* of 1867 describes a rope leading forward from the edge of a square sail, keeping its windward edge tight when the ship sails close to the wind; the bowline knot, in the same book, once fastened it to the sail [^smyth]. Sailors said "bo-lin", Colcord notes, and Bullen and Terry print it "Bowlin'" [^colcord][^bullen][^terry].

**"The bowline haul."** The same book defines it as "A hearty and simultaneous bowse": a pull by everyone at once, the leading man singing out "one, two, three" and the men throwing in their whole weight on the last [^smyth]. The chorus ends on "haul", and that is where the crew pulled [^onceaweek][^sharpscan].

**Kitty.** She is already in the 1868 lines, and John Short sings "O Kitty you are my darling" [^onceaweek][^sharpscan]. Other singers had her living in Liverpool [^colcord]. No early source says who she was.

**The verses.** "Because she had a fore-top, fore and main to bow-line" reads more like a tally of the ship than a sentence. A top is the platform at the head of a lower mast, and the mizen is a ship's aftermost mast [^smyth]. The shantyman's lines were his own, made up as he went or drawn from stock lines; only the chorus was fixed [^onceaweek][^sharp].

### The full words

These are all the words of No. XXXVII in Cecil Sharp's *English Folk-Chanteys* (1914), pages 42 and 43, as John Short sang them, with Sharp's spelling [^sharpscan]. Sharp wrote that he changed his singers' words only by cutting a few lines and softening two or three expressions [^sharp]. The words are traditional, printed in 1914, and Sharp died in 1924, so they are in the public domain [^csp][^hymnary].

**The call**
Haul on the bow-line, O Kitty you are my darling,
Haul on the bow-line, the bow-line, haul.

**Verse 1**
Because she had a fore-top, fore and main to bow-line;
Haul on the bow-line, the bow-line haul.

**Verse 2**
Because she had a main-top main and mizen to bow-line;
Haul the bow-line, the bow-line haul.

**Chorus**
Haul on the bow-line, O Kitty you are my darling,
Haul on the bow-line, the bow-line haul.

## How it is sung

It is a short-drag shanty, for jobs that needed a few hard pulls, one to each chorus, rather than the steady work of the capstan or the longer hauls of the halyards [^colcord][^winick][^sharpscan]. The books agree on the pull and disagree about the rope.

![[4]]

- **The sheets.** Bullen filed it among his "fore sheet Chanties", always sung, he says, when hauling that rope "with one collective drag", and says it was used continually for the fore and main sheet; Masefield filed it among the sheet, tack and bowline chanties, and Colcord had it sheeting home the fore and main sails [^bullen][^masefield][^colcord]. Smith has it hauling the tacks and sheets taut after the ship turns through the wind [^smith].
- **Sweating up.** Richard Terry, who learned it from Captain John Runciman, called it the most popular shanty for sweating up, the last few very strong pulls that set a rope tight [^terry][^sharp].
- **Halyards.** Alden's scene has the men hoisting a topsail yard [^alden].
- **The bowline itself.** Bullen thought the name an anomaly, since by his day the bowline was a light rope that never needed a shanty [^bullen]. Colcord thought that on Elizabethan ships "bowline" meant what was later called the fore-sheet [^colcord].

The last word was a pull, not a note. Bullen marks it "(shout)", cut off as short as possible, and Terry writes that it was always shouted [^bullen][^terry]. Bullen marks his version "Briskly", while Masefield describes a slow tune, with the crew falling back on the rope at its end [^bullen][^masefield].

Our arrangement is read from Sharp's page, a tone higher, in D, on the harmony of his piano part, so the harmony is Sharp's, not the ships' [^sharpscan]. It sings every word he prints, for 2 to 4 singers: the soprano, the shantyman, sings the call and each verse's first line alone, and everyone joins each verse's last line and the closing chorus, landing together on the last "haul", where Sharp marks the pull. As Sharp prints it, that "haul" is sung, not shouted [^sharpscan].

## Versions and other words

- **"So early in the morning."** Alden, Smith and Terry give the shantyman "We'll haul the bowline, so early in the morning" [^alden][^smith][^terry].
- **Other lines.** Smith's second version has the packet rolling and the captain growling, Terry adds the fore-topgallant bowline, and Smith's introduction quotes "the bully, bully bowline" [^smith][^terry]. Colcord's American shantyman sings of payday being a long way off [^colcord].

## Did you know?

- In a medieval poem about seasick pilgrims sailing to the shrine of St James, the orders shouted on deck include "Hale the bowelyne!" Its Victorian editors dated the Cambridge manuscript to Henry VI's reign; a later catalogue dates it to the late 1400s or early 1500s [^wright][^furnivall][^arlima].
- Terry heard from Sir Walter Runciman of about thirty apprentices, gathered on one ship while their crews were ashore, who hoisted a topsail for fun, sang "Haul the Bowlin'" to sweat it up, and kept on until the topsail yard broke in two [^terry].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/haul-on-the-bowline/soprano): you are the shantyman. You sing the call alone, reaching your top E on its first "bowline" and turning quickly through B, C-sharp, B and A on "darling". Each verse climbs from your low D on "Because" to the D an octave up on "foretop" and "maintop", and every "haul" lands on that high D.
- [Alto](/songs/haul-on-the-bowline/alto): you rest through the call and the solo lines, then join each verse's last line and the whole last chorus, mostly on F-sharp and G. Your top A comes only once, on "bowline, O" in the last chorus, and you drop to D for "are my darling".
- [Tenor](/songs/haul-on-the-bowline/tenor): you rest through the call and the solo lines, then sing mostly on A and D. You open verse 1's answer on your low F-sharp, "Haul on the", and reach your top E once, on the last "bowline" before the final "haul".
- [Bass](/songs/haul-on-the-bowline/bass): you rest through the call and the solo lines, then sing mostly on B, then A and D. You dip to your low G on "darling" in the last chorus, rise to F-sharp on "bowline, the" in its last line, and land every "haul" on D: that is the pull.

## Questions

### What does "haul on the bowline" mean?

It means pull on the bowline, a rope that kept the forward edge of a square sail tight when a ship sailed close to the wind [^smyth]. A "bowline haul" was a hard pull by everyone at once, and in the shanty the crew pulled on the last word, "haul" [^smyth][^onceaweek]. In practice it was sung on other ropes, mostly the fore and main sheets [^bullen][^colcord].

### What are the original lyrics of Haul on the Bowline?

There is no single original: the chorus was fixed and the shantyman made up his own lines [^onceaweek][^sharp]. The earliest words we found, printed in London in 1868, are "Haul the bowline, the fore and main-top bowline, Haul the bowline, the bowline haul", with a second line about Kitty [^onceaweek]. The words we sing are John Short's, as Cecil Sharp printed them in 1914 [^sharpscan].

### Is it the same song as "Haul Away the Bowline"?

Yes. The Traditional Ballad Index lists "Haul Away the Bowline" as another title of this shanty, and gives the Greig-Duncan collection from Aberdeenshire's text as "Haul Away Your Bowline" [^balladindex]. Other printings call it "Haul the Bowline" or "We'll Haul the Bowline" [^onceaweek][^alden][^terry]. It is not Haul Away, Joe, a different pulling song that Alden printed beside it in 1882 [^alden].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune. In our arrangement the soprano is the shantyman, singing the opening call and each verse's first line alone; the alto, tenor and bass join each verse's last line and the closing chorus on the harmony of Sharp's 1914 piano part [^sharpscan].
