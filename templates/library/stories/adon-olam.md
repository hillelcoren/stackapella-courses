Adon Olam, "Lord of the world", is a Hebrew hymn about Hashem, who reigned before anything was made and will reign alone when everything is gone.[^sefaria] It stands at both ends of the morning prayers. It is printed at the start of the daily service, and at the close of the Sabbath and festival morning service many congregations sing it together.[^je][^ej][^posen] Its last lines put the singer's spirit into Hashem's hand, "when I sleep and when I wake", so it is a night prayer too: it is said at bedtime and beside the dying.[^abrahams][^ej][^je]

## Where it comes from

**The words.** Nobody knows who wrote Adon Olam, or when. The Jewish Encyclopedia of 1901 says only that its date and author are unknown.[^je][^etzion] It is traditionally credited to Solomon ibn Gabirol, the Spanish Jewish poet of the eleventh century,[^jegab] and some scholars have held that he wrote it, but no record proves it.[^abrahams][^ej][^etzion] Israel Abrahams, in his companion to the English prayer book, set it in the age of the Geonim, the leading Jewish authorities of the East in the Middle Ages, and noted that it had been credited to several of them as well as to Gabirol. The Encyclopaedia Judaica allows that it may be much older than Gabirol and come from Babylonia.[^abrahams][^ej]

What is certain is its form. The Jewish Encyclopedia calls it "one of the few strictly metrical hymns in the Jewish liturgy", and its syllables fall in a steady pattern, one short followed by three long.[^je][^ej][^abrahams] Every line ends on the same sound, -ra: nivra, nikra, nora, and on to the last word, ira.[^abrahams][^sefaria] Prayer books do not agree on its length. Ashkenazi prayer books print ten lines, as the Jewish Encyclopedia counts them, though the Encyclopaedia Judaica says twelve. Sephardi books have more: two or more extra lines in Abrahams's count, twelve in the Jewish Encyclopedia's and sixteen in the Encyclopaedia Judaica's.[^je][^abrahams][^ej][^etzion][^sefaria]

**The tune.** Eliezer Gerovitsch was born in Ukraine in 1844. He studied music in Berdichev, in Odessa and at the conservatory in St Petersburg, and in 1887 he became cantor in Rostov-on-Don, where he served for twenty-five years.[^ejger][^huji] The Encyclopaedia Judaica says his synagogue music grew from traditional Jewish melodies.[^ejger] He died in 1913 by the Encyclopaedia Judaica's account; the Hebrew University's Jewish Music Research Centre and the European Institute of Jewish Music give 1914.[^ejger][^huji][^iemj]

![[1]]

His Shirei Tefilah (Sinagogen-Gesänge), songs for the synagogue, was printed in Rostov, and the edition we read from, which Zemereshet calls the second, is dated 1897.[^ubffm][^zemer] Its second part, for the Sabbath and the three pilgrim festivals, has Adon Olam as No. 16, on pages 89 and 90.[^ubffm][^zemer] It opens with an eight-bar strain in D minor for four voices, soprano, alto, tenor and bass, and in its heading Gerovitsch wrote "A. W.", for alte Weise, "old tune". After the strain the setting goes on in music that seems to be his own, beginning with a solo recitative on the tenor's staff, marked "K. Recit.", most likely for the cantor.[^ubffm]

![[2]]

**Myth and record.** Often told: the tune is Gerovitsch's own, and Zemereshet, the Israeli song archive, notes that it is sometimes credited to him. What the record shows: his own book marks the setting "A. W.", an old tune, so he harmonised a melody already sung. Who first sang it is not known.[^zemer] Zemereshet adds that the form most people sing today is simpler than the one he wrote down.[^zemer]

### Timeline

- 11th century: Solomon ibn Gabirol, to whom tradition credits the hymn, lives in Spain. [^abrahams][^etzion][^jegab]
- 1622 to 1623: Salamone Rossi prints an eight-voice setting in Venice, in his Songs of Solomon. [^ej][^imslp][^musicweb]
- 1844: Eliezer Gerovitsch is born in Ukraine. [^ejger][^huji]
- 1887: Gerovitsch becomes cantor in Rostov-on-Don. [^ejger][^huji]
- 1897: an edition of his Shirei Tefilah dated this year, printed in Rostov, heads its Adon Olam "A. W.", an old tune. [^ubffm][^zemer]
- 1913 or 1914: Gerovitsch dies; the sources differ on the year. [^ejger][^huji]
- 1976: Uzi Hitman writes a new tune for the eighth Hasidic Song Festival; Zemereshet calls it the most widespread tune of the last generation. [^zemer][^ice][^fau]

## What the words mean

The hymn moves from Hashem beyond time to the singer's own trust: Abrahams saw its charm in joining the faith's great doctrines to simple thoughts of the heart.[^abrahams] Here is each of the ten lines in plain words, as the Ashkenazi prayer book prints them.[^sefaria]

**Line 1 (sung).** Lord of the world, who was King before anything at all was formed. Adon is "lord", and olam is "world", a word that also means "forever". Malach is "reigned".

**Line 2 (sung).** When everything was made by His will, then He was called King.

**Line 3 (sung).** And when everything comes to an end, He alone, the awesome One, will reign.

**Line 4 (sung).** He was, He is, and He will be, in glory. Tifara is "glory" or "splendour".

**Line 5 (sung).** He is One, and there is no second to compare with Him or set beside Him.

**Line 6 (sung).** Without beginning, without end; strength and rule are His. Misra is "dominion".

**Line 7 (not sung).** He is Hashem, my living redeemer, my rock in my pain, in a time of trouble.

**Line 8 (not sung).** He is my banner and my refuge, my portion on the day I call.

**Line 9 (sung).** Into His hand I entrust my spirit, when I sleep and when I wake. Afkid is "I entrust", ruchi "my spirit". They echo Psalm 31, "Into Thy hand I commit my spirit".[^ps31]

**Line 10 (sung).** And with my spirit, my body too. Hashem is with me; I will not fear. Geviyati is "my body". The last words echo Psalm 118:6, where the psalmist, with Hashem on his side, says he will not fear.[^ps118][^barth]

In line 10 the Hebrew has the four-letter name, and we sing "Adoshem li", the substituted name customary when singing outside prayer. Many Jews change Hashem's names when they say them outside prayer, out of reverence and to avoid taking the name in vain, and a practice song is outside prayer.[^ohr][^outorah]

Gerovitsch printed the words in the Ashkenazi pronunciation, "Adon olom ascher molach".[^ubffm] We sing them in modern Israeli pronunciation, on the same notes.

### The full words

These are the ten lines as the Ashkenazi prayer book prints them, in the Hebrew of the Metsudah Siddur on Sefaria, each line split at its comma.[^sefaria] The Hebrew and the transliteration both give Hashem's names in the substitute forms customary when singing outside prayer: Keli in line 7 and Adoshem in line 10.

**Lines 1 and 2**
> אֲדוֹן עוֹלָם אֲשֶׁר מָלַךְ,
*Adon olam asher malach,*
Lord of the world, who was King
> בְּטֶֽרֶם כָּל יְצִיר נִבְרָא:
*b'terem kol yetzir nivra.*
before anything at all was formed.
> לְעֵת נַעֲשָׂה בְחֶפְצוֹ כֹּל,
*L'et naasa vecheftzo kol,*
When everything was made by His will,
> אֲזַי מֶֽלֶךְ שְׁמוֹ נִקְרָא:
*azai melech shemo nikra.*
then He was called King.

**Lines 3 and 4**
> וְאַחֲרֵי כִּכְלוֹת הַכֹּל,
*Veacharei kichlot hakol,*
And when everything comes to an end,
> לְבַדּוֹ יִמְלֹךְ נוֹרָא:
*levado yimloch nora.*
He alone, the awesome One, will reign.
> וְהוּא הָיָה וְהוּא הֹוֶה,
*Vehu haya vehu hove,*
He was, He is,
> וְהוּא יִהְיֶה בְּתִפְאָרָה:
*vehu yihye betifara.*
and He will be, in glory.

**Lines 5 and 6**
> וְהוּא אֶחָד וְאֵין שֵׁנִי,
*Vehu echad veein sheni*
He is One, and there is no second
> לְהַמְשִׁיל לוֹ לְהַחְבִּֽירָה:
*lehamshilo lehachbira.*
to compare with Him or set beside Him.
> בְּלִי רֵאשִׁית בְּלִי תַכְלִית,
*Beli reshit beli tachlit,*
Without beginning, without end;
> וְלוֹ הָעֹז וְהַמִּשְׂרָה:
*velo haoz vehamisra.*
strength and rule are His.

**Lines 7 and 8 (not sung)**
> וְהוּא קֵלִי וְחַי גּוֹאֲלִי,
*Vehu Keli vechai goali,*
He is Hashem, my living redeemer,
> וְצוּר חֶבְלִי בְּעֵת צָרָה:
*vetzur chevli b'et tzara.*
my rock in my pain, in a time of trouble.
> וְהוּא נִסִּי וּמָנוֹס לִי,
*Vehu nisi umanos li,*
He is my banner and my refuge,
> מְנָת כּוֹסִי בְּיוֹם אֶקְרָא:
*menat kosi beyom ekra.*
my portion on the day I call.

**Lines 9 and 10**
> בְּיָדוֹ אַפְקִיד רוּחִי,
*Beyado afkid ruchi*
Into His hand I entrust my spirit
> בְּעֵת אִישַׁן וְאָעִֽירָה:
*b'et ishan veaira.*
when I sleep and when I wake.
> וְעִם רוּחִי גְּוִיָּתִי,
*Veim ruchi geviyati,*
And with my spirit, my body too.
> יְיָ לִי וְלֹא אִירָא:
*Adoshem li velo ira.*
Hashem is with me; I will not fear.

## How it is sung

Adon Olam has two homes in the service. It is printed at the start of the daily morning prayers, and it is best known at the end.[^je][^ej][^sefaria] In 1901 the Jewish Encyclopedia reported that Sephardi Jews, and British synagogues generally, sang it all together at the close of the Sabbath and festival morning services, while Ashkenazi Jews often sang it at the close of the evening service in place of the hymn Yigdal.[^je] Today, the Encyclopaedia Judaica says, its main place is at the close of the Musaf, the additional service that ends the Sabbath and festival morning.[^ej] The Jewish Encyclopedia found it chanted almost everywhere on the eve of Yom Kippur, and the Encyclopaedia Judaica places it at the close of the Kol Nidre service that evening.[^je][^ej][^abrahams]

![[3]]

It also belongs to the night. Because line 9 speaks of sleeping and waking, the scholar Seligman Baer thought it was first meant to be said before going to sleep, and Abrahams thought that very likely. The Jewish Encyclopedia was not so sure: perhaps, it said, the hymn's beauty and grandeur brought it into the service, at its start or its close.[^je][^abrahams] It stands among the prayers said at bedtime, and it is said at the bedside of the dying.[^ej][^barth][^abrahams]

Our arrangement is read from Gerovitsch's page, in D minor as printed, in 4/4. We sing only the old strain, not the music that follows it in his book. The strain carries two lines of the poem, each line two phrases of the tune, and it is sung four times: lines 1 and 2, 3 and 4, 5 and 6, and 9 and 10. At the end of each time through, the tune's low D is held before a breath. Bars 1 and 2 and 5 to 8 keep his chords and bass, with small changes for beginners; bars 3 and 4, where his tenor's quick notes were hard to read in the first photograph we worked from, are our own voicing over his chords. With two singers it is soprano and alto, with three the bass joins, and with four the tenor.

## Versions and other words

**Other tunes.** Adon Olam has a great number of melodies, some written for it and some borrowed, Jewish and not; its simple, regular metre fits them, and American congregations often sing it to popular tunes, "Rock Around the Clock" among them.[^ej][^posen] Yet in 1901 the Jewish Encyclopedia found the traditional tunes few, only four or five, the oldest seeming to be a short melody of Spanish origin.[^je]

![[4]]

Salamone Rossi included a setting for eight voices in his collection of Hebrew songs for the synagogue, printed in Venice in 1622 or 1623.[^ej][^imslp][^musicweb] In 1976 Uzi Hitman wrote the tune that Zemereshet calls the most widespread of the last generation.[^zemer][^ice]

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/adon-olam/soprano): the old tune, most often on A, then D. Once each time through it leaps up to its top E, on the "rem" of "b'terem", the "va-" of "levado", the "ham-" of "lehamshilo" and the "i-" of "ishan", just after the second phrase begins, and it walks down to its low D at the end of the first and last phrases each time through.
- [Alto](/songs/adon-olam/alto): mostly a third or a sixth under the tune. Listen at the end of the first and last phrases, on "malach," or "nikra.": the tune falls F, E, D while the alto climbs D, E, F, so the two cross and the alto ends a third above it. Solo the alto in the player to hear it.
- [Tenor](/songs/adon-olam/tenor): the middle of the chord, most often on A, then C. It opens each time through on its top D, on "Adon olam", "Veacharei", "Vehu echad" and "Beyado", and sinks to its low F in the third phrase, on the "sa" of "naasa" and the "ve-" of "vecheftzo" the first time.
- [Bass](/songs/adon-olam/bass): the bass line, most often on D, then A. It reaches its top F where the second phrase begins, on the "b'te-" of "b'terem", the "le-" of "levado" and of "lehamshilo", and "b'et", and goes down to its low F, an octave under it, in the last phrase, on the "lech" of "melech", the "ye" of "yihye", the "oz" of "haoz" and "li".

## Questions

### Who wrote Adon Olam?

Nobody knows. The hymn is traditionally credited to Solomon ibn Gabirol, the eleventh-century Spanish poet, but the Jewish Encyclopedia of 1901 calls its author and date unknown. Israel Abrahams placed it in the age of the Geonim, and the Encyclopaedia Judaica allows that it may be older than Gabirol and come from Babylonia.[^je][^abrahams][^ej][^etzion]

### What is the history of Adon Olam?

It is an anonymous medieval Hebrew hymn. Tradition credits Solomon ibn Gabirol, of eleventh-century Spain, but no record proves it, and some scholars think it older.[^je][^abrahams][^ej][^etzion] It is first found in prayer books of the Ashkenazi, or German, rite in the fourteenth century.[^ej][^etzion] Its line on sleeping and waking led scholars to think it began as a bedtime prayer.[^je][^abrahams] By the end of that century prayer books also set it at the start of the morning service and at the close of the Sabbath and festival Musaf, where it is best known today.[^etzion][^ej]

### Why is Adon Olam sung to so many tunes?

Because its lines are short and strictly regular, many melodies fit them, as the Posen Library explains. The Encyclopaedia Judaica counts a great number of tunes, some written for it and some borrowed from other songs, Jewish and not. Yet in 1901 the Jewish Encyclopedia found that only four or five "deserve to be called traditional".[^posen][^ej][^je]

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the old tune all the way through, four times, once for each pair of lines. With two singers the alto sings under it, mostly a third or a sixth below, crossing above it at the ends of the first and last phrases. With three the bass joins, and with four the tenor fills in the middle of each chord.
