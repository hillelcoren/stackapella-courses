London's Burning is an English round sung in schools and at community sings: four short lines, "London's burning", "Fetch the engines", "Fire! Fire!" and "Pour on water", sung by voices starting one after another [^goss1927][^pentregwyn1909]. In America and in older books it is Scotland's Burning [^vinton1816][^parley1837][^ballad]. It is often said to be about the Great Fire of London of 1666 [^londonmuseum]. The record points elsewhere: a roll of rounds dated 1580, 86 years before that fire, has a round that begins "Scotland, it burneth" and ends "Cast on more water" [^lindahl][^londonmuseum]. The London words we found are in print by 1909, and "Fetch the engines" by 1927 [^pentregwyn1909][^goss1927]. No one knows who made it [^ballad].

## Where it comes from

**A roll of 1580.** At King's College, Cambridge, a roll of rounds known as the Lant manuscript is dated 1580 [^lindahl][^lindahl-songbook][^londonmuseum]. One of its rounds has four short lines: "Scotland, it burneth, / Look out, look out, / Fire fire, fire fire, / Cast on more water" [^lindahl]. It is the earliest known round on this theme, and it is about Scotland, not London [^lindahl][^londonmuseum]. Pammelia, the London book of rounds of 1609 credited to Thomas Ravenscroft, has no fire round in the table of its 100 songs [^pammelia1609].

**A joke in Shakespeare.** In The Taming of the Shrew, as printed in the 1623 collection of his plays, the servant Grumio arrives half frozen, calls "A fire good Curtis", and, asked whether his master and the new wife are coming, answers yes, "and therefore fire, fire, cast on no water" [^folio1623][^shrew]. In 1780 a note in a supplement to Johnson and Steevens's edition explained the line by quoting "an old popular catch of three parts": "Scotland burneth, Scotland burneth. Fire, fire; Fire, fire; Cast on some more water." That note is signed only "E."; the editions of 1785 and 1788 print the same note over the name Blackstone [^supp1780][^shakes1785][^bell1788]. A catch is a round, and Grumio turns its cry upside down: he wants a fire lit, not put out [^webster-catch][^folio1623].

**Printed with music, in America.** The earliest printing with music we found is in David Vinton's The Masonick Minstrel, a songbook for Freemasons printed at Dedham, Massachusetts, in 1816 [^vinton1816]. On page 21, after a Masonic song, is "Scotland's Burning! Canon." in 3/4, four numbered lines: "Scotland's burning, Scotland's burning", "Look out, Look out", "Fire! Fire!" twice (the second pair partly lost), and a fourth line that begins "Cast on", the rest lost to damage in the copy we read [^vinton1816]. In 1837 the Boston music teacher Lowell Mason supplied "Round. Scotland's Burning." to Parley's Magazine for its page of juvenile music, in 4/4, its last line now "Pour on water!" [^parley1837]. Even in London, an index to the music in John Curwen's Standard Course, a sol-fa singing course for classes, printed in a book of his of 1861, lists "Scotland's burning" among its rounds, on page 18 [^curwen1861]. The Ballad Index finds the Scotland round in the United States and in Canada's Maritimes [^ballad].

**London's turn.** The earliest London words we found are in a Welsh book of 1909, Y Pentre Gwyn, "a story of early life", by the writer who signed himself Anthropos, Robert David Rowland, born about 1853, looking back on his schooldays [^pentregwyn1909][^dwb-rowland]. In its chapter on the British School, village and town children sang a round in turns, one side starting the first line as the other reached the second, and "one of the pieces sung with relish" was "London's burning! Look yonder! Fire! Fire! Pour on water!" [^pentregwyn1909]. In James Joyce's Ulysses (Paris, 1922), Leopold Bloom, seeing a glow in the sky, hums "London's burning, London's burning! On fire, on fire!" [^ulysses1922]. The earliest printing we found of "Fetch the engines" is our source, John Goss's book of 1927, which names no one for the line; who made it is not known [^goss1927].

**The book we sing from.** The "Daily Express" Community Song Book was "collected and edited by John Goss" and published in London in 1927 [^goss1927]. Its foreword says the paper's community singing movement began on 20 November 1926, when ten thousand people met in the Albert Hall [^goss1927]. The book's editor was alive when it appeared: he signed its note "John Goss, Lancaster Gate, February, 1927", so he is not the church composer Sir John Goss, who died in 1880 [^goss1927]. He was the baritone John Goss, known for sea shanties, who led the Daily Express community singing and compiled the book [^mc1928][^goss-cdnenc]. Sources agree he died in 1953, but one gives his birth as 1894 and another as 1891 [^goss-cdnenc][^goss-rah]. London's Burning is on page 195, among its fifteen "Rounds and Canons", with no composer named [^goss1927].

**Myth and record.** Often told: London's Burning is about the Great Fire of London [^londonmuseum]. That fire began on 2 September 1666 in a baker's house in Pudding Lane [^pepys][^londonmuseum]. What the record shows: the earliest known version of the round is about Scotland and is dated 1580, before the fire; the printings of 1780, 1816, 1837 and 1861 still have Scotland; and the earliest London words we found are in a Welsh book of 1909, whose writer, born about 1853, remembers singing them at school [^lindahl][^supp1780][^vinton1816][^parley1837][^curwen1861][^pentregwyn1909][^dwb-rowland]. Londoners may have sung it of their own city earlier still, but no source we read shows it was made about 1666. London Museum adds that the Scottish words are said to be about the English army's burning of Edinburgh in 1544 [^londonmuseum][^scottishhistory]. No source we read gives evidence for it, and the Ballad Index, doubting readings that tie the round to Scotland's political crises, calls it just a round [^ballad].

## What the words mean

1. **"London's burning, London's burning."** A fire has broken out, and the singer shouts the alarm. Older versions have Scotland burning [^lindahl][^vinton1816].
2. **"Fetch the engines, fetch the engines."** Go and get the fire engines. A fire engine was a portable pump, usually on wheels, for throwing water on a fire; in 1666 London had engines and hand squirts, but no fire brigade [^webster-fire][^londonmuseum]. Older versions sing "Look out" (1580, 1816, 1837) or "Look yonder", that is, look over there [^lindahl][^vinton1816][^parley1837][^pentregwyn1909][^webster-yonder].
3. **"Fire! Fire! Fire! Fire!"** The cry itself, on the highest notes of the tune. Goss marks three of the four with accents [^goss1927].
4. **"Pour on water, pour on water."** Put the fire out. The roll of 1580 and the note of 1780 say "Cast on (some) more water": to cast is to throw [^lindahl][^supp1780][^webster-cast]. "Pour on water" is in print by 1837 [^parley1837].

So the round is the whole story of a fire in four lines: the alarm, the call for help, the shout, and the water. Sung as a round, all four happen at once.

### The full words

These are the words as printed on page 195 of the "Daily Express" Community Song Book (London, 1927), collected and edited by John Goss, who died in 1953. The words are traditional, and "Fetch the engines", printed here, has no known maker. The book was printed before 1931 by an editor who died more than 70 years ago, so the words are in the public domain.

London's burning, London's burning,
Fetch the engines, fetch the engines,
Fire! Fire! Fire! Fire!
Pour on water, pour on water.

## How it is sung

**A round.** Every voice sings the same tune, starting one line apart. Goss's page numbers its four lines 1 to 4 at the left, for the entries, and 4, 3, 2, 1 at their right-hand ends, and after the last line prints "Back to beginning" [^goss1927]. Vinton's page of 1816 numbers the line ends 2, 3, 4 and 1, and the book's directions for catches explain such numbers: a singer who reaches the end of line 1 goes on to line 2 as the next singer starts line 1, and all keep going until the signal is given to stop [^vinton1816].

**What our arrangement sings.** Our round is Goss's, in 3/4, written a whole tone lower, in F instead of G. The soprano starts alone and sings the tune three times through. The alto comes in two bars later at the same pitch, then the tenor and the bass, each two bars after the last, an octave lower. We read Goss's numbers at the line ends, 4, 3, 2, 1, as where each voice stops, so all four stop together on one chord.

## Did you know?

- On the same page of the 1623 Shakespeare, Grumio also says "Why Iacke boy, ho boy"; a note signed Sir John Hawkins, printed in an edition of 1788, calls it the beginning of an old round in three parts, and Pammelia (1609) lists "Iacke boy, ho boy" as its song No. 56 [^folio1623][^bell1788][^pammelia1609].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/londons-burning/soprano): you start alone and sing the tune three times through. You go up to the top C on every "Fire!" and every "pour", and down to the C an octave below on every "London's". The tune has no rests, so snatch your breaths between lines.
- [Alto](/songs/londons-burning/alto): the same tune at the same pitch, two bars after the soprano: nothing new to learn. The test is holding your place while the soprano shouts "Fire!" over your "Fetch the engines". You stop with everyone on your last "Fire!".
- [Tenor](/songs/londons-burning/tenor): the same tune an octave lower, two bars after the alto. You stop with everyone on "engines", at the end of the second line, not the last, so count your way there.
- [Bass](/songs/londons-burning/bass): the tenor's low tune, coming in last, two bars after it; from your entry all four lines sound at once. You stop with everyone on "burning", having sung the first line one last time.

## Questions

### Is London's Burning about the Great Fire of London?

Not as far as the record shows. It is often said to be [^londonmuseum], but the earliest known version, on a roll of rounds dated 1580, sings "Scotland, it burneth", 86 years before the fire of 1666 [^lindahl][^londonmuseum]. Printings of 1780, 1816 and 1837 still have Scotland, and the London words we found are in print by 1909 [^supp1780][^vinton1816][^parley1837][^pentregwyn1909]. No source shows it was made about that fire.

### Who wrote London's Burning, and when?

No one knows. The Ballad Index gives its author as unknown [^ballad]. Its earliest known version is on the Lant roll, dated 1580, sung about Scotland [^lindahl]. The earliest printing with music we found is from 1816, in Massachusetts [^vinton1816]. Our source, John Goss's book of 1927, the earliest printing we found of the line "Fetch the engines", names no maker for the round or the line, though it names Matthew White for the next round on its page [^goss1927].

### How do you sing London's Burning as a round?

Everyone sings the same four lines, starting one line apart. Goss's page numbers the lines 1 to 4: when the first singer reaches line 2, "Fetch the engines", the second starts line 1, and so on, up to four voices, each going back to the beginning at the end [^goss1927]. Our arrangement has the voices enter two bars apart and stop together.

### Which part sings the tune?

Everyone does. In a round there is no separate harmony part: the harmony comes from the tune sounding against itself. Goss prints one line of melody for all [^goss1927]. In our arrangement the soprano and alto sing it at the same pitch, and the tenor and bass sing it an octave lower.
