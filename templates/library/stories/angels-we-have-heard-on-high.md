Angels We Have Heard on High is a French Christmas carol, Les anges dans nos campagnes, "The angels in our countryside", sung in English with its refrain left in Latin: Gloria in excelsis Deo, "Glory to God in the highest", the angels' words to the shepherds in Luke's Gospel [^lamb1842][^hutchins][^vulgate-org]. Its French words were in print in Marseille by 1805, and words and tune together in Paris by 1842 [^roche1805][^lamb1842][^ha]. The English began with James Chadwick, a priest and later the Catholic bishop of Hexham and Newcastle, in a London hymn book of 1860 [^hfh1860][^julian-chadwick][^ce-chadwick]. Hymnal editors put its popularity down to its refrain: the first syllable, "Glo", runs down three bars of falling notes before the word is finished [^psh][^lamb1842][^hfh1860].

## Where it comes from

**The French words.** No one knows who wrote them. Their earliest known printing is in Noëls français et provençaux, a book of Christmas songs in French and Provençal. The copy at the University of Toronto has lost its title page, and a handwritten note inside its cover gives Marseille, the printer J. Mossy, the year 1805 and the name Roche [^roche1805][^ha]. Later editions from the same printer credit the book to "le R. P. Roche", Father Roche, a Recollect friar, and hymnologists think he may have gathered some of its songs rather than written them all [^bnf-roche1829][^ha]. On its pages 81 to 83 the carol is headed simply "NOEL" and set "sur l'air: Cherche-moi", to a tune the page does not print [^roche1805]. It opens "J'Entends, là sur ces collines, Les Anges descendus des Cieux", I hear, there on these hills, the angels come down from heaven, and it runs to twelve verses, the first ending "Gloria in excelsis Deo" and the rest "Gloria, etc." [^roche1805][^ha].

**The tune, and the carol we know.** In Paris in 1842 a book of church songs set to new tunes, Choix de cantiques sur des airs nouveaux, arranged for three or four voices with organ or piano by the Jesuit musician Louis Lambillotte, printed the carol as No. 3 [^lamb1842][^sibley1867][^ha]. Its title there is L'écho des montagnes de Béthléem, "The echo of the mountains of Bethlehem", "Noël languedocien", a Christmas song of Languedoc, and it gives the music as "Musique de W. M." [^lamb1842][^sibley1867]. Who W. M. was is not known [^ha]. It is the earliest known printing of the tune and of the opening "Les Anges, dans nos campagnes" [^lamb1842][^sommervogel].

![[1]]

**Chadwick's English.** James Chadwick, born in Drogheda, Ireland, in 1813, was a priest who taught at Ushaw College near Durham [^julian-chadwick][^ce-chadwick]. In 1860 Richardson and Son of London published The Holy Family Hymns, for the Society of the Holy Family, with Cardinal Wiseman's approval [^hfh1860]. Its contents page credits No. 9, "Christmas Hymn", to "Rev. J. Chadwick" [^hfh1860][^julian]. It begins "The Angels we have heard on high, Most sweetly singing o'er our plains", with an extra syllable at the start of each line, and its companion music book sets it to the French tune, adding a pickup note to carry that syllable [^hfh1860][^ha]. Chadwick became a bishop in 1866 and died in 1882 [^julian-chadwick][^ce-chadwick].

![[2]]

**The form we sing.** In 1862 The Crown of Jesus, a Catholic prayer book printed in London, gave the hymn as "Angels we have heard on high, Sweetly singing o'er our plains", in shorter lines that fit the tune without the extra note [^julian][^ha][^crown]. The book names no editor (library catalogues give its compilers as R. R. Suffield and C. F. R. Palmer), and who reworked the hymn is not known; the reworking also changed the fourth verse [^ha][^crown][^canterbury-crown]. Some hymnal handbooks credit the change to the Newcastle organist Henri Hemy, in a Crown of Jesus Music dated 1862, but Hemy's music for the book came out in 1864, two years after the words [^psh][^canterbury-hemy][^ha].

**Myth and record.** Often told: the refrain goes back to the year 129, when Pope Telesphorus ordered it sung at Christmas; or the carol is from eighteenth-century Languedoc [^deeproots][^psh]. What the record shows: a book of popes' lives, compiled some four hundred years after Telesphorus, credits him with night Masses at Christmas and the angels' hymn, Gloria in excelsis Deo, before the sacrifice [^loomis][^gloria-ce]. Historians treat its early decrees as later customs, and that hymn is the Gloria of the Mass, not this carol [^loomis][^ce-lp]. No copy older than 1805 is known; "Languedoc" comes from the 1842 subtitle [^roche1805][^ha][^lamb1842].

### Timeline

- 1805: "J'Entends, là sur ces collines", twelve verses with a Gloria refrain, in a book of noëls printed in Marseille [^roche1805][^ha]
- 1842: "Les Anges, dans nos campagnes" with its tune, in Paris, credited to W. M. [^lamb1842][^sommervogel]
- 1860: James Chadwick's "The Angels we have heard on high" in The Holy Family Hymns, London [^hfh1860][^julian]
- 1862: The Crown of Jesus prints "Angels we have heard on high", the opening sung today [^julian][^ha]
- 1891: Charles L. Hutchins prints the carol in four parts, with one Gloria, in his Boston weekly The Parish Choir [^parishchoir1891][^hutchins]
- 1916: Hutchins reprints the same page in Carols Old and Carols New, Boston [^hutchins]
- 1937: Edward Shippen Barnes harmonizes the tune for The New Church Hymnal, New York [^ha][^psh]

## What the words mean

Chadwick's English is a free version of the French, not a translation: it keeps the angels, the mountains' echo, the questions to the shepherds, the call to Bethlehem and the manger, in four verses to the 1842 French's ten [^hfh1860][^lamb1842].

**Verse 1.** Angels have been heard singing over the plains, and the mountains answer with an echo. That echo is the French carol's own picture: "l'écho de nos montagnes", the echo of our mountains, sings the angels' song back [^lamb1842][^hutchins].

**Verse 2.** The singer asks the shepherds what the celebration is for. A "jubilee" is a time of great public joy, and a "strain" is a song or a passage of a tune, so "why your rapturous strain prolong?" asks why their delighted singing goes on and on [^webster-jubilee][^webster-strain]. "Gladsome tidings" are glad news. The French asks the same: who is all this for, and what victory deserves such shouts? [^lamb1842]

**Verse 3.** The answer is an invitation: come to Bethlehem, see the child the angels sing about, and kneel to worship Christ the Lord, the newborn king [^hutchins].

**Verse 4.** The child lies in a manger, praised by the angels; the singers ask Mary and Joseph to help them as they offer their love [^hutchins].

### The French of 1842

These are the ten verses of Les anges dans nos campagnes as Choix de cantiques sur des airs nouveaux printed them in Paris in 1842, the first verse under the notes on page 8 and the rest on page 9 [^lamb1842]. Spelling and punctuation are the print's, except four misprints we have corrected: "sous" for "sons" in verse 5, "Où" for "Ou" and "redirons" for "rediront" in verse 6, and "Au" for "An" in verse 10. Nine of these verses are in the 1805 book too, in close to the same words; it lacks the first, opening instead with "J'Entends, là sur ces collines", and has two more of its own [^roche1805]. Under each French line is our own plain English.

### The full words

These are the four verses of Carol 181, "Angels we have heard", as Carols Old and Carols New (Boston: The Parish Choir, 1916) prints them, headed "Old French Carol" [^hutchins]. The book prints the refrain once under the first verse's notes and "Gloria in excelsis Deo" after each of the others. We sing verses 1 to 3. The Crown of Jesus differs in places, with "Echo still their joyous strains", "Say what may the tidings be", "Come to Bethlehem, come and see", "The Infant Christ, the new-born King" and a fourth verse that begins "See, within a manger laid" [^crown].

**Verse 1**
Angels we have heard on high,
Sweetly singing o'er our plains,
And the mountains in reply,
Echoing their joyous strains.
Gloria in excelsis Deo.

**Verse 2**
Shepherds, why this jubilee?
Why your rapturous strain prolong?
What the gladsome tidings be
Which inspire your heavenly song?
Gloria in excelsis Deo.

**Verse 3**
Come to Bethlehem, and see
Him whose birth the angels sing;
Come, adore on bended knee
Christ the Lord, the new-born King.
Gloria in excelsis Deo.

**Verse 4**
See Him in a manger laid,
Whom the choirs of angels praise;
Mary, Joseph, lend your aid,
While our hearts in love we raise.
Gloria in excelsis Deo.

## How it is sung

It is a Christmas carol, not an Advent song: it sings of the night of the birth, and Christmas is its season. Its English began in Catholic books, The Holy Family Hymns of 1860 and The Crown of Jesus of 1862, but other churches sing it as a hymn too; the Psalter Hymnal of the Christian Reformed Church in North America prints it at No. 347, to the tune GLORIA [^hfh1860][^crown][^psh]. Its one tie to Advent is the Mass's Gloria, left out on the Sundays of Advent and sung again at Christmas [^girm][^gloria-ce]. Its early printings set the Gloria apart from the verse. In the Paris book of 1842, marked "très doux", very soft, two upper voices sing the verse and the bass comes in only for the Gloria, sung twice [^lamb1842]. The London book of 1860 marks the verse "Solo" and the Gloria "Chorus", and its preface explains how: a few singers who know the tune sing the verse, and the whole congregation answers [^hfh1860].

The run is the same in the 1842, 1860 and 1916 printings: "Glo" starts on a long note and falls away in quick notes, five to a bar, each bar starting a step lower [^lamb1842][^hfh1860][^hutchins]. The French tune ends its second and fourth lines on A, a third above the home note F; the Boston book of 1916, like Chope's of 1875, brings those lines down to F, the shape many hymnals now print [^lamb1842][^hutchins][^chope][^ha]. Its Gloria, sung once, is marked "Unison. ad lib." [^hutchins].

In hymnals the tune is called GLORIA, and its meter is 7.7.7.7 with the refrain [^psh][^hymnary]. Many hymnals print the harmony that Edward Shippen Barnes (1887 to 1958), an American organist, made for The New Church Hymnal of 1937, with the Gloria sung twice [^ha][^psh][^canterbury-barnes]. It takes over, note for note, the four-part harmony that Charles L. Hutchins (1838 to 1920) printed in Boston in 1891, in The Parish Choir, the weekly he edited, and in Carols Old and Carols New in 1916; Barnes added the second Gloria [^ha][^parishchoir1891][^hutchins].

Our arrangement reads the tune and words from Carol 181 in Carols Old and Carols New, which prints it in four parts in F, and sings it a tone higher, in G: verses 1 to 3, each followed by the refrain once, as the book prints it [^hutchins]. The soprano sings that tune, and the alto, tenor and bass are our own, checked against the chords of the book's unsigned four-part setting, for 2 to 4 singers (soprano and alto, then bass, then tenor).

## Did you know?

- The refrain's Latin is the wording of the Mass, not of the Latin Bible. In the Latin Vulgate, Luke 2:14 reads "Gloria in altissimis Deo", while the Mass's hymn, and the carol, say "in excelsis" [^vulgate-drbo][^vulgate-org][^gloria-ce].
- The Catholic Mass leaves out its Gloria on the Sundays of Advent, the weeks before Christmas, so at Christmas the angels' words come back [^girm][^gloria-ce].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/angels-we-have-heard-on-high/soprano): the tune, most often on B, then D. Each "Glo-" holds D, then runs down in quick notes, each bar a step lower, touching the top E just after it starts. Take the breath marked before it and pace it to the "ri-a", which drops to the low D.
- [Alto](/songs/angels-we-have-heard-on-high/alto): mostly a sixth below the tune. After a breath it leaps an octave, low B to top B, for each "Glo-", then holds one note a chord under the run. On "ri-a" it stays on F-sharp, above the tune's low D, the only place it crosses the tune.
- [Tenor](/songs/angels-we-have-heard-on-high/tenor): the middle of the chord, mostly G, A and B, between F-sharp and D in the verses. Under each run it holds long notes, up to its top E, then D, B and A; "Deo." steps G, F-sharp down to the low D.
- [Bass](/songs/angels-we-have-heard-on-high/bass): the roots of the chords, mostly G, low and high, and D. Each verse climbs G, B, D from the low G to the high G at the end of its first line. Under the run it holds G, A, G, A, then D for "ri-a", and the last "o" lands on the low G.

## Questions

### Who wrote the original Angels We Have Heard on High?

No one knows. The French carol's earliest known printing is in a book of noëls that a library copy, missing its title page, dates to Marseille, 1805; later editions credit the book to Father Roche, who may have collected some of its songs rather than written them [^roche1805][^bnf-roche1829][^ha]. The opening "Les anges dans nos campagnes" is first known from a Paris book of 1842 [^lamb1842][^sommervogel]. The English began with James Chadwick in 1860, and was revised in The Crown of Jesus in 1862 [^hfh1860][^julian].

### Who wrote the music for Angels We Have Heard on High?

It is not known. The tune's earliest known printing, in Paris in 1842, says only "Musique de W. M.", and nobody has shown who that was [^lamb1842][^sommervogel][^ha]. Later four-part harmonies came from Charles L. Hutchins, in his Boston weekly The Parish Choir (1891), and from Edward Shippen Barnes, who extended Hutchins's harmony for The New Church Hymnal (1937) [^parishchoir1891][^ha][^psh].

### What does Gloria in excelsis Deo mean, and what Bible verse is it from?

It is Latin for "Glory to God in the highest", the angels' song to the shepherds on the night of Jesus' birth, in Luke 2:14, where they go on: and on earth peace to people of good will [^vulgate-org][^vulgate-drbo]. The carol retells Luke 2:8 to 15, the angel's news and the child in the manger, with that song as its refrain [^psh][^vulgate-org]. The same words open the Gloria, the ancient hymn sung at Mass, which Catholic tradition calls the angelic hymn [^gloria-ce][^loomis].

### Is Angels We Have Heard on High public domain?

The carol itself is old. Its French words and its tune are anonymous, in print by 1805 and 1842 [^roche1805][^lamb1842]. James Chadwick, who wrote the English in 1860, died in 1882, and Charles L. Hutchins, whose page of 1916 our arrangement reads, died in 1920 [^hfh1860][^julian-chadwick][^ha][^hutchins]. Edward Shippen Barnes's harmony of 1937, printed in many hymnals, is a later work of its own and not what we sing [^ha][^canterbury-barnes]. Our arrangement is our own: the soprano sings the 1916 tune, and the alto, tenor and bass are our parts.

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano. In our arrangement it sings the tune as Carols Old and Carols New printed it in 1916, a tone higher, long run on "Glo-" and all, while the alto, tenor and bass, our own parts, hold long notes under the run [^hutchins]. The alto crosses above the tune only on the "ri-a" after each run, where the tune drops to its low D.
