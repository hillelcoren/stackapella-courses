O the Beautiful Treasure, headed "Valuable Riches" in its manuscript, is a short Shaker song in a minor key. Its two short strains praise a treasure laid up for the wise, brighter than the diamonds on a prince's brow. Mary Hazzard, a Shaker sister at New Lebanon, New York, copied it into her handwritten songbook about 1849 and signed it "Holy Ground", which the historian Edward Deming Andrews gives as the Shakers' name for Canterbury, New Hampshire [^hazzard][^andrews]. No maker is named [^hazzard]. Its picture of stored-up treasure echoes Jesus's saying about laying up treasures in heaven, an image the Shakers sang about again and again [^kjv-bg][^kjv-pg][^hazzardbook][^andrews]. In 1940 Andrews printed it in his book of Shaker songs, indexed as "Oh the beautiful treasures" [^andrews].

## Where it comes from

**The Shakers and their songs.** The Shakers, the United Society of Believers, lived in villages apart from the world, celibate, and worshipped with their own songs and dances [^shakermuseum][^andrews]. They held their property in common: members of the senior order, the Church Family, gave theirs to the community when they signed its covenant [^andrews][^devinne]. From 1837 a long revival, Mother Ann's Work, filled Shaker meetings with new short songs that worshippers believed were given from the spirit world [^andrews][^wintcoll][^devinne]. O the Beautiful Treasure comes from the later years of that revival, which lasted into the 1850s [^andrews][^wintcoll].

**The songbook.** Mary Hazzard was a sister of the Church Family at New Lebanon, the Shakers' leading village, and Andrews counted her among the busiest Shaker copyists for the beauty of her hand [^andrews][^smportrait][^shakermuseum]. Her book is titled "A Collection of Extra Songs of Various Kinds Written and Pricked For the Purpose of Retaining them", and she began it on 7 February 1847 [^hazzardbook][^wintcat]. Songs from many Shaker villages fill it, most signed with the place they came from. Winterthur Library, in Delaware, holds it as ASC 893 [^wintcat]. The same book gives us [Simple Gifts](/songs/simple-gifts), whose story tells more about it.

**The page.** The song is on Hazzard's page 70, under "Valuable Riches" in a large, flourished hand [^hazzard]. Under each line of words runs the tune in Shaker letteral notation: small letters for the pitches, strokes and joining lines for how long each note lasts, and bar lines between the measures [^hazzard][^wintcoll]. Beside the bracket that opens the first line stand a small "3" and a crossed double bar, which we read as two beats to the bar, and the letters move from a to a, a tune in A minor [^hazzard]. Dotted bars mark both strains to be sung twice [^hazzard]. Under the song, in a smaller hand, she wrote "Holy Ground", and below that, after a gap, comes a different tune with no words, signed "Enfield N.H." [^hazzard].

**When it was copied.** The page has no date [^hazzard]. Two pages on, a wordless tune is labelled "Elder Joseph Brackett's Song March 1849", and on page 57 another is signed "James Bishop Sept 10th 1849" [^hazzardbook]. The book is not in strict date order, so the song can be dated only loosely, to about 1849 [^hazzardbook]. Andrews dated it to 1849 as well [^andrews].

**Holy Ground.** During the revival most Shaker villages were given a spiritual name. In Andrews's list, New Lebanon was Holy Mount, Enfield in New Hampshire was Chosen Vale, and Canterbury was Holy Ground; Christine De Vinne's table of Shaker place names, which draws partly on Andrews, gives the same [^andrews][^devinne]. (Groveland, New York, and South Union, Kentucky, gave the same name to their outdoor worship grounds [^devinne].) Other songs Hazzard signs "Canterbury" outright, like "I know the treasure I have gain'd" on her page 104 [^hazzardbook].

**Who made it.** Nobody is named. Many songs in Hazzard's book name the person or the spirit who gave them; this one carries only a place [^hazzardbook][^hazzard]. When Andrews printed it in 1940, as No. 66 of his collection, he called it "An Enfield (N. H.) song of the year 1849" [^andrews]. That disagrees with Hazzard's "Holy Ground". Andrews knew her book well: his Fig. 4 is its Simple Gifts page, and for "Me bless the Cross", which she signed "Given at Holy Ground June 17th 1847", he named Canterbury as the place and her 1847 hymnal as his source [^andrews][^hazzardbook]. For No. 66 he names no manuscript. In her book the song sits among songs from Enfield: the songs on her pages 68 and 69, and the wordless tune just below it, are all signed "Enfield N.H." [^hazzardbook][^hazzard]. Whether Andrews had another copy or took the place from those neighbours, the record does not say.

### Timeline

- 1837: Mother Ann's Work begins, the revival of new songs [^andrews][^devinne]
- 1847: Mary Hazzard begins her book of extra songs, on 7 February [^hazzardbook][^wintcat]
- 1847: on 17 June she signs another song "Given at Holy Ground", which Andrews later places at Canterbury [^hazzardbook][^andrews]
- 1849: songs dated March and September 1849 sit a few pages from "Valuable Riches" [^hazzardbook]; Andrews dates the song to this year [^andrews]
- 1940: Andrews prints it as No. 66 of The Gift to Be Simple, as a song from Enfield [^andrews]

## What the words mean

**"O the beautiful treasure laid up for the wise."** To lay something up is to store it away for later. The line recalls Jesus's words in the Sermon on the Mount, which in the King James Bible read "lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust doth corrupt" (Matthew 6:20), with the warning that "where your treasure is, there will your heart be also" [^kjv-bg][^kjv-pg]. "The wise" may echo Proverbs, where "There is treasure to be desired and oil in the dwelling of the wise" (Proverbs 21:20) [^kjv-bg][^kjv-pg]. The song quotes neither verse, so these are echoes, not sources.

**"How precious the value, how glorious the prize."** The treasure is also a prize, a reward. The reward runs all through the gift songs Andrews gathered; one sung at New Lebanon in 1839 begins "O what a pretty treasure Mother has for every one" [^andrews]. In Hazzard's book a Canterbury song makes the same pairing: its treasure is "The greatest prize that can be found" [^hazzardbook].

**"Far brighter than diamonds on Princes brow."** The diamonds are a crown's. Hazzard wrote "Princes" with no apostrophe, so it can be one prince or many. Andrews printed "prince's"; we print "Princes'" [^hazzard][^andrews].

**"And richer than royalty can bestow."** To bestow is to give. No king could give anything worth as much. For people who had given up their own property, the song's point was plain: they had traded earthly riches for the better kind, like the man in Jesus's parable who found "treasure hid in a field" and sold all he had to buy it (Matthew 13:44) [^kjv-bg][^kjv-pg][^andrews].

Treasure comes up again and again in Hazzard's book. Four pages before this song comes a different one headed "A Beautiful Treasure", and later she copied "What a pure and spotless treasure", whose treasure is "laid up in store in the mansions of heaven" [^hazzardbook].

### The full words

These are the words on Hazzard's page 70, with her spelling and punctuation, in her two strains [^hazzard].

O the beautiful treasure laid up for the wise
How precious the value, how glorious the prize

Far brighter than diamonds on Princes brow,
And richer than royalty can bestow.

## How it is sung

Hazzard's book is a book of "extra songs": short songs sung in worship besides the regular ones [^andrews][^hazzardbook]. Each strain is a single couplet, marked to be sung twice [^hazzard].

Our arrangement is read from Hazzard's page [^hazzard]. Every pitch is as she wrote it, moved down a fourth from A minor to E minor. Her letters carry no octave, so the octave of the last two notes, "be-stow", is our arranger's choice: the upper one, because she wrote them above the strain's E [^hazzard]. Her two beats to the bar are written as four, with every value doubled. Both strains are sung twice, as her repeat signs mark them, and breaths are written in for beginners. The song is for 2 to 4 singers, with the tune in the soprano, the words for the alto and tenor, and "oo" for the bass. The harmony is ours.

## Versions and other words

Andrews's 1940 printing has the same words and writes each strain out twice [^andrews]. It is not the song Hazzard headed "A Beautiful Treasure" on her page 66, which begins "O here I have found a beautiful treasure" and has its own tune [^hazzardbook].

## Did you know?

- Holy Ground is one of the spiritual names that, as Christine De Vinne reads them, joined a feature of the land to a Bible allusion [^devinne]. The phrase is the one Moses hears at the burning bush: "the place whereon thou standest is holy ground" (Exodus 3:5) [^kjv-bg][^kjv-pg].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/o-the-beautiful-treasure/soprano): you carry the minor-key tune. The first strain stays close around E, F sharp and G, then drops to a low B on "wise". The second strain lifts: "Far" takes two notes, G and A, and "brighter" and "richer" reach C. At the very end, "can be-stow" climbs B, D, E to your top E, the highest note of the song.
- [Alto](/songs/o-the-beautiful-treasure/alto): you sing the words close beneath the tune, mostly a third under it after the first three notes. Your low G comes on "O", "wise" and "prize". In the second strain you rise to G on "than"; the second time through it, start "Far" on D, not B.
- [Tenor](/songs/o-the-beautiful-treasure/tenor): you sing the words low, between D and C. "How precious" sits on a low D for three notes. The first strain ends differently each time: the second time you rise to C on "glorious" and end on G, not E. The second strain stays mostly on G and ends on B for "be-stow".
- [Bass](/songs/o-the-beautiful-treasure/bass): you sing "oo", mostly on the roots of the chords, coming in on E after the "O the" pickup. Hold the long C under "sure laid up for the" for six beats. In the second strain, wait through "Far" and come in on a low G, your lowest note.

## Questions

### Who wrote O the Beautiful Treasure?

No one is named. Mary Hazzard copied it about 1849 under the heading "Valuable Riches" and signed it only "Holy Ground", which Edward Deming Andrews gives as the Shakers' name for Canterbury, New Hampshire [^hazzard][^andrews]. Yet when Andrews printed the song in 1940, he called it a song from Enfield, New Hampshire, of 1849 [^andrews]. It is anonymous, as many songs in her book are [^hazzardbook].

### What does "laid up for the wise" mean?

It means stored away for wise people to receive. The phrase echoes Matthew 6:20, "lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven", where thieves and rust cannot reach them [^kjv-bg][^kjv-pg]. The Shakers had given their own property to the community, and their songs often promised a heavenly reward to the faithful [^andrews][^devinne]. The treasure is that reward, worth more than any crown.

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune all the way through. Two singers take the soprano and alto; three add the bass; the tenor makes four. The alto and tenor sing the same words close beneath the tune, and the bass sings "oo". Hazzard's page has the tune alone, so the harmony is ours [^hazzard].
