Good Christian Men, Rejoice, in many newer hymnals Good Christian Friends, Rejoice, is John Mason Neale's English Christmas carol of 1853 [^neale1853][^ha][^hymnary]. It is not a close translation but a free version of In dulci jubilo, "in sweet rejoicing", a German carol that mixes Latin and German lines and is known from manuscripts of the late 1300s [^ha][^canterbury-idj][^hsc-mainz]. Neale and his friend Thomas Helmore took its tune from Piae Cantiones, a Latin songbook of the Swedish and Finnish church printed in 1582 [^ha][^julian][^pc1582]. Helmore misread the old notes and added two long ones, and Neale filled them with "News! News!": a mistake that some books still sing [^canterbury-gcmr][^ha].

## Where it comes from

**The carol.** In dulci jubilo is a macaronic song: its lines take turns between Latin and German [^julian][^obc]. The oldest known copy of its words is in a manuscript at the city library of Mainz, Hs I 164, in a section written at the end of the 1300s, without music [^ha][^hsc-mainz]. The oldest copy with the tune is on folio 116r of a manuscript at Leipzig University Library, Ms 1305 [^ha][^hsc-leipzig][^obc]. Its date is disputed: the Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology places it at the end of the 1300s, Woodward in 1910 at the end of the 1300s or the beginning of the 1400s, and the paper's watermarks led Clytus Gottwald in 1964 to about 1420 [^canterbury-idj][^woodward][^hsc-leipzig][^ha]. Its first lines run "In dulci iubilo / singet und sit vro", in sweet rejoicing, sing and be glad [^woodward][^ha]. Philipp Wackernagel's great collection of German hymns prints eight versions, of three to seven stanzas [^julian][^canterbury-idj].

**Into print.** The Hymnology Archive thinks its earliest printing was probably Joseph Klug's Wittenberg hymnal of 1529, of which no copy survives [^ha]. Klug's edition of 1533 is the earliest surviving printing of the tune [^ha][^hymnary]. In the Lutheran hymnals of the 1540s the carol gained a new third stanza, "O Patris caritas", O love of the Father. The Oxford Book of Carols and Woodward place it first in Babst's Leipzig hymnal of 1545, Luther's last, and give it to Luther; the Hymnology Archive finds it already in a Leipzig book of 1543 and calls Luther its likely author [^obc][^woodward][^ha].

**Piae Cantiones.** In 1582 Theodoricus Petri of Nyland published the carol, printed in Greifswald, in Piae Cantiones, his book of the old church and school songs of Sweden and Finland [^pc1582][^woodward][^julian]. In the National Library of Finland's copy it begins partway down one page and ends on the next, a leaf signed C2. The tune is a single line, without bar lines, in square and diamond-shaped notes, with the figure 3, a sign for triple time, after the clef. The Latin keeps its place, but Swedish takes the place of the German: "Nu siunge wij io io", now we sing, io, io. Three more stanzas follow as text, "O Iesu parvule", "O Patris charitas" and "Ubi sunt gaudia", in the same order as Babst's book [^pc1582][^ha]. Woodward printed it again in his edition of 1910 [^woodward].

**Neale and Helmore.** A copy of this rare book was brought from Stockholm to the editors of the Hymnal Noted, Neale and Helmore, in the early 1850s [^julian][^woodward][^ha]. Neale, an English priest, lived from 1818 to 1866; Helmore, priest and musician, from 1811 to 1890 [^julian][^canterbury-helmore][^ha]. From its tunes came their Carols for Christmas-tide of 1853 [^julian][^obc]. In the folio edition, Good Christian men, rejoice is No. 6, on pages 18 and 19, marked "Animato", in 6/8 time in F major, for four voices and keyboard; verses 2 and 3 are printed below the music [^neale1853]. In the small vocal edition, dated 1854 on its title page, it is No. VI, on pages 20 and 21, with all three verses under the notes [^cvp1854]. A notice signed by both men, dated on the feast of Saints Simon and Jude, 1853, says "we give the music without alteration" and calls the words "free imitations" of old carols [^cvp1854].

**Myth and record.** Often told: the carol was written by Heinrich Seuse, also called Henry Suso, a German Dominican friar and mystic who died at Ulm in 1366, or he learned it from angels [^woodward][^canterbury-gcmr][^ce-suso]. What the record shows: in the story of his life, written in his last years at Ulm, Seuse tells of himself, "the Servitor", that on the feast of All Angels heavenly youths drew him into a dance, and their leader began a joyful song about the infant Jesus, "In dulci jubilo", and sang the burden "Ergo merito", therefore rightly, three times [^knox][^ha][^ce-suso]. He names it by its first words and does not say he wrote it, so scholars think it was already known [^ha][^obc]. Woodward in 1910 thought it "almost certainly" his, the Canterbury Dictionary says "possibly" [^woodward][^canterbury-gcmr]. An older guess, Peter of Dresden, of the early 1400s, is too late: the carol is older [^julian][^woodward]. Sources differ on Seuse's dates: born about 1295 or in 1300, died in 1366 or 1365 [^ce-suso][^ha][^knox][^woodward].

**Myth and record.** Often told: Neale misread the old notes. What the record shows: the music was Helmore's work, and the Canterbury Dictionary and the Hymnology Archive put the misreading on him. Reading the 1582 notation, he added two long notes, two whole beats after the third phrase, and Neale gave them words: "News! News!", "Joy! Joy!" and "Peace! Peace!" [^canterbury-gcmr][^ha]. The 1854 edition marks "News!" and "Joy!" fz, sharply accented, and "Peace!" p, soft [^cvp1854]. The German carol, Pearsall's setting and many later hymnals have no such line [^pearsall1836][^obc][^hymnary].

### Timeline

- 1360s: Heinrich Seuse's Life tells of angels dancing to "In dulci jubilo". [^knox][^ha][^ce-suso]

- About 1420, or earlier: the earliest known copy of the tune, in Leipzig, Ms 1305. [^ha][^hsc-leipzig][^obc]

## What the words mean

Neale wrote three verses. They do not follow the old stanzas line by line: Neale and Helmore themselves called their words "free imitations" of old carols [^cvp1854][^ha]. Each verse keeps the same frame, "Good Christian men, rejoice / With heart, and soul, and voice", and changes the rest [^neale1853]. The Latin and German lines of the original are given below, with our own translation.

**Verse 1.** "Give ye heed to what we say": listen. The news is the birth. The ox and the ass stand at the manger in older carols too: in the same book of 1582, Puer natus in Bethlehem has "Ad praesepe stant bos et asinus", the ox and the ass stand at the manger [^pc1582]. "And he is in the manger now" echoes the original's "in praesepio", in a manger [^neale1853][^obc].

**Verse 2.** "Endless bliss" is the joy of heaven. "He hath oped the heav'nly door": oped is opened, a short form used only in poetry [^webster]. "And man is blessed evermore": people are blessed for ever [^neale1853].

**Verse 3.** "Now ye need not fear the grave": death has lost its terror. Christ "calls you one, and calls you all" to "gain His everlasting hall", the hall of heaven. The original's last stanzas also end in heaven, in "regis curia", the King's court [^neale1853][^obc].

### In dulci jubilo: the Latin and German

This is the four-stanza form of Babst's book and of Piae Cantiones, as Robert Lucas Pearsall printed it in his score dated 1836, under the heading "The original words are as follow", with his spellings; he marks each last line for the chorus [^pearsall1836][^ha]. Older manuscripts differ in their stanzas and wording [^ha][^woodward]. The English is our own, checked against the Oxford Book of Carols' glosses [^obc].

### The full words

These are Neale's three verses as printed in the folio Carols for Christmas-tide of 1853: verse 1 under the music, verses 2 and 3 below it [^neale1853]. The vocal edition of 1854 has the same words, with small differences of print: "And He is in the manger now" with a capital, and "Peace Peace!" with no mark after the first "Peace" [^cvp1854]. Neale died in 1866, so all of it is in the public domain [^julian][^ha].

**Verse 1**
Good Christian men, rejoice
With heart, and soul, and voice;
Give ye heed to what we say:
News! News!
Jesus Christ is born to-day:
Ox and ass before him bow,
And he is in the manger now.
Christ is born to-day! Christ is born to-day!

**Verse 2**
Good Christian men, rejoice
With heart, and soul, and voice;
Now ye hear of endless bliss:
Joy! Joy!
Jesus Christ was born for this!
He hath oped the heav'nly door,
And man is blessed evermore.
Christ was born for this! Christ was born for this!

**Verse 3**
Good Christian men, rejoice
With heart, and soul, and voice;
Now ye need not fear the grave:
Peace! Peace!
Jesus Christ was born to save!
Calls you one, and calls you all,
To gain His everlasting hall:
Christ was born to save! Christ was born to save!

## How it is sung

It is a Christmas carol. In Germany In dulci jubilo was sung, Pearsall wrote in 1837, in processions on Christmas Eve, and still was in remote parts of the country [^pearsall1836]. In England Neale's words spread through Bramley and Stainer's Christmas Carols New and Old, which printed them as No. 8 with News! News! kept; Julian's Dictionary of Hymnology named it among the best carols in that book [^bram][^ha][^julian]. Hymnary.org lists it in hundreds of hymnals [^hymnary].

The tune is IN DULCI JUBILO [^hymnary][^ha]. In Neale and Helmore's book it is in 6/8 time; every verse is sung to the same music, and the last line, "Christ is born to-day!", is sung twice [^neale1853][^cvp1854]. Hymnals print it in two shapes: with Helmore's extra line, or without it, as in the German carol, which gives the meter 6.6.7.7.7.8.5.5 [^ha][^hymnary]. Many newer hymnals begin "Good Christian friends, rejoice" [^ha][^hymnary]. Another English version, wholly in English, is Arthur T. Russell's "Now sing we, now rejoice" of 1851 [^ha][^julian].

Our arrangement takes its tune and words from the 1854 vocal edition, No. VI, in F major and 6/8, starting on a quick pickup, with all three verses as printed there [^cvp1854]. So verse 3 sings "Peace Peace!" as that page has it, and holds both "Peace" notes long, where verses 1 and 2 cut the first "News!" and "Joy!" short [^cvp1854]. The alto, tenor and bass are our own, one note to each syllable, on the chords of the unsigned four-part harmony in Bramley and Stainer's Christmas Carols New and Old [^bram]. It is for two to four singers: two take soprano, on the tune, and alto, a third adds the bass, and the tenor makes four.

## Versions and other words

- **In Scots English.** The earliest English version, "In dulci jubilo, Now let us sing with mirth and jo", is in The Gude and Godlie Ballatis. The Oxford Book of Carols dates it to about 1540 and credits John Wedderburn; Julian cites the edition of 1568, and the Hymnology Archive shows one of 1578 [^obc][^julian][^ha].
- **Pearsall's setting.** Robert Lucas Pearsall, an English composer living at Willsbridge in Gloucestershire, wrote an eight-part setting with his own English, "In dulci jubilo, let us our homage show", keeping the Latin lines. His preface, dated 31 January 1837, says it was written for the choral society at Karlsruhe and sung there in the autumn of 1834. It was published in London by D'Almaine, its title page dated 1836 [^pearsall1836][^ha]. Julian names only later printings, in the Musical Times and in Novello's Part Song Book [^julian]. Pearsall lived from 1795 to 1856 [^canterbury-pearsall][^obc].

## Did you know?

- Pearsall took his tune, he wrote, from a German book of 1570 made for the Protestant congregations of Zweibrücken and Neuburg, which already called it a very ancient song for Christmas Eve [^pearsall1836].
- The Oxford Book of Carols of 1928 did not print Neale's words. It gave In dulci jubilo a new English version that keeps the Latin lines, with Bartholomew Gesius's harmony of 1601 and, for the last verse, Bach's [^obc].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/good-christian-men-rejoice/soprano): the old tune, from low D to top D, most often on F, then C. Its top D comes as the quick middle note of the held "re-joice" and "voice", each sung C, D, C on one syllable, then on "ye" in "Give ye heed" and "and" in "Ox and ass" ("ye" and "hath" in verse 2, "ye" and "you" in verse 3). Its low D comes only at the start of the last line, "Christ is" ("Christ was" in verses 2 and 3). The first "day!" of that line rises from F to C on one syllable; give it its full length.
- [Alto](/songs/good-christian-men-rejoice/alto): under the tune, one note to each syllable, most often on E, then C. It holds E under the tune's long "joice" and "voice". Its top F comes four times a verse, on "ye" and "what" in the third line and on "and" and "fore" in "Ox and ass before" ("ye", "end-", "hath", "heav'n-"; "ye", "fear", "you", "calls"). Its low A comes only on the two last words of each verse, "day!", "this!" or "save!".
- [Tenor](/songs/good-christian-men-rejoice/tenor): the middle of the chord, most often on A, then F and G. Its top C comes on "ian men," and "and soul," in the first two lines and on the first word of lines 3 and 5 ("Give", "Ox"; "Now", "He"; "Now", "Calls"). It ends verses 1 and 2 on F, and drops to its low C only for the song's last note, "save!".
- [Bass](/songs/good-christian-men-rejoice/bass): the bass line, most often on F, then C. It holds F under "Good Christian men, re-" and "heart, and soul, and", and drops to C for each held "joice" and "voice". Under "News! News!" it sings B-flat, then D. Its low F comes only once, on the song's last "save!", an octave under the F it sings everywhere else.

## Questions

### Who wrote Good Christian Men, Rejoice?

John Mason Neale, an English priest and hymn writer who lived from 1818 to 1866, wrote the English words; their earliest known printing is Carols for Christmas-tide, 1853 [^julian][^neale1853][^ha]. He based them freely on In dulci jubilo, a German and Latin carol known from manuscripts of the late 1300s. Nobody knows who wrote that carol; a story links it to the friar Heinrich Seuse, but he did not claim it [^ha][^knox][^canterbury-gcmr].

### What does In dulci jubilo mean in English?

It means "in sweet rejoicing" or "in sweet jubilation" [^obc][^ha]. The carol goes on, in German, "now sing and be glad", and in Latin and German says that our hearts' delight lies in a manger and shines like the sun in his mother's lap [^pearsall1836][^obc]. The whole song with our line-by-line translation is above, under "In dulci jubilo: the Latin and German".

### Why do some versions sing "News! News!" and others don't?

Because of a misreading. Thomas Helmore, who read the tune from the old notes of Piae Cantiones for Neale's carol in 1853, added two long notes after the third phrase, and Neale gave them words: "News! News!", "Joy! Joy!", "Peace! Peace!" [^canterbury-gcmr][^ha]. Books that follow Neale and Helmore keep the line; hymnals that follow the German tune drop it [^ha][^hymnary].
