While Shepherds Watched Their Flocks, often sung as While Shepherds Watched Their Flocks by Night, is a Christmas hymn that retells the angel's message to the shepherds in Luke's Gospel, almost clause by clause, in six short verses [^sup1700][^kjv]. Its earliest known printing is in London in 1700, in A Supplement to the New Version of Psalms by Nahum Tate and Nicholas Brady, and the words are generally credited to Tate [^sup1700][^canterbury][^dnb-tate]. In 1703 the Queen in Council allowed that Supplement in churches, and of the six hymns it added, this was the one for Christmas [^sup1704][^eb1911]. British churches sing it to WINCHESTER OLD, a psalm tune printed in 1592; many American books use CHRISTMAS, a tune made from an aria by Handel [^ha-wsw][^hymnary-text][^umh236].

## Where it comes from

**The New Version.** Nahum Tate was born in Dublin in 1652, the son of a clergyman, and took his degree at Trinity College, Dublin, in 1672 [^dnb-tate][^hymnary-tate][^ha-tatebrady]. He became Poet Laureate in 1692 [^dnb-tate][^rpo-tate]. With Nicholas Brady, an Irish-born clergyman, he published A New Version of the Psalms of David in 1696: the psalms in English verse, meant to replace the "Old Version" of Sternhold and Hopkins [^dnb-tate][^eb1911][^ha-tatebrady]. William III's council allowed the new book to be used in churches that chose it [^eb1911][^ha-tatebrady].

**The Supplement, 1700.** Four years later came a companion volume. Its title page reads "A Supplement to the New Version of Psalms by N. Tate and N. Brady", printed in London by J. Heptinstall and sold at Stationers' Hall and by other booksellers, dated 1700, price sixpence in sheets, "to be stitch'd singly, or to bind up with the foresaid Version" [^sup1700][^ha-wsw]. On page 18, under the heading "Additional Hymns which may be Sung to any of the Tunes of Common Measure, printed towards the end of this Supplement", the first is "Song of the Angels at the Nativity of our Blessed Saviour", marked "Luke II. from v.8. to v.15." [^sup1700][^canterbury]. It has no music of its own. It is set in three blocks of eight lines, with a quotation mark at the start of every line the angel speaks [^sup1700][^ha-wsw]. Two hymns for Easter Day follow, then three for Holy Communion, the last a verse form of the Gloria from the communion service: six in all, and this the only one for Christmas [^sup1700][^eb1911].

**Who wrote it.** The 1700 page names no author, and the title page names both men [^sup1700]. The Dictionary of National Biography says the hymn is "generally attributed" to Tate, and the Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology says it is normally given to him although the part each man played in the book is not clear [^dnb-tate][^canterbury]. The Encyclopaedia Britannica of 1911 found its simplicity unlike the rest of Tate's work, while the Canterbury Dictionary finds that plainness typical of both men, and the Hymnology Archive suggests crediting Tate and Brady together [^eb1911][^canterbury][^ha-wsw].

**Which year?** The reference books disagree. The Psalter Hymnal Handbook and the Hymnology Archive give 1700 [^hymnary-text][^ha-wsw]; Julian's Dictionary of Hymnology says the hymn appeared in the Supplement in 1702, in six stanzas of four lines; the Encyclopaedia Britannica of 1911 and some hymnals give 1703 [^hymnary-text][^eb1911]. The 1700 title page is the earliest we have seen [^sup1700]. The third edition of 1702 prints the hymn again, and 1703 is the year of the royal order [^sup1702][^sup1704].

**The royal allowance, 1703.** The fifth edition of 1704 opens with the order under the royal arms: "At the Court at Hampton-Court, the 30th Day of July, 1703", the Queen in Council, on the petition of "Nicholas Brady, and Naham Tate", ordered that the Supplement "Is hereby Allowed and Permitted to be used in all Churches, Chappels, and Congregations, as shall think fit to receive the same", signed William Blathwayt [^sup1704]. The Encyclopaedia Britannica of 1911 named Queen Anne's order as the latest act of any public authority to sanction psalms or hymns in the Church of England [^eb1911].

**Myth and record.** Often told: this was the first Christmas hymn the Church of England allowed, and for a time the only one [^christiantoday]. What the record shows is narrower. The order of 1703 allowed and permitted the Supplement, as the order of 1696 had the psalms; it did not require anyone to sing it [^sup1704][^eb1911].

**Tate's last years.** Tate died in 1715 in the Mint in Southwark, where he had taken refuge from his creditors, and was buried at St George's nearby [^dnb-tate][^hymnary-tate][^rpo-tate]. The day is disputed: the Dictionary of National Biography and the Hymnal 1940 Companion give 12 August, while Representative Poetry Online, and the Hymnology Archive in a note to a 1911 account, give 30 July [^dnb-tate][^hymnary-tate][^rpo-tate][^ha-tatebrady].

**The tune, 1592.** The tune is older than the words by more than a century. In 1592 the London printer Thomas Este, "the assigne of William Byrd", published The Whole Booke of Psalmes "with their wonted tunes, as they are song in Churches, composed into foure parts" [^este1592][^ha-wsw]. His preface says he asked many musicians to set them, "for varieties sake", and laid out the book so that "the 4. parts lye alwayes together in open sight"; under it, a printed pointing hand is explained as "a direction for the Church Tune" [^este1592]. On pages 162 and 163, Psalm 84, which begins (in modern spelling) "How pleasant is thy dwelling place, O Lord of hosts, to me", spreads across the opening: Cantus and Tenor on the left page, Altus and Bassus on the right, in diamond-headed notes, with "Psalme. 84." and "G.Kirby." at the head of each page's first part. The pointing hand stands beside the Tenor, which carries the tune we sing; the page gives the tune no name [^este1592][^hymnary-tune]. George Kirbye, whose name heads the setting, died in 1634 [^hymnary-tune][^ha-wsw]. Who made the melody is not known. Some scholars link it to a tune in Christopher Tye's Actes of the Apostles of 1553 [^ha-wsw][^hymnary-tune]. Thomas Ravenscroft's psalter of 1621 called it WINCHESTER [^ha-wsw][^hymnary-text].

**Words and tune meet, 1861.** Hymns Ancient and Modern, whose musical editor was William Henry Monk, came out with tunes in 1861 [^dnb-monk][^hymnary-monk]. It prints these words as Hymn 44, under the heading "Christmas" and the text "Unto you is born this day in the city of David a Saviour which is Christ the Lord", with the tune above and an Amen at the end; the page itself gives no tune name [^am1861]. The book's Index of Tunes lists "Winchester, Old", from "Este", "Harmonized or Arranged by" W. H. Monk, for Hymns 44, 130 and 157; "Winchester New", credited to Crasselius, is a different tune in the same index [^am1861]. This is the earliest known pairing of the words with WINCHESTER OLD [^ha-wsw][^hymnary-tune]. In the Revised Edition of 1875 the hymn is No. 62, headed "Winchester Old" and "C.M." with a speed of 112 minims a minute, dynamics in the margin, a separate Amen, and "44 Orig. Ed." at the foot [^am1875][^hymnary-monk]. Monk died in London on 1 March 1889 [^dnb-monk][^ha-monk].

## What the words mean

Tate's six verses follow Luke 2:8 to 14 closely, adding only a few words to make the lines rhyme and scan [^kjv][^sup1700].

**Verse 1.** Luke has "shepherds abiding in the field, keeping watch over their flock by night", and "the angel of the Lord came upon them, and the glory of the Lord shone round about them" [^kjv]. Tate adds the picture of the shepherds "All seated on the ground".

**Verse 2.** "Fear not": Luke says they "were sore afraid", which Tate makes "mighty dread / Had seized their troubled mind". The angel's "good tidings of great joy, which shall be to all people" become "Glad tidings of great joy I bring / To you and all mankind" [^kjv].

**Verse 3.** "David's town" is Bethlehem, which Luke calls "the city of David" [^kjv]. Tate adds that the child is "born of David's line", of King David's family, as Luke says Joseph was. The 1700 page has "The Saviour"; our book has "A Saviour", as Luke does [^sup1700][^am1875][^kjv].

**Verse 4.** The sign: the child "wrapped in swaddling clothes, lying in a manger" [^kjv]. "Swathing bands" are bands of cloth bound round the body, and "meanly" means poorly or humbly [^webster1913]. "To human view display'd" means shown to human eyes; the 1700 page spells it "humane" [^sup1700].

**Verse 5.** A "seraph" is one of an order of heavenly beings with three pairs of wings, the creatures of Isaiah's vision; Tate uses the word for the angel [^webster1913][^kjv]. "Forthwith" is at once. The "shining throng" is Luke's "multitude of the heavenly host praising God" [^kjv]. The 1700 page reads "and thus / addrest"; later books changed it to "who thus", the main change almost all books made [^sup1700][^canterbury].

**Verse 6.** The angels' song is Luke 2:14, "Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace, good will toward men" [^kjv]. Tate's "henceforth" makes good will begin that night "and never cease". Our 1875 book reads "to the earth be peace", as in 1700; the first edition of 1861 has "in the earth" [^am1875][^sup1700][^am1861].

### The full words

These are the six verses as Hymns Ancient and Modern, Revised Edition (1875), No. 62, prints them and as we sing them, with its spelling, capitals and quotation marks; the book's small capitals of Lord, Christ and God are written here as ordinary words [^am1875]. The 1700 text is the same in substance, with the differences noted above [^sup1700]. We sing "heavenly" in verse 4 in two syllables, "heav'nly", as the 1700 page spells it. Tate died in 1715, so every verse is in the public domain.

**Verse 1**
While shepherds watch'd their flocks by night,
All seated on the ground,
The Angel of the Lord came down,
And glory shone around.

**Verse 2**
"Fear not," said he; for mighty dread
Had seized their troubled mind;
"Glad tidings of great joy I bring
To you and all mankind.

**Verse 3**
"To you in David's town this day
Is born of David's line
A Saviour, Who is Christ the Lord;
And this shall be the sign:

**Verse 4**
"The heavenly Babe you there shall find
To human view display'd,
All meanly wrapp'd in swathing bands,
And in a manger laid."

**Verse 5**
Thus spake the seraph; and forthwith
Appear'd a shining throng
Of Angels praising God, who thus
Address'd their joyful song:

**Verse 6**
"All glory be to God on high,
And to the earth be peace;
Good will henceforth from Heav'n to men
Begin and never cease."

## How it is sung

It is sung at Christmas, in common metre, four lines of eight and six syllables [^hymnary-text][^methodist]. Hymnary.org finds two tunes in common use, WINCHESTER OLD and CHRISTMAS, often both in one hymnal [^hymnary-text]. British books use WINCHESTER OLD: the Methodist Church in Britain's Singing the Faith prints it at No. 221, with LYNGHAM, which repeats lines, as the other choice [^methodist]. The United Methodist Hymnal in the United States sets it to CHRISTMAS, No. 236 [^umh236][^classicalmusic]. The Episcopal Church's Hymnal 1982 has it to WINCHESTER OLD at No. 94 and to HAMPTON at No. 95 [^eh1982-94][^eh1982-95]. The Psalter Hymnal Handbook suggests singing it as a small oratorio: a choir tells the story, a soloist sings the angel's words, and everyone takes the last verse as the throng of angels [^hymnary-tune].

Our arrangement is read from Hymn 62 of Hymns Ancient and Modern, Revised Edition (1875), in a later printing that includes the 1889 Supplement: Monk's four parts, in the book's key of F [^am1875]. The soprano sings WINCHESTER OLD note for note as the book prints it, the lower parts are Monk's with a few notes revoiced for beginners, and the Amen is left out. We sing all six verses. It is for 2 to 4 singers: two take soprano and alto, a third adds the bass, and the tenor makes four.

## Versions and other words

- **The Scottish version.** In 1745 the Church of Scotland's Translations and Paraphrases rewrote the first verse as "While humble shepherds watched their flocks" and made small changes to the rest [^hymnary-text][^ha-wsw].
- **CHRISTMAS.** This tune comes from an aria, "Non vi piacque ingiusti dei", in Handel's opera Siroe of 1728. Its earliest known printing as a hymn tune is in The Psalms of David for the Use of Parish Churches (London, 1791), credited to Samuel Arnold by the Hymnology Archive and to Arnold and John Wall Callcott by Hymnary.org [^ha-wsw][^hymnary-text]. Sung to it, the last line of each verse is repeated [^hymnary-text].
- **Other tunes.** Older American books often used SHERBURNE, a fuguing tune, and Hymnary.org lists these words with many more, among them CAROL, CRANBROOK and LYNGHAM [^ha-wsw][^hymnary-text].

## Did you know?

- Richard Storrs Willis's tune CAROL, now sung to It Came Upon the Midnight Clear, was meant for these words, the Hymnology Archive says, and Hymns of the Church with Tunes (New York, 1869) prints it with them as PIERCE, No. 110 [^ha-wsw][^hotc1869][^ha-itcame].
- CRANBROOK, a tune published in 1805 by Thomas Clark, a Canterbury shoemaker, was long sung to these words, and is now the tune of the Yorkshire song On Ilkla Moor Baht 'at [^wgma][^ilkley][^hymnary-text].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/while-shepherds-watched/soprano): WINCHESTER OLD, from low E to top D, most often on F, then A. The top D comes once a verse, on the second note of the third line ("An-gel", "ti-dings", "Sav-iour", "mean-ly", "An-gels", "will"), and from there the tune walks down note by note to the low E at the line's end ("came down,", "I bring", "the Lord;", "swathing bands,", "who thus", "to men"). Its one note outside the key is the B natural just before the end of the second line: "the" in "on the ground", "bled" in "troubled", "vid's" in "David's", "dis-" in "display'd", "ing" in "shining" and "be" in "be peace".
- [Alto](/songs/while-shepherds-watched/alto): under the tune, spread almost evenly over D, F and E. Its C-sharp, outside the key, comes at the end of the third line each verse, under the tune's low E ("down,", "bring", "Lord;", "bands,", "thus", "men"): tune it carefully. Its top G comes once a verse, on the third note of the second line ("ed" in "seated", "their", "of", "man" in "human", "a", "the"), and its low A only on the last note of each verse.
- [Tenor](/songs/while-shepherds-watched/tenor): the middle of the chord, on C almost half the time, then A. Its top D comes twice a verse, on the seventh note of the first line and the fifth of the second: "by" and "the" in verse 1, then "ty" and "bled", "this" and "vid's", "shall" and "dis-", "forth-" and "ing", "on" and "be". Its low F comes only on the last note of each verse, in unison with the bass.
- [Bass](/songs/while-shepherds-watched/bass): the bass line, most often on F, then C and B-flat, nearly always on the root of the chord. Its top B-flat comes with the tune's top D ("An-" in "Angel", "ti-", "Sav-", "mean-", "An-", "will"), and its low A at the end of the third line, under the alto's C-sharp ("down,", "bring", "Lord;", "bands,", "thus", "men").

## Questions

### Who wrote While Shepherds Watched Their Flocks by Night?

Nahum Tate, Poet Laureate from 1692, is generally credited with the words [^dnb-tate][^canterbury]. Their earliest known printing, in 1700, gives no author's name: it is in a Supplement to the New Version of the Psalms whose title page names both Tate and Nicholas Brady, and the Hymnology Archive suggests crediting them together [^sup1700][^ha-wsw]. The tune, WINCHESTER OLD, is by an unknown composer; its earliest known printing is in Thomas Este's psalm book of 1592, in four parts signed "G. Kirby" [^este1592][^ha-wsw].

### Is there an alternative tune for While Shepherds Watched Their Flocks by Night?

Yes, several. The words are in common metre, and the 1700 printing itself says they may be sung to any of the common-measure tunes printed in the book [^sup1700]. British books mostly use WINCHESTER OLD; many American books use CHRISTMAS, adapted from an aria in Handel's Siroe, repeating each verse's last line [^hymnary-text][^umh236][^ha-wsw]. Singing the Faith offers LYNGHAM, and the Hymnal 1982 offers HAMPTON [^methodist][^eh1982-95]. CRANBROOK, now the tune of On Ilkla Moor, was also sung to it [^ilkley][^hymnary-text].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings WINCHESTER OLD, as Hymns Ancient and Modern prints it [^am1875]. In the earliest known printing of the tune, in 1592, it was the tenor that carried it, marked with a printed pointing hand [^este1592]. With two singers, take soprano and alto; a third singer adds the bass, and a fourth the tenor.
