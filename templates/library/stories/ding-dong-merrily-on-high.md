Ding Dong Merrily on High, printed as "Ding dong! merrily on high", is an English Christmas carol in which the church bells on earth answer the angels' song in heaven [^ccb1924][^hymnary-text]. George Ratcliffe Woodward, a Church of England priest, wrote its three verses, and their earliest known printing is The Cambridge Carol Book of 1924, which he edited with the composer Charles Wood [^ccb1924][^canterbury-ddm][^hymnary-woodward]. The tune is more than three centuries older: the "Branle de l'Official", a dance air in Thoinot Arbeau's dance manual Orchésographie, printed at Langres in France in 1589 [^orch1589][^spck][^canterbury-ddm]. After every verse comes "Gloria, Hosanna in excelsis!", and in Wood's setting the tune carries its first syllable across 31 notes [^spck]. Hymnary.org finds it in 20 hymnals [^hymnary-text].

## Where it comes from

**The dancing canon.** Thoinot Arbeau is an anagram of Jehan Tabourot, a canon of the cathedral at Langres [^grove-orch][^pittaway][^canterbury-ddm]. He was born in Dijon in 1520, by most accounts, and died in 1595 [^kendall][^pittaway][^canterbury-ddm]. Grove's dictionary says he died aged 76, which would put his birth a year or so earlier [^grove-orch]. His Orchésographie is a dialogue in which Arbeau teaches dancing to a young man named Capriol [^orch1589][^grove-orch]. Its title page reads "Par Thoinot arbeau demeurant a Lengres", by Thoinot Arbeau, living at Langres, with a motto from Ecclesiastes 3, "Tempus plangendi, & tempus saltandi", a time to mourn and a time to dance [^orch1589][^kjv]. It was printed by Jehan des Preyz, whose shop stood near the church of Saint Mammès, and dated M. D. LXXXIX, 1589 [^orch1589]. For each dance the book prints the tune on a staff that runs down the page, with every step written beside the note it goes with [^orch1589][^czerwinski].

**A new dance.** Capriol has noticed that good company usually begins its dancing with branles, and Arbeau explains that branles are danced sideways, not walking forward [^orch1589]. Of the Branle de l'Official he says it has not been in fashion for long [^orch1589][^czerwinski]. It goes in duple time with little hops: a double step to the left and one to the right, repeated, then six single steps always to the left. At the end of them the players make the cadence, and the men take the women by the waist and throw them leaping into the air, to come down on the cadence, while keeping their own feet planted to hold them up [^orch1589][^czerwinski]. Capriol finds it hard work: dancing it well, he says, depends partly on the nimbleness and good cheer of the young woman lifted [^orch1589]. The book doesn't explain the name, "the official's branle". One study of the book suggests it may be a joke on Tabourot's own church office, since "official" was a French word for a bishop's vicar general, his post; another writer thinks it probably refers to household servants [^kendall][^pittaway].

**1588 or 1589?** At the back of the book, an "Extraict du Privilege" gives Jehan des Preyz the sole right to print "l'Orchésographie", under the name of Thoinot Arbeau, for six years, by royal letters given at Blois on 22 November 1588 [^orch1589][^grove-orch]. The title page is dated 1589 [^orch1589][^pittaway]. So both years are in the book: 1588 is the year of the privilege, 1589 the year of printing. Wood's music page and the Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology give 1588; Grove's dictionary gives 1589, the year on the title page, which we use [^spck][^canterbury-ddm][^grove-orch][^orch1589].

**A dance, not a carol.** Music editions often credit the tune only as a "16th-century French" tune or melody, which is true but says nothing of what it was for [^musicanet][^cyber]. Its earliest known printing is a dance tune for instruments, with steps and no words, in a book about dancing [^orch1589][^czerwinski]. It is known as a Christmas carol only from 1924, when The Cambridge Carol Book printed it with Woodward's English words [^ccb1924][^canterbury-ddm][^pittaway].

**Woodward and Wood.** George Ratcliffe Woodward was born in Birkenhead in 1848 and studied at Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, taking his degree in 1872 [^canterbury-woodward][^hymnary-woodward]. He was ordained in 1874 and served parishes in London and Norfolk [^canterbury-woodward][^hymnary-woodward][^londonrem]. He translated many hymns from Greek, Latin and German, holding that a translation should keep the sense of the original and its meter and rhyme as well [^hymnary-woodward]. In the autumn of 1901 he put out The Cowley Carol Book, 39 carols for the church of St John the Evangelist at Cowley, enlarged in a second edition; Charles Wood helped harmonise its tunes and checked the music's proofs, and in 1919 the two published a second series together [^cowley1912][^canterbury-carols][^hymnary-woodward][^cowley2]. In 1910 he edited Piae Cantiones, a songbook first printed in 1582 [^hymnary-woodward][^woodward1910]. Wood was born in Armagh in 1866, came to Caius in 1889 as organ scholar, lectured in harmony and counterpoint at Cambridge from 1897, and became the university's Professor of Music in 1924 [^canterbury-wood][^cod-wood][^cai2016].

**The Cambridge Carol Book, 1924.** Together they made The Cambridge Carol Book, published in 1924 by the Society for Promoting Christian Knowledge (S.P.C.K.): words mostly by Woodward, music mostly harmonised by Wood [^canterbury-ccb][^hymnary-woodward][^ccb1924]. Its title promises "Fifty-two Songs for Christmas, Easter, and Other Seasons"; the Canterbury Dictionary counts 53, 34 of them for Christmas [^ccb1924][^canterbury-ccb]. The words edition sold for threepence; on the back of its title page it advertises the music edition, 68 pages, in paper or cloth, and says "Printed in Great Britain 1924". The Bodleian Library's copy is stamped 6 February 1925 [^ccb1924]. On page 10, between the last lines of No. VII and "From Galilee they came", No. VIII is "Ding dong! merrily on high": three verses of two long lines, "io" in italics, and the Latin line printed once, in italics, after the third verse [^ccb1924].

**The music page.** The music we read is a single undated S.P.C.K. page of the same setting. Under the heading it says "Tune, Branle de l'Official from Thoinot Arbeau's Orchésographie, 1588 (C.W.)", and it is signed at the foot "G. R. Woodward" [^spck]. Four voices share two staves in B-flat. Verse 1 is printed under the treble staff, verses 2 and 3 under the bass, and the Gloria sits between repeat marks, so it is sung twice after every verse, with one long slur carrying "Glo-" across more than five bars [^spck]. Its words differ from the words edition in two small places, "priest" for "priests" and "Song" for "song" [^spck][^ccb1924]. IMSLP catalogues the page as "[1910]" and gives 1910 for the carol's first publication, but the page carries no date, and the book itself is dated 1924 [^spck][^ccb1924]. The reference books give 1924 as the carol's first publication [^canterbury-ddm][^hymnary-woodward][^pittaway].

**The last years.** Wood died in Cambridge on 12 July 1926, two years after the book [^canterbury-wood][^cmt][^cod-wood]. Woodward died at Highgate, London, on 3 March 1934 [^canterbury-woodward][^londonrem].

## What the words mean

The carol tells of two choirs of bells and voices: the angels in heaven at Christ's birth, and the ringers and singers of a parish church on earth, joining in [^ccb1924][^hymnary-text]. Woodward wrote it in deliberately old-fashioned English, and said why. In the preface to the second series of Cowley carols, made with Wood, he wrote that if some of his new carol words had "an old-world ring", no apology was needed: it seemed only fitting "that the words should be in keeping with the somewhat antiquated tunes whereto they have here been wedded" [^cowley2]. The Canterbury Dictionary's history of English carols notes the old-fashioned wording of his carols too [^canterbury-carols].

**Verse 1.** In heaven the bells ring "merrily on high", and "verily", truly, the sky is "riv'n", torn open, with angels singing [^webster1913]. It is Luke's "multitude of the heavenly host praising God", the passage Hymnary.org links to the carol [^kjv][^hymnary-text].

**Verse 2.** "E'en so here below": even so, down here on earth, let the steeple bells be swung, and let priests and people sing "io". "Io" is an old cry of joy or triumph, from Latin, and the Canterbury Dictionary hears it as a form of "eia", another cry of rejoicing [^webster1913][^canterbury-ddm]. "Swungen" and "sungen" are swung and sung with the old ending "-en" that English once gave the past participles of strong verbs, as broken and trodden still have it [^webster1913]. The same spellings are found in Old English poems, "swungen" in Andreas and "sungen" in Elene [^clasp]. Here they rhyme with each other and give the verse an old sound.

**Verse 3.** The ringers are asked to "prime", make ready, their "Matin chime", the bells for morning prayer; Prime is also the name of an early morning hour of prayer, the one after Lauds [^webster1913]. The singers are asked to "rime", the old spelling of rhyme, their "Eve-time song", their song at evening [^webster1913]. Morning and evening, the parish keeps up the angels' praise.

**The Latin.** The refrain mixes Latin into an English song, as many medieval carols did: In dulci jubilo, for one, takes turns between Latin and German [^canterbury-carols]. "Gloria" is glory, the first word of the angels' song in the Latin Bible, "Gloria in altissimis Deo", and of the hymn that begins with their words at Mass, "Gloria in excelsis Deo", glory to God in the highest [^vulgate][^gloria-ce][^kjv]. "Hosanna in excelsis", hosanna in the highest, is the crowd's shout on Palm Sunday in Matthew's Gospel, and is said twice in the Sanctus of every Mass [^kjv][^hosanna-ce]. "Hosanna" itself comes from the Hebrew of Psalm 118, "save now" [^hosanna-ce][^kjv]. Woodward joins the angels' word to the crowd's: glory, and hosanna in the highest.

### The full words

These are the three verses and the refrain as the words edition of The Cambridge Carol Book (1924) prints them, No. VIII, page 10, with its spelling and capitals [^ccb1924]. The refrain is printed there once, after verse 3; the music page repeats it after every verse, and we sing it twice each time [^spck]. Woodward died in 1934, so all of it is in the public domain.

**Verse 1**
Ding dong! merrily on high in heav'n the bells are ringing:
Ding dong! verily the sky is riv'n with Angel singing.

**Verse 2**
E'en so here below, below, let steeple bells be swungen,
And io, io, io, by priests and people sungen.

**Verse 3**
Pray you, dutifully prime your Matin chime, ye ringers;
May you beautifully rime your Eve-time song, ye singers:

**Refrain**
Gloria, Hosanna in excelsis!

## How it is sung

It is a Christmas carol, in 7.7.7.7 with a refrain: each verse is four lines of seven syllables, printed as two long lines [^hymnary-text][^ccb1924]. Its tune name is BRANLE DE L'OFFICIAL, spelled several ways, and some books call it FRENCH MELODY [^hymnary-text]. On Wood's page each verse is sung to the same four bars twice, and the Gloria follows in eight bars between repeat marks: the tune comes down from its highest note in a run of quavers, the same six-note turn five times over, each a step lower, all on the one syllable "Glo-" [^spck].

Our arrangement is Wood's harmony from that page, sung a tone lower, in A-flat, with the lower parts made simpler for beginners: the tune is his note for note, and so is the bass in the verses. We sing all three verses, each followed by the Gloria twice, about two and a half minutes in all. It is for 2 to 4 singers: two take soprano and alto, a third adds the bass, and the tenor makes four.

## Did you know?

- Charles Wood wrote bell music too: the chimes of the clock at Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge. Their tune grows from the opening of his part-song It Was an English Ladye Bright [^cod-wood][^cai2016][^copley1972].
- Woodward and Wood were both Caius men: Woodward took his degree there in 1872, and Wood came as organ scholar in 1889 and stayed until his death [^canterbury-woodward][^hymnary-woodward][^cod-wood][^cai2016].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/ding-dong-merrily-on-high/soprano): the tune, from low E-flat to top E-flat, most often on A-flat. The verses stay low, never above B-flat: each line runs down in quick notes from "mer-" to a held low E-flat on "high", "sky", "low,", "o,", "prime" and "rime". The Gloria starts on the top E-flat and carries "Glo-" across 31 notes, the same six-note turn five times, each a step lower. Breathe where the guide does, inside the long first notes of the Gloria's fourth and sixth bars, and go on with "(o)-" and "ri-".
- [Alto](/songs/ding-dong-merrily-on-high/alto): mostly a sixth under the tune in the verses, most often on C, with its low G on the held "high" and "sky" (and "low,", "o,", "prime", "rime") and on the Gloria's "a,". In the Gloria it comes in a beat after the tune each time and mostly holds one note a chord; its one run, quavers from A-flat down to E-flat in the Gloria's second bar, is Wood's own, and the only place it reaches its top A-flat.
- [Tenor](/songs/ding-dong-merrily-on-high/tenor): a narrow part, from low D-flat to B-flat, most often on A-flat. Each verse line starts on E-flat for "Ding dong!", dips to the low D-flat on the quick syllables before the held note ("ly on", "ly the" in verse 1), and touches the top B-flat on "are" and "gel" (in verse 1). In the Gloria it comes in a beat after the tune, rocks between B-flat and A-flat for two bars, then after one G holds F for five beats while the tune runs above it.
- [Bass](/songs/ding-dong-merrily-on-high/bass): from low G up to F, most often on A-flat and E-flat. Each verse line starts on low A-flat, climbs to D-flat, drops to B-flat, settles on E-flat under the held "high", and ends on low A-flat. In the Gloria it comes in a beat after the tune on its lowest note, G, and moves mostly one note a beat; its top F comes on "the" and "with" in verse 1, and in the Gloria once under the long "Glo-" and on "na".

## Questions

### What does Ding Dong Merrily on High mean?

It is a carol about bells at Christmas. In verse 1 the bells of heaven ring and the sky is "riv'n", torn open, by the angels' song; in verse 2 the church bells on earth answer, and priests and people sing "io", a cry of joy; in verse 3 the ringers and singers keep it up morning and evening [^ccb1924][^webster1913]. The Latin refrain means "Glory, hosanna in the highest" [^gloria-ce][^hosanna-ce].

### What year was Ding Dong Merrily on High written?

The earliest known printing of the words is 1924, in The Cambridge Carol Book, edited by George Ratcliffe Woodward and Charles Wood [^ccb1924][^canterbury-ddm][^hymnary-woodward]. IMSLP gives 1910, a date the music page it holds doesn't carry, and no earlier printing is known [^spck]. The tune is much older: it is a dance air in Thoinot Arbeau's Orchésographie, whose title page is dated 1589, under a royal privilege of 1588 [^orch1589][^grove-orch].

### How many notes are in the Gloria of Ding Dong Merrily on High?

In Charles Wood's setting the tune sings 31 notes on the first syllable, "Glo-", before "ri-a, Ho-san-na in ex-cel-sis!" adds nine more: 40 in all [^spck]. The 31 run down from the tune's highest note, the same six-note turn five times, each a step lower, then one more. The Gloria is sung twice after every verse, so in three verses the tune sings it six times.

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, the Branle de l'Official, note for note as Wood's page prints it, a tone lower [^spck]. The alto mostly follows a sixth below it in the verses. With two singers, take soprano and alto; a third adds the bass, and a fourth the tenor.
