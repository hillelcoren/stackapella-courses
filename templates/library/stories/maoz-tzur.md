Maoz Tzur is a Hebrew hymn for Hanukkah. In Ashkenazi homes it is sung once the Hanukkah lights are kindled, and it is sung in the synagogue too.[^je][^ej] Its poet, Mordecai, wrote it in the German lands in the Middle Ages and left his name in the first letters of its stanzas.[^je][^ej] The hymn walks through four rescues, from Egypt, from Babylon, from Haman's plot in Persia and from the Greeks in the days of the Hasmoneans, and it asks for the Temple to be rebuilt.[^ej][^melamed] Its bright tune became so much the sound of the festival that other Hanukkah hymns and prayers are sung to it as well.[^je][^ej] English-speaking congregations also sing it as Rock of Ages.[^ej][^uh1897]

## Where it comes from

Almost nothing is known of the poet but his name. The first letters of the first five stanzas spell Mordecai in Hebrew.[^je][^ej] The stanzas open Maoz, Ra'ot, Dvir, Krot and Yevanim: mem, resh, dalet, kaf, yod.[^id1912] The scholar Zunz placed the poet in the middle of the thirteenth century, in Germany; a 2016 article puts him a little earlier, in the late twelfth or early thirteenth century.[^je][^ej][^melamed] Some scholars have identified him with Mordecai ben Isaac, who wrote the Sabbath table song Mah Yafit.[^je][^ej] The hymn belonged first to the home, sung after the lights were kindled, and only later came into the synagogue.[^je][^idel29]

A sixth stanza, "Chasof zeroa", pleads for the redemption to come near and for God to avenge His servants. Most prayer books leave it out.[^je][^ej] Its first letters spell chazak, "be strong".[^ej][^melamed] It is not known in print until centuries after the hymn was written, so some scholars think it was added later; others, the Encyclopaedia Judaica among them, take the acrostic as a sign that it is the poet's own.[^ej][^melamed] Idelsohn's songbook prints five stanzas.[^id1912]

The tune has no known composer. Its earliest known printing is in Isaac Nathan's Hebrew Melodies, published in London in 1815, where it carries Lord Byron's poem "On Jordan's Banks".[^ej][^je] The Jewish Encyclopedia reports that the Königsberg cantor Eduard Birnbaum identified it as an adaptation of an old German folk song, "So weiss ich eins, dass mich erfreut, das pluemlein auff preiter heyde": "I know a thing that gladdens me, the little flower on the wide heath".[^je][^idel29] Martin Luther set that folk melody to his hymn "Nun freut euch, lieben Christen gmein", written in 1523.[^je][^idel29][^lh1941]

In his history of Jewish music of 1929, A. Z. Idelsohn found only the first four and last two bars of Luther's chorale in Maoz Tzur. The middle bars, he wrote, recall the Benzenauer, a German battle song. He thought a Jewish singer had joined the two German songs into a single Hanukkah tune.[^idel29] The Encyclopaedia Judaica answers that the likeness Birnbaum and Idelsohn saw, to early Protestant chorales and a German soldiers' song, rests on isolated motifs, and it finds a closer match for the whole line in a Latin church melody.[^ej] Idelsohn himself, in his songbook of 1911 to 1912, headed the tune neima mekubala, "a received tune". His preface gives that name to foreign tunes the people took in and made their own.[^id1912] They disagree on its age too. The Jewish Encyclopedia of 1904 says the folk song was widely known among German Jews as early as 1450, Idelsohn that in 1450 the poem was still sung to another melody, and the Encyclopaedia Judaica dates the tune we sing to around the early fifteenth century.[^je][^idel29][^ej]


### Timeline

- 13th century: Mordecai writes the poem in the German lands, by Zunz's dating; a 2016 article says the late 12th or early 13th century. [^je][^melamed]
- 1523: Martin Luther writes "Nun freut euch, lieben Christen gmein", the chorale later compared with Maoz Tzur's tune. [^idel29][^lh1941]
- 1724: Benedetto Marcello, in Venice, prints a different melody, one the Ashkenazi Jews of Venice sang to Maoz Tzur. [^je][^ej][^idel29]
- 1815: Isaac Nathan's Hebrew Melodies, London, has the earliest known printing of the tune sung today, to Byron's words. [^ej][^je]
- 1897: The Union Hymnal of the Central Conference of American Rabbis prints the tune with the English hymn "Rock of Ages, let our song". [^uh1897][^je]
- 1911 to 1912: A. Z. Idelsohn's Sefer ha-Shirim, a Hebrew songbook for schools dated 5672, prints it in two and three voices; our arrangement is read from this page. [^id1912]

## What the words mean

The title is the hymn's first words. Maoz is a stronghold or refuge, tzur is a rock, and yeshuati is "my salvation": God is called the stronghold and rock of the singer's rescue.[^ej] The phrase echoes Isaiah, who scolds Israel for forgetting "the Rock of thy stronghold".[^isaiah][^ej]

We sing stanzas 1 and 5.

**Stanza 1 (sung).** Stronghold, rock of my salvation, it is right to praise You. Let my house of prayer, the Temple, stand again, and there we will bring an offering of thanks. When You make ready the slaughter of the foe who barks at us, then I will finish, with a psalm, the dedication of the altar. Chanukat, "dedication", is the festival's own name: Hanukkah is the Feast of Dedication.[^je][^melamed]

**Stanza 2.** My soul had its fill of troubles, my life made bitter by hard labor under Egypt. With His great hand God brought out His treasured people, and Pharaoh's army sank like a stone into the deep.[^id1912][^ej]

**Stanza 3.** God brought me to His holy house, but there too I found no rest. The oppressor came and drove me into exile, because I had served strange gods. I drank a cup of staggering wine and almost perished. Babylon fell, Zerubbabel came, and after seventy years I was saved.[^id1912][^ej]

**Stanza 4.** Haman, the Agagite, son of Hammedatha, plotted to cut down the tall cypress, but the plot became his own trap. God lifted up the Benjamite, Mordecai, blotted out the enemy's name, and hanged his many sons on the gallows. This is the Purim story.[^id1912][^ej]

**Stanza 5 (sung).** The Greeks gathered against me in the days of the Hasmoneans. They broke down the walls of my towers and made all the oils unclean. From what was left in the last of the jars, a miracle was done for the lilies, the people of Israel. Then the people of understanding set eight days for song and joyful praise.[^id1912][^talmud]

**Stanza 6 (not sung).** Show Your holy arm and bring the redemption near.[^ej][^melamed]

Our Jewish songs sing God's names in the substituted forms customary when singing outside prayer, but neither stanza we sing names God: the hymn speaks to God as "You", lecha.

## How it is sung

Maoz Tzur belongs to the Ashkenazi rite, where it follows the kindling of the Hanukkah lights, at home and in the synagogue.[^je][^ej] The Encyclopaedia Judaica notes that Reform and Conservative synagogues also sing Rock of Ages, in English, with the Hebrew or in its place.[^ej][^uh1897]

Our arrangement is read from page 77 of A. Z. Idelsohn's Sefer ha-Shirim, a songbook for Hebrew schools published in Berlin and Jerusalem and dated 5672, that is 1911 to 1912, where it is No. 83, in E major, in common time.[^id1912] We sing stanzas 1 and 5, for 2 to 4 singers, with four parts written on the harmony of his two and three voices. The words are in modern Israeli pronunciation, which fits his book: his preface faults earlier songbooks for using the Ashkenazi accent, which pupils in the East, who spoke Hebrew with the Sephardi accent, could not follow.[^id1912]

Each stanza sings its last line twice, as Idelsohn's repeat signs ask.[^id1912] The Encyclopaedia Judaica calls that repeat recent, absent from the standard sources of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries; Idelsohn's page is an early witness to it.[^ej][^id1912]

## Versions and other words

**Other tunes.** The Ashkenazi Jews of Venice sang Maoz Tzur to a melody of their own, which Benedetto Marcello printed there in 1724 and used as the theme of his Psalm 15.[^je][^ej][^idel29] The Encyclopaedia Judaica says it is still sung in Italy, and sometimes in Israel and the United States.[^ej]

**Other words.** In English the hymn is Rock of Ages, "Rock of Ages, let our song / Praise Thy saving power", as the Union Hymnal printed it in 1897.[^uh1897] The 1932 edition credits it to Marcus Jastrow and Gustav Gottheil, adapted from a German version by Leopold Stein; the Jewish Encyclopedia credits Gottheil alone.[^uh1932][^hymnary][^je]

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/maoz-tzur/soprano): the tune. It starts low, dipping to its low B on "Maoz" and "Tikon", and climbs in the third line to its top E, held for two beats on "matbeach,". Verse 5 does the same on "Yevanim" and "kankanim,".
- [Alto](/songs/maoz-tzur/alto): mostly a third or a sixth under the tune, on G-sharp more than anywhere. Listen for the end of every "hamizbeach." and "urenanim.", where the alto leaps up a fourth to G-sharp, a third above the tune's last note.
- [Tenor](/songs/maoz-tzur/tenor): the middle of the chord, most often on B, then E. Each time verse 1 ends on "chanukat hamizbeach." it holds its B for over three beats while the tune steps down above it to its last note; solo it in the player to hear that hold.
- [Bass](/songs/maoz-tzur/bass): mostly the roots of the chords, E just over half the time. It reaches up to A on "hamenabeach." and "naasa", and drops to its low A on every "chanukat hamizbeach." and "kav'u shir urenanim.".

## Questions

### What does "Maoz Tzur" mean?

"Stronghold, Rock", the hymn's first words, which go on "of my salvation, it is right to praise You". The words speak to God and echo Isaiah's "the Rock of thy stronghold"; the English version is called Rock of Ages.[^ej][^isaiah][^uh1897]

### Who wrote Maoz Tzur?

A poet named Mordecai, known only because he spelled his name in the first letters of the first five stanzas, in the German lands of the twelfth or thirteenth century. The tune is not his: Idelsohn writes that in 1450 the poem was still sung to another melody.[^je][^ej][^melamed][^idel29]

### Where does the Maoz Tzur tune come from?

No one knows who made it; its earliest known printing is from 1815. Scholars hear in it a German folk song that Luther also used, a German battle song, or a Latin church melody, and they disagree on its age, from the early fifteenth century to the sixteenth.[^ej][^je][^idel29][^melamed]

### Is Rock of Ages the same song as Maoz Tzur?

Yes and no. Rock of Ages is the English Hanukkah hymn sung to the Maoz Tzur tune, and Reform and Conservative synagogues sing it beside the Hebrew or in its place. It is not a line-by-line translation but an adaptation, credited to Marcus Jastrow and Gustav Gottheil, of a German version by Leopold Stein, and it departs a little from the Hebrew.[^uh1932][^hymnary][^ej][^uh1897]

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune throughout, as Idelsohn printed it. The alto sings with it, mostly a third or sixth below. With two singers it is soprano and alto, with three the bass joins, and with four the tenor fills the middle of each chord.[^id1912]
