Comin' Thro' the Rye, also printed as Coming Through the Rye and known by its first line, Gin a Body Meet a Body, is a Scots song about meeting someone on a path and stealing a kiss. Robert Burns reworked it from an older, much bawdier song, and it was printed in Edinburgh, in two sets, in the fifth volume of The Scots Musical Museum, which libraries date to 1796.[^smm][^dick][^mm1911] The second set, with the words "Ilka body has a body", is the one that became popular, and its tune belongs to the same family of Scottish dance tunes as Auld Lang Syne.[^graham][^dick][^glen] Its line about meeting a body in the rye later gave J. D. Salinger the title of The Catcher in the Rye.[^litcharts][^tearle]

## Where it comes from

**The two sets of 1796.** James Johnson, an Edinburgh engraver and music-seller, published The Scots Musical Museum in six volumes, and Burns gathered and wrote songs for it until his death.[^smm][^dick][^rpopoet] The fifth volume carries no date. The National Library of Scotland and the Burns scholar James C. Dick date it 1796, Dick to December of that year; in 1876 two writers in Notes and Queries, among them the English music historian William Chappell, put it in 1797.[^smm][^dick][^nq1876] On page 430 is No. 417, "Comin thro' the rye. 1st Sett", marked Very Slow, in G. Its chorus, "Oh Jenny's a' weet poor body, Jenny's seldom dry", is about Jenny, whose petticoat is soaked from the walk (weet is wet), and two short verses follow: "Gin a body kiss a body Need a body cry", and the same "thro' the glen", where the question is "Need the warld ken!", need the world know.[^smm][^dick-gloss] A letter B is engraved at the foot of the page, which Dick reads as Burns's signature.[^smm][^dick] On the next page, No. 418, the "2d Sett", is in B flat, also Very Slow. Here the familiar question gets a new answer: "Ilka body has a body, ne'er a ane hae I, But a' the lads they loe me, And what the war am I." Two more verses follow, "comin frae the well" and "comin frae the town".[^smm]

![[1]]

**The older song.** A correspondent in Notes and Queries in 1876 wrote that Burns took an old, well-known song and kept its Jenny verse unaltered; another thought the opening verse and its Jenny chorus were probably all that remained of the old song.[^nq1876] Dick says the refrain was very popular in Scotland in the 18th century, and that a private version is in The Merry Muses of Caledonia, the bawdy collection printed after Burns's death.[^dick][^mm1911] The 1911 reprint calls it an old song that Burns's "purified version completely superseded".[^mm1911]

**Who wrote the second set?** It is unsigned. One 1876 writer found no trustworthy evidence tying it to Burns, though it might owe something to his hand. Representative Poetry Online calls it unsigned "but widely accepted as his".[^nq1876][^rpo]

![[2]]

**The tune.** Burns wrote against the title "Tune, Miller's Wedding, a Strathspey", as Dick reads his manuscript list. A strathspey is a Scottish dance tune.[^dick][^ballad] Both Dick and the collector John Glen trace it to a tune printed as The Miller's Wedding in Robert Bremner's collection of reels of 1759; Glen adds that the strathspey The Miller's Daughter was taken from it.[^dick][^glen] William Stenhouse, whose notes were compiled to accompany the Museum, said the first set's air came from the third and fourth strains of The Miller's Daughter in Niel Gow's First Collection.[^stenhouse][^graham] Dick lists No. 418 among the variants of the Auld Lang Syne air, and Glen calls the first set "closely allied" to it.[^dick][^glen]

![[3]]

**Watlen's version.** Stenhouse wrote that the "Gin a body meet a body" song was "parodied from the first set, which was published as a single sheet song before it was copied into the Museum", and that John Watlen, an Edinburgh musician and music-seller, altered the first strain of the tune a little and published it with the new words. "His edition had a considerable run."[^stenhouse][^graham] Chappell, reading Stenhouse, wrote in 1876 that Watlen's edition came after the London pantomime described below. Glen agreed that No. 418 is Watlen's version, but worked out from the dates of Watlen's numbered series that it was printed in August 1794, more than a year before the pantomime.[^nq1876][^glen]

**Myth and record.** Often claimed in the 19th century: the song is English. Chappell argued in Popular Music of the Olden Time that it was altered for the Museum from "If a body meet a body going to the Fair", sung by a Mrs Henley in a London pantomime, Harlequin Mariner, in the Christmas season of 1795 to 1796, and entered at Stationers' Hall in June 1796: on the 29th, says Chappell, on the 6th, says Dick.[^chappell][^nq1876][^dick] What the record shows: Burns died on 21 July 1796, within weeks of that entry, and Dick answers that the Merry Muses version was much earlier and that Burns already knew much of the fifth volume.[^dick][^nq1876][^rpopoet]

## What the words mean

The song is in Scots, and the singer is a young woman, teasing.[^graham][^smm] We sing two verses as G. F. Graham printed them in Edinburgh in 1848. His text follows the Museum's second set, not Burns's first, and changes some words, as set out with the full words below.[^graham][^smm]

**Verse 1 (sung).** If one person meets another coming through the rye, and one kisses the other, need anyone cry out? Every girl has her young man, and people say I have none. Yet all the lads smile at me when I come through the rye. Gin means if, a body is a person, and ilka means each or every.[^graham][^dsl-body]

**Verse 2 (sung).** The same meeting, coming from the well: if someone kisses someone, need anyone tell? Ne'er a ane ha'e I is "never a one have I".[^rpo] Graham also prints a third verse, "comin' frae the town", in which the lads "lo'e me weel" (love me well) and the singer asks "what the waur am I?", meaning "what am I the worse for it?".[^graham][^dick-gloss][^dsl-waur] His footnote says "kiss" was often sung "greet". In Scots, greet usually means to weep, but here it seems to stand in for a polite greeting.[^graham][^dick-gloss]

**Field or stream?** Most readers take the rye as a field of rye grain, crossed by a narrow path. In 1876 a reader of Notes and Queries asked whether it might instead be the Rye, a stream in Ayrshire. Four replies in one issue took it up, and all four favoured the field. One explained that a path through standing rye after rain would soak a girl's skirts, as the song says.[^nq1876] There is a real Rye Water, a small tributary of the River Garnock that joins it near Dalry.[^groome][^art] In 1889 Helen Kendrick Johnson's Our Familiar Songs said the song was about fording "the little River Rye", and that custom allowed a lad to claim a kiss as a toll from any lass he met on the stepping-stones. She gave no source.[^johnson1889] The early printings do not say which is meant, and the words fit both.

**The bawdy versions.** The Merry Muses version keeps the "Gin a body meet a body" pattern, but replaces "kiss" with a coarse word for sex.[^mm1911][^dick] One 1876 writer said all the old versions "had more wit than decency".[^nq1876] Burns's printed song keeps the old Jenny chorus, in which draigl't means soaked with mud or water.[^smm][^dick-gloss][^dsl-draigle]

**The Catcher in the Rye.** In Salinger's novel, Holden Caulfield hears a small boy sing the line with "catch" for "meet", and pictures himself catching children in a field of rye before they fall. His sister Phoebe corrects him from Burns's poem.[^litcharts][^tearle]

### The full words

These are the words as G. F. Graham printed them in The Songs of Scotland, vol. 2 (Edinburgh, 1848), pp. 10 and 11: the verse under the music, then two more stanzas.[^graham] We sing the first two. Where the Museum's second set of 1796 has "Ilka body has a body" and "But a' the lads they loe me, And what the war am I", Graham has "Ilka lassie has her laddie" and, in his first two verses, the lads smiling "when comin' thro' the rye".[^smm][^graham] In his third, gloom means to frown. Graham adds that Miss Stephens, a singer, used to close the song with four more lines, sung to the first part of the air, given last here; a swain is a young man or sweetheart, whaur his hame is "where his home is", and dinna is "do not".[^graham] Burns's own first set, the Jenny song, is described above.

**Verse 1**
Gin a body meet a body
Comin' thro' the rye,
Gin a body kiss a body,
Need a body cry?
Ilka lassie has her laddie,
Nane, they say, ha'e I!
Yet a' the lads they smile at me,
When comin' thro' the rye.

**Verse 2**
Gin a body meet a body
Comin' frae the well,
Gin a body kiss a body,
Need a body tell?
Ilka lassie has her laddie,
Ne'er a ane ha'e I;
But a' the lads they smile on me
When comin' thro' the rye.

**Verse 3**
Gin a body meet a body
Comin' frae the town,
Gin a body greet a body,
Need a body gloom.
Ilka lassie has her laddie,
Nane they say ha'e I;
But a' the lads they lo'e me weel,
And what the waur am I?

**Miss Stephens's ending**
Amang the train there is a swain
I dearly lo'e mysel';
But whaur his hame, or what his name,
I dinna care to tell.

## How it is sung

It is a Scottish song for one voice with keyboard: the Museum prints two staves, and Graham a piano part by T. M. Mudie, Moderato at 72 beats a minute, in G.[^smm][^graham] Graham chose the second set because, he wrote, it was "the most popular, as well as the most characteristic".[^graham]

![[4]]

Our arrangement is in G major, in 4/4, in our own four parts. The soprano sings Graham's tune with every pitch as printed, leaving out two small grace notes, and every rhythm but one: the long "I!" is cut short to leave a breath before "Yet". It goes at 60 beats a minute, a little slower than his 72. Mudie's piano part was used only to check the chords. Two singers sing soprano and alto, and a third adds the bass.

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/comin-thro-the-rye/soprano): you sing the tune, with its Scotch snaps, a short note then a long one, on "com-in'", "las-sie" and "lad-die". Both "Gin a body" lines start on a low D, and the second jumps an octave to the high D on "bo-" of its first "body", just before "kiss". "Nane, they say, ha'e I!" climbs to your top E on "I!" and "Yet"; then "smile" and "at" take two notes each, sliding down from C to G.
- [Alto](/songs/comin-thro-the-rye/alto): you sing mostly in thirds and sixths under the tune, and end each "Gin a body" line on a low B ("rye", "cry?", "well", "tell?"). In the third line you climb to your top B on "Nane" ("Ne'er" in verse 2) and hold G through "they say, ha'e I!", then sing F-sharp for "smile at me" while the tune slides down above you.
- [Tenor](/songs/comin-thro-the-rye/tenor): you fill in the chord, mostly on G and A, and each "Gin a body" line falls to your low D on "rye", "cry?", "well" and "tell?". The third line is your high point: you rise to D on "die," and hold it through "Nane, they say, ha'e", settling on C for "I!", then touch D once more on "a' the".
- [Bass](/songs/comin-thro-the-rye/bass): you sing the low line on just five notes, G, A, B, C and D, and start and end each "Gin a body" line on the low G, moving up to D under "meet a bo-" and "kiss a bo-" and back to G on "-dy". Your only A comes on "lads they", just before D for "smile at me".

## Questions

### Who wrote the original poem Comin' Thro' the Rye?

No one knows who wrote the first version. Robert Burns reworked an older, bawdier Scots song, and The Scots Musical Museum printed his text in 1796. The second set, "Ilka body has a body", the words most people know, was printed unsigned on the next page, and writers disagree on whether it is his.[^smm][^dick][^mm1911][^nq1876][^johnson1889]

### What does "comin thro the rye" mean?

It means walking along a path through a field of rye grain, in the reading most writers give. The singer asks whether two people who meet there and kiss need tell anyone. Another reading makes the Rye a stream with stepping-stones and a toll of kisses, but the earliest sources we found for it, of 1876 and 1889, give no evidence.[^nq1876][^johnson1889][^graham]

### Is Comin' Thro' the Rye the same tune as Auld Lang Syne?

Not the same, but related. Both grew from the strathspey printed as The Miller's Wedding in 1759; James C. Dick counts them as variants of one air, and John Glen calls them closely allied. Chappell wrote in 1876 that George Thomson chose this air for Burns's Auld Lang Syne.[^dick][^glen][^nq1876]
