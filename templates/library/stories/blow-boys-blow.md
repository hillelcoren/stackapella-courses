Blow, Boys, Blow, also printed as "Blow My Bully Boys", "Blow, Bullies, Blow" and "A Yankee Ship", is a sea shanty: a work song sailors sang to pull together on a rope [^terry][^smith][^masefield]. It is a halliard shanty, sung while hoisting the sails: the shantyman sings a line alone, and the crew answer "Blow, boys, blow" and "Blow, my bully boys, blow" [^terryscan][^bullen][^masefield]. In the words we sing, a Yankee ship comes down the river with her masts and yards shining like silver, and the crew are fed on bullock's lights and weevilled bread [^terryscan]. No one knows who made it. Richard Terry wrote in 1921 that every shanty collection he knew included it, and Cecil Sharp had written in 1914 that all the chief collections did [^terry][^sharp].

## Where it comes from

**A slaving song first?** The master mariner W. B. Whall, who printed the shanties he had learnt at sea, believed this one began on the slave ships of West Africa [^whall][^terry]. His version opens on the Congo River [^whall]. He judged it at least sixty years old, probably more, and said shantymen had long since begun at "A Yankee ship comes down the river" instead [^whall]. Joanna Colcord, a sea captain's daughter raised on his ship, also called it a slaving song at first, opening on the Congo River [^colcord][^winick]. Colcord knew Whall's book, and not everyone agrees: a Library of Congress folklorist writes that opinions are divided, some scholars holding that the slaving verses came first and others that they came later [^colcord][^winick2024]. Whall prints racist verses, and Colcord's account of that early version uses a racist slur; we do not print them [^whall][^colcord].

**The Yankee packets.** Colcord wrote that the crews of the Atlantic packet ships then took it over and sang of the brutal life aboard [^colcord]. A packet was a ship that sailed on fixed days with mail, passengers and goods [^webster][^clark]. The packets were known for hard discipline: a history of the clipper ships says "belaying pin soup" was first served on the packets of the Black Ball Line [^clark]. Terry's note on this shanty agrees: spotless decks, and masts and yards that shone like silver, marked a Yankee packet, and they were kept that way by a terrible discipline "in which the belaying pin was a gruesome factor" [^terry]. A belaying pin is the short wooden or iron bar the ropes are made fast to [^smyth].

![[1]]

**Dandy Jim and the minstrel stage.** The skipper's name changed from singer to singer [^whall][^colcord][^balladindex]. In the earliest printing we have seen, Laura Alexandrine Smith's *The Music of the Waters* (1888), and again in Terry's, he is "Dandy Jim", and Smith has him "from old Carolina" [^smith][^terryscan]. That is the name, and nearly the home, of the title character of "Dandy Jim of Caroline", a blackface minstrel song in print by 1844, its sheet music naming the Ethiopian Serenaders and the Virginia Serenaders [^levy][^penn]. Blackface minstrelsy was a stage show in which white performers darkened their faces to mock Black people. Terry wrote that minstrel songs were often pressed into service as shanties [^terry]. Frank Bullen and W. F. Arnold's book of 1914 spells the first line in a mock Black dialect [^bullen]. Shanty singing as a whole owed much to Black singers: Colcord called Black American sailors the best singers who ever led a shanty [^colcord][^winick].

**Into the books.** John Masefield printed a version in *A Sailor's Garland* (we read the 1908 edition) about a Black Ball barque, her captain "bully Hains" and her hard-driving mate [^masefield]. The age of the shanty was over by then: by the end of the 1870s, Terry wrote, steam had driven the sailing ship from the seas, and steam winches did the work the crews had sung to [^terry][^bullen].

**The page we read.** Our version is No. 15 in Richard Runciman Terry's *The Shanty Book, Part I* (1921), "Blow my bully boys", headed "Halliard shanty" [^terryscan]. Terry, director of music at Westminster Cathedral from 1901, grew up in Northumberland hearing shanties from his sailor uncles [^terry][^hymnary][^oxford]. The page is in F major and 4/4, with a piano part. "SOLO." and "CHORUS." take turns line by line, and accents mark the first "Blow" and the last "blow" of the first answer. Verses 2 to 8 follow in type, the third marked "(twice)" [^terryscan].

### Timeline

- 1888: Laura Alexandrine Smith prints "A Yankee Ship" with its tune, the earliest printing we have seen [^smith][^terry]
- 1908: John Masefield's *A Sailor's Garland* (first edition 1906; we read the second) prints "Blow, Bullies, Blow" among the halliard shanties [^masefield]
- 1913: W. B. Whall's *Ships, Sea Songs and Shanties* (earlier editions 1910 and 1912; we read the third) prints it as a slaving song [^whall][^terry]
- 1914: Cecil Sharp prints John Short's version, and Bullen and Arnold print theirs among the halliard chanties [^sharp][^bullen]
- 1921: Richard Terry prints "Blow my bully boys", the version we sing [^terryscan]
- 1924: Joanna Colcord prints an American version in *Roll and Go* [^colcord]

## What the words mean

Each verse is two solo lines from the shantyman, most often a question and its answer, with the crew's reply after each line [^terryscan]. We sing six of Terry's eight verses: his 1, 2, 5, 6, 7 and 8, which are our verses 1 to 6. Below, verses go by Terry's numbers.

**The chorus.** "Bully" is old slang for fine or first-rate, so "my bully boys" means "my fine lads" [^webster]. None of the books we read says what "blow" means in this shanty. In verse 7 the shantyman turns it to the weather: blow together, boys, and blow for better weather.

**Verses 1 and 2.** A Yankee ship, an American one, comes down the river [^webster]. Her masts and yards, the spars that carry the sails, shine like silver, the mark of a strictly run packet [^smyth][^terry]. How do we know she is a Yankee packet? She flies the American flag.

**Verses 5 and 6.** The food. "Lights" are an animal's lungs, so dinner is a bullock's lungs and a donkey's liver [^webster]. Supper is weevilled bread. At sea "bread" meant biscuit, flat cakes of flour and water baked hard, and weevils were the beetles that bored into it [^smyth]. No book we read explains "Yankee leather"; Smith's version of 1888 has it too [^smith].

**The verses we leave out.** Terry's verse 3 asks who was skipper of her. His verse 4 answers with the name Dandy Jim, the name of the minstrel song above, and a racist slur [^terryscan]. We do not sing either.

### The full words

These are the words of No. 15, "Blow my bully boys", in Richard Runciman Terry's *The Shanty Book, Part I* (1921), pages 32 and 33, with his spelling and numbering and the crew's answers written out after each solo line [^terryscan]. Terry prints his verse 3 as one line sung twice. His verse 4 uses a racist slur, so we leave it out and keep his numbers: the verses run 1, 2, 3, then 5 to 8. The words are traditional, printed in 1921, and Terry died in 1938, so they are in the public domain [^hymnary][^oxford].

**Verse 1**
A Yankee ship came down the river,
Blow, boys blow.
Her masts and yards they shine like silver.
Blow my bully boys blow.

**Verse 2**
And how d'ye know she's a Yankee packet?
Blow, boys blow.
The Stars and Stripes they fly above her.
Blow my bully boys blow.

**Verse 3**
And who d'ye think was skipper of her.
Blow, boys blow.
And who d'ye think was skipper of her.
Blow my bully boys blow.

**Verse 5**
And what d'ye think they had for dinner?
Blow, boys blow.
Why bullock's lights and donkey's liver.
Blow my bully boys blow.

**Verse 6**
And what d'ye think they had for supper?
Blow, boys blow.
Why weevilled bread and Yankee leather.
Blow my bully boys blow.

**Verse 7**
Then blow my boys, and blow together.
Blow, boys blow.
And blow my boys for better weather.
Blow my bully boys blow.

**Verse 8**
A Yankee ship came down the river.
Blow, boys blow.
Her masts and yards they shine like silver.
Blow my bully boys blow.

## How it is sung

It is a halliard shanty: Terry, Bullen and Arnold, and Masefield file it with the halliard shanties, and Sharp with the pulling chanteys [^terryscan][^bullen][^masefield][^sharp]. Halliards hoist a sail on its yard, and the same Library of Congress folklorist calls the halyard shanties probably the largest class of hauling songs [^smyth][^winick]. Terry describes the type used for the long hoist: two short choruses with a double pull in each, the solo lines giving the crew time to shift their hands [^terry]. Sharp marks the pulls with arrowheads, two in each answer, on its first and last "blow" [^sharp]. Smith's version keeps the work in its words: "up aloft that yard must go", "one more pull and then belay", make the rope fast [^smith][^smyth].

![[4]]

Our arrangement sings six verses for 2 to 4 singers, in G, a whole tone above Terry's F. The soprano is the shantyman and sings Terry's voice part note for note, both solo lines of every verse alone, then the answers with everyone. The alto, tenor and bass sing only the answers, one note a syllable, on the chords of Terry's piano part, so the harmony is Terry's, not the ships' [^terryscan].

## Versions and other words

- **Whall's slaving version (1913)**: it begins "Oh, blow, my boys, I long to hear you", and the skipper may be "Bully Semmes of Alabama" or "One-eyed Kelly the Bowery Runner" [^whall].
- **Smith's version (1888)**: dinner is monkey's lights and donkey's liver [^smith].
- **Sharp's "Blow, Boys, Come Blow Together" (1914)**: John Short's version, where the master is "Bully Brag of New York City" and supper is "belaying-pin soup and a roll in the gutter" [^sharp].
- **Colcord's version (1924)**: Bully Hayes is captain and dinner is pickled eels' feet and bullock's liver; she adds a couplet she dates to the Civil War, about breaking the embargo [^colcord].
- **The rough verses.** Whall's, Smith's, Terry's and Colcord's versions all name the skipper or mates in racist terms; we do not print them [^whall][^smith][^terryscan][^colcord].

## Did you know?

- Colcord believed her Bully Hayes was the Captain Hayes lost with the clipper Rainbow in 1848, a loss that a history of the clipper ships also records [^colcord][^clark].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/blow-boys-blow/soprano): you are the shantyman. Sing both solo lines of every verse alone, mostly on B and G. Each verse starts on your low D ("A", "And" or "Then") and leaps straight up to B; each second solo line starts on your top E ("Her", "The", "Why" or "And"). Then lead the answers with everyone.
- [Alto](/songs/blow-boys-blow/alto): you rest through the solo lines and come in on the answers, the same in every verse. "Blow, boys blow." rises E, G-sharp, A, your top note; "Blow my bully boys blow." falls G, F-sharp, D to a low B on the last "blow".
- [Tenor](/songs/blow-boys-blow/tenor): you rest through the solo lines and answer in the middle of the chord, mostly on G and A. In verse 1 you start "Blow, boys blow." on your top C; in every verse after, on your low E.
- [Bass](/songs/blow-boys-blow/bass): you rest through the solo lines and sing the bass of Terry's chords: C, E, A under "Blow, boys blow.", with your top E on "boys", then D for "Blow my bully boys" and down to a low G on the last "blow".

## Questions

### What does "Blow, my bully boys, blow" mean?

"My bully boys" means "my fine lads": "bully" was slang for fine or first-rate [^webster]. It is the crew's answer to the shantyman, and Sharp marks two pulls in it, for hoisting the sail [^sharp]. No book we read says what "blow" meant here; in Terry's verse 7 the crew blow for better weather [^terryscan].

### Who wrote Blow, Boys, Blow?

No one knows; it is a traditional shanty that sailors made and remade. The earliest printing we have seen is Laura Alexandrine Smith's in 1888, but it may be older in print: the Ballad Index gives 1874 as its earliest date, without naming the source [^smith][^balladindex]. W. B. Whall and Joanna Colcord believed it began as a slaving song on the Congo River, though opinions are divided [^whall][^colcord][^winick2024]. Our words and tune are Richard Terry's, printed in 1921 [^terryscan].

### Is "Blow, boys, blow for California" the same song?

No. That chorus belongs to "The Banks of the Sacramento", a gold-rush shanty that Masefield printed in 1908 and Bullen and Arnold in 1914 [^masefield][^bullen][^colcord]. Colcord noted that its tune is the minstrel song "Camptown Races", and Sharp listed "Doo-dah-day" among the street songs sailors made into shanties [^colcord][^sharp]. Blow, Boys, Blow has its own tune.

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano. In our arrangement the soprano is the shantyman, singing both solo lines of every verse alone and the tune of the answers; the alto, tenor and bass join only on the two answers, one note a syllable, on Terry's chords [^terryscan].
