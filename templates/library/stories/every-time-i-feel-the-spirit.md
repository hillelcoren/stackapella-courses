Every Time I Feel the Spirit, also printed as Ev'ry Time I Feel the Spirit, is an African American spiritual about prayer: every time the singer feels the Spirit moving in the heart, they pray [^hampton1909][^work1907]. No maker's name is known [^canterbury][^hymnarchive]. Its earliest known printing is from 1907, at Fisk University in Nashville, under the title "I Will Pray" [^work1907][^hymnarchive]. Two years later Hampton Institute in Virginia printed it with three different verses, about the Lord speaking on the mountain, everything shining around the singer and the chilly Jordan river; we sing the first two [^hampton1909][^hymnarchive]. The United Methodist Hymnal, The Presbyterian Hymnal and the Chalice Hymnal all print it, under the tune name PENTECOST [^hymnary-umh][^hymnary-ph1990][^hymnary-chalice].

## Where it comes from

**A song without a maker.** Who first sang it, and when, is not known. The Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology suggests its roots may lie in the South before the Civil War [^canterbury]. The earliest singing anyone reported was during the war, and no printing followed for about forty years [^hymnarchive].

**Myth and record.** Often told: during the Civil War, President Abraham Lincoln heard this song at a camp in Washington for people who had escaped slavery. The Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology calls it an often-cited report: a group led by Mary Dines, who had escaped slavery in Maryland, sang it for him, and Lincoln sang with them [^canterbury]. The Hymnology Archive tells it from Dines's own account: the President was moved to tears and came back another day to hear more [^hymnarchive]. What the record shows: the story is Dines's own memory, printed in 1942 in John E. Washington's book They Knew Lincoln, nearly eighty years later [^hymnarchive][^militaryimages]. No source from the war years that we have read names the song.

**Fisk, 1907.** The earliest known printing is in Folk Songs of the American Negro, Number Two, edited by Frederick J. Work, with an introduction by his brother John W. Work, signed at Fisk University on 15 May 1907 [^work1907][^folklorists][^cdh-fjwork]. On page 4 the song is headed "I Will Pray", marked "Infervorato", fervently, in E-flat [^work1907][^hymnarchive]. Everyone sings the refrain in four parts; then a solo voice sings three verses, "When you hear me pray, my Jesus", over hummed chords, and everyone returns to the refrain [^work1907]. Its introduction says that to sing these songs well, a newcomer to them "must abandon himself entirely to his spiritual nature" [^work1907].

![[2]]

**Hampton, 1909.** Hampton Institute had published its students' songs since 1874, and its new edition of 1909, Religious Folk Songs of the Negro as Sung on the Plantations, "arranged by the musical directors" of the Institute "from the original edition by Thomas P. Fenner", prints "Ev'ry Time I Feel the Spirit" on page 169 [^hampton1909][^lva-dett][^hymnarchive]. The refrain has the same words, again in E-flat and four parts, but the three verses are new [^hampton1909][^work1907]. Each verse is one line of tune over an empty lower staff: one voice alone. The refrain ends with repeat marks and "Fine.", and "D. C." after the verses sends the singers back to it; the page gives no tempo and names no voice [^hampton1909]. Our tune and words are read from this page. The book's note, signed by Robert R. Moton, Hampton's commandant, thanks Professor F. J. Work of Fisk and others for some of its new songs, but this page names no source, and its verses are not Fisk's [^hampton1909][^work1907][^hymnarchive].

![[3]]

**A quartet in Virginia, 1918.** The collector Natalie Curtis-Burlin printed it in 1918, in the Hampton Series of her Negro Folk-Songs [^burlin1918][^hymnarchive]. Her foreword says she set up an Edison phonograph in Virginia and tried to write down every part as it was sung [^burlin1918]. She names her four singers and their trades: Ira Godwin, the "Lead", in agriculture; Joseph Barnes, tenor, a tinsmith; William Cooper, baritone, a schoolteacher; and Timothy Carper, bass, a bricklayer [^burlin1918]. The leader carries the tune and "must sound above the other voices", slowly, "with simplicity, breadth and reverence", in the Hampton verses with the Jordan verse second [^burlin1918].

**For the concert hall and the islands, 1925.** The Book of American Negro Spirituals, edited by James Weldon Johnson, set it for solo voice and piano, arranged by J. Rosamond Johnson, "Moderately Slow", with the same three verses [^johnson1925][^hymnarchive]. The same year, the Penn School on St. Helena Island, South Carolina, printed it in Saint Helena Island Spirituals, giving Hampton's page 169 as its source and adding a fourth verse, about a train to heaven [^sthelena1925][^hymnarchive].

**Dett's book, 1927.** The composer R. Nathaniel Dett (1882 to 1943) taught music and directed Hampton's choirs from 1913 [^lva-dett][^ewb-dett]. His 1927 edition prints the 1909 setting again, newly typeset, on page 169, under the heading "Hymns of Meditation" [^dett1927][^hampton1909]. It adds markings the 1909 page lacks: "Andante molto espress.", at a walking pace and very expressive, 68 to 72 beats a minute; "Tutti", everyone, for the refrain, loud and growing quieter; and "Solo", fairly quiet, for the verses [^dett1927][^hampton1909]. These marks are Dett's; the pace of 70 beats a minute is our own, inside his range.

## What the words mean

The refrain is a promise: whenever I feel the Holy Spirit moving in my heart, I will pray. The Bible's Letter to the Romans says the Spirit helps people to pray when "we know not what we should pray for as we ought" [^kjv]. Hymnals also point to Romans 8:15 to 17 and to Luke 11:13, where the Father gives "the Holy Spirit to them that ask him" [^hymnary-umh][^hymnary-ph1990][^kjv].

- **"Upon the mountain my Lord spoke, Out His mouth came fire and smoke."** The mountain is most likely Sinai, as the Hymnology Archive reads it, where the Book of Exodus says the Lord came down "in fire" and the mountain was "altogether on a smoke"; a psalm says "fire out of his mouth devoured" [^hymnarchive][^kjv].
- **"All around me looks so shine, Ask my Lord if all was mine."** "Shine" here means shining: everything around the singer looks bright, and they ask the Lord whether all of it was theirs. One hymnologist hears a possible echo of the garden of Eden [^hymnarchive].
- **"Jordan river is chilly and cold, Chills the body but not the soul."** The Israelites crossed the Jordan into the promised land [^kjv]. The cold water chills the body but cannot touch the soul.

In 1855 Frederick Douglass wrote that when he and his friends sang of Canaan while planning to escape, "the north was our Canaan" [^douglass1855]. That double meaning is documented; for this song, no source from the 1800s that we have read gives one. Fisk's 1907 verses are about prayer too: Jesus hearing the singer pray, Jesus dying "for ev'ry sinner", "for Jew and Gentile" [^work1907].

### The full words

These are the refrain and three verses as Hampton's 1909 page prints them; the refrain is sung twice, as its repeat marks say, and again after each verse. They were printed in the United States in 1909, before 1931, and are in the public domain [^hampton1909]. Dett's 1927 page prints the same words [^dett1927]. We sing verses 1 and 2.

**Refrain**
Ev'ry time I feel the Spirit moving in my heart I will pray.

**Verse 1**
Upon the mountain my Lord spoke,
Out His mouth came fire and smoke.

**Verse 2**
All around me looks so shine,
Ask my Lord if all was mine.

**Verse 3**
Jordan river is chilly and cold,
Chills the body but not the soul.

## How it is sung

A folk spiritual is sung by ear, the way a congregation learned it; a concert spiritual is written out and arranged for trained singers. Burlin wrote down each part as her Virginia men sang it [^burlin1918]. Fisk's and Hampton's books set its refrain in four parts, with each verse for one voice [^work1907][^hampton1909]. The Johnsons' 1925 book made it a song for one voice and piano [^johnson1925]. William L. Dawson (died 1990), who led Tuskegee Institute's School of Music from 1931, arranged it for mixed choir, No. 117 in his Tuskegee Choir Series, and that setting is still sold [^cbb-dawson][^alamhof-dawson][^musicanet-dawson][^hebu-dawson].

In churches it is sung as a song of prayer: the United Methodist Hymnal files it under prayer, and the Chalice Hymnal under prayer and the Holy Spirit [^hymnary-umh][^hymnary-chalice]. Its meter is irregular [^hymnary-umh][^hymnary-ph1990][^hymnary-chalice].

Our arrangement keeps the 1909 page's E-flat, at 70 beats a minute, for 2 to 4 singers [^hampton1909]. Everyone sings the refrain in harmony, twice, as the repeat marks say; then the alto sings verse 1 alone, the refrain comes twice, then verse 2, and the refrain twice more. The Jordan verse is left out. In the refrain the tune is the printed top voice, and our lower parts are our own, checked against the printed chords. Breaths are written in for beginners.

## Versions and other words

- **"I Will Pray", Fisk, 1907.** The same refrain with three other verses, about prayer and about Jesus, sung over humming [^work1907][^hymnarchive].
- **St. Helena Island, 1925.** Hampton's verses in a different order, the Jordan second and the shining last, with a verse about the one train that runs to heaven and right back [^sthelena1925][^hymnarchive].
- **In the hymnals.** The United Methodist Hymnal (1989) and the Chalice Hymnal (1995) share one copyrighted adaptation; The Presbyterian Hymnal (1990) has another [^hymnary-umh][^hymnary-chalice][^hymnary-ph1990].

## Did you know?

- Burlin noted that her singers said the river's name as "Jerdan", and the 1925 St. Helena book spells it that way too [^burlin1918][^sthelena1925].
- The St. Helena book says its collector took down 103 spirituals in versions it believed had not been printed before, and that it also reprints "a few of the most familiar", like this one, No. 107 [^sthelena1925].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/every-time-i-feel-the-spirit/soprano): you carry the tune of the refrain across an octave, from your top E-flat on every "Ev'ry" and "feel" down to the E-flat below on every "heart" and "pray.". You rest while the alto sings the verses.
- [Alto](/songs/every-time-i-feel-the-spirit/alto): you sing both verses alone, "Upon the mountain my Lord spoke" and "All around me looks so shine", on C, E-flat and G, with an F on "fire" and "all". In the refrain you sing with everyone, mostly on G and A-flat, up to A-flat on "time I feel the" and the start of "moving", and down to C on every "heart".
- [Tenor](/songs/every-time-i-feel-the-spirit/tenor): you sing the refrain on just two notes, mostly B-flat, rising to E-flat on every "time I feel the" and "moving". You rest while the alto sings the verses.
- [Bass](/songs/every-time-i-feel-the-spirit/bass): you sing the refrain mostly on the roots of the chords, most often on E-flat, down to your low A-flat on every "time I". You rest while the alto sings the verses.

## Questions

### Who wrote Every Time I Feel the Spirit?

No one knows. It is a traditional African American spiritual, and its earliest known printing is "I Will Pray" in Frederick J. Work's Fisk book of 1907 [^work1907][^hymnarchive]. The verses we sing, about the mountain and everything shining, were printed by Hampton Institute in 1909 [^hampton1909]. William L. Dawson's choir arrangement is his own, under his copyright; the words and tune it arranges are traditional [^musicanet-dawson][^hebu-dawson][^hampton1909].

### What does Every Time I Feel the Spirit mean?

It is a promise to pray: whenever the singer feels the Holy Spirit moving in the heart, they will pray [^hampton1909]. Hymnals link it to Romans 8 and Luke 11 [^hymnary-umh][^hymnary-ph1990]. Its verses picture the Lord speaking from the mountain, most likely Sinai, in fire and smoke, everything shining around the singer, and the cold river Jordan, which chills the body but not the soul [^hampton1909][^kjv].

### Which part sings the tune in this arrangement?

In the refrain the soprano sings the tune, the printed top voice, with the other parts in harmony below [^hampton1909]. In the verses the alto sings the tune alone. The 1909 page gives the verses to one voice without naming it, and Dett's 1927 reprint marks them "Solo"; the line sits low, from middle C up to G, so we give it to the alto [^hampton1909][^dett1927].
