Ein Keloheinu, also spelled En Kelohenu, is a Hebrew hymn of five short verses that praise Hashem by four titles: Hashem, our Lord, our King, our Saviour. In the Ashkenazi rite it comes at the close of the additional service on Sabbaths and festivals, though Ashkenazi prayer books for Israel print it on weekdays too; Sephardi Jews say it on weekdays as well.[^ej][^huji][^sefaria] Nobody knows who wrote it or when, but it is already in the prayer book of Amram Gaon, from the ninth century.[^ej][^milken] Its words are older than any tune, so composers keep setting them: the Union Hymnal, an American Reform hymnal of 1897, printed four different settings, one after another.[^je][^uh1897][^uh1932] The tune we sing is the one Julius Freudenthal wrote for the synagogue of Braunschweig, printed there in 1843.[^idel29][^austerklein]

## Where it comes from

**The words.** How old the hymn is, nobody knows. The Jewish Music Research Centre and the Posen Library place it in the early Middle Ages, the period of the Geonim.[^huji][^posen] The oldest prayer book known to include it is Amram Gaon's, of the ninth century: the earliest record of the words, though not proof of when they were made.[^ej][^milken] Its verses there stand in another order: the Jewish Encyclopedia of 1903 read it as opening with the question, "Who is like Hashem?", and only then answering, "There is none like Hashem", and the Posen Library's translation of Amram's text opens the same way.[^je][^posen] The two encyclopedias disagree on when today's order settled. The Jewish Encyclopedia says the hymn had its present form by the time of Rashi, who remarked on its acrostic; the Encyclopaedia Judaica says Rashi's prayer book still had the stanzas in a different sequence.[^je][^ej]

That acrostic hides a word in the hymn's opening letters. The first letters of the first three verses, Ein, Mi and Node, spell Amen. The last two verses open with Baruch and Ata, "Blessed are You", the words that begin every Jewish blessing.[^je][^ej][^miller]

**The tune.** Julius Freudenthal was born in Braunschweig in 1805 and died there in 1874. He was a leading violinist in the Duke of Brunswick's court orchestra and became court music director.[^adb][^idel29][^nds] In 1842 the city's Jewish community took on a new cantor, Hirsch Goldberg, and the two men modernized the synagogue's singing together.[^idel29][^nds] Goldberg published the result in 1843: a book of songs for the cantor and a small choir, some of them, his preface says, set in two parts. A. Z. Idelsohn, the historian of Jewish music, wrote in 1929 that Freudenthal gave it several tunes, "especially his famous tune for En Kelohenu", which Idelsohn dated to 1841.[^idel29][^austerklein]

![[1]]

The first edition sold out in barely a year, Goldberg wrote in the second, of 1845, which carries a foreword by Freudenthal.[^bs1845] There the tune is No. 2 of two settings of the hymn, signed only "J. F.", in the Sabbath morning pages right after the additional service. The choir sings verses 1, 3 and 5 in two parts, and the cantor answers alone with verses 2 and 4, to a second strain of the tune.[^bs1845]

![[2]]

From Germany the tune crossed to America. The Union Hymnal of the Central Conference of American Rabbis, made for Reform congregations, printed it in four voices in 1897 as the first of its four settings of the hymn, with no composer on the page and only "German" in the index.[^uh1897][^uh1932][^idel29] Its third edition, of 1932, sets it in the music for the Sabbath morning service and names Julius Freudenthal.[^uh1932]

**Myth and record.** Often told: this tune, like other German synagogue tunes, began as an old German drinking song.[^austerklein] What the record shows: a named court musician wrote it for the Braunschweig synagogue, and the synagogue's own songbook printed it; the 1897 hymnal's index, naming no composer, says only "German".[^idel29][^bs1845][^uh1897] Idelsohn heard in its first part a likeness to a German church melody of 1774, and printed them together in a table. A 2026 article on the tune reports that a master's thesis on the Braunschweig synagogue's music rejects that borrowing.[^idel29][^austerklein]

## What the words mean

The hymn is built from four titles. Each verse runs through the same four titles, Elokeinu, Hashem; Adoneinu, our Lord; Malkeinu, our King; Moshienu, our Saviour, and changes only the word in front.[^je][^mjl]

**Verse 1.** There is none like Hashem, none like our Lord, none like our King, none like our Saviour. Ke- means "like".

**Verse 2.** Who is like Hashem, our Lord, our King, our Saviour? The question comes after its answer; in Amram's day, as the Jewish Encyclopedia reads it, the question came first.[^je]

**Verse 3.** We give thanks to Hashem, our Lord, our King, our Saviour. Node means "we thank".

**Verse 4.** Blessed is Hashem, our Lord, our King, our Saviour.

**Verse 5.** You are Hashem, our Lord, our King, our Saviour. The hymn ends by speaking to Hashem directly. In the Ashkenazi prayer book a further line follows, recalling the incense the ancestors offered, which leads into the passage on the Temple incense read next.[^je][^posen]

The page shows our words in modern Israeli pronunciation. The 1897 hymnal spelled them the Ashkenazi way, "En Kelohenu", "Boruch", "Atto hu", with the same syllables under the same notes.[^uh1897] We sing it with the substituted names customary when singing outside prayer, Kelokeinu and Elokeinu: it is the custom not to say Hashem's name in full outside prayer, and a practice song is outside prayer. The title keeps the spelling the prayer is known by.

### The full words

These are the five verses and the line that follows them as the Ashkenazi prayer book prints them, in the Hebrew of the Metsudah Siddur on Sefaria; some editions add Hashem's name to that last line.[^sefaria] It prints adonenu, "our Lord", where we sing adoneinu, another spelling of the same word, and the transliteration uses the substituted names we sing.

## How it is sung

In the Ashkenazi rite Ein Keloheinu follows the additional service, Musaf, on Sabbaths and festivals; in the Sephardi rite it ends the weekday morning service too, and Ashkenazi prayer books for use in Israel print it in the weekday morning service as well.[^ej][^huji][^sefaria] It was also sung at home, at the Sabbath table.[^je][^huji] The medieval commentator Rashi recorded the Ashkenazi custom of keeping it for Sabbaths and festivals. The Encyclopaedia Judaica gives his reason: the Sabbath Amidah, the central prayer, has seven blessings in place of the weekday nineteen, and the hymn's praises make up some of the difference.[^ej][^posen][^mjl]

Freudenthal's tune moves in short, even phrases, and in Braunschweig it was a dialogue between choir and cantor.[^bs1845] The Union Hymnal turned it into a plain four-part hymn for the whole choir, all five verses.[^uh1897]

![[3]]

Our arrangement is read from the 1897 Union Hymnal, No. 17. The book prints it in F; we sing it a tone higher, in G, which happens to be the key of the Braunschweig book of 1845.[^uh1897][^bs1845] All five verses are sung in order, for 2 to 4 singers. Verses 1, 3 and 5 share the opening tune, and verses 2 and 4 take the second strain, the one the Braunschweig cantor once sang alone; verse 5 comes in after a short rest, on a pickup note of its own.[^bs1845][^uh1897]

## Versions and other words

**Other tunes.** The 1897 Union Hymnal follows Freudenthal's tune with three more settings of the same words, by Louis Lewandowski, I. M. Japhet and Alois Kaiser.[^uh1897] The Braunschweig book of 1845 prints another setting just before his.[^bs1845] Sephardi congregations sing it to traditional melodies of their own.[^je][^milken]

## Did you know?

- The 1897 Union Hymnal prints four settings of Ein Keloheinu in a row, Nos. 17 to 20; no other hymn in its Hebrew section has more than two. [^uh1897]
- In his preface of 1843, Goldberg wrote that his book spells the Hebrew halfway between the German and the Portuguese pronunciation: he meant to adopt the Portuguese fully, but not to leave behind singers who could not follow so fast. [^bs1845]

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/ein-keloheinu/soprano): Freudenthal's tune, most often on G, then B. It climbs to its top E on the second "ke-" of "kemalkenu," in verse 1 and the "ke-" of "lemalkenu," in verse 3 and "malkenu," in verse 5, and dips to its low C-sharp only near the end of verses 2 and 4, on the "shi-" of "ch'moshienu." and "moshienu.".
- [Alto](/songs/ein-keloheinu/alto): under the tune, mostly a third or a sixth below, most often on D. It touches its low B on the opening "Ein", "Node" and the "ta hu" of "Ata hu", and on the very last "moshienu." it ends on its top B, a third above the tune.
- [Tenor](/songs/ein-keloheinu/tenor): the middle of the chord, most often on A. It stays on A for about three quarters of verses 2 and 4, and rises to its top E on the last syllable of "kemalkenu," in verse 1 and "lemalkenu," in verse 3, just after the tune's high point, and on the "a-" of the last "ata" in verse 5.
- [Bass](/songs/ein-keloheinu/bass): the bass line, mostly on the chords' roots and mostly on G and D. It drops to its low A only in verses 2 and 4, on the "mi chado-" of "mi chadoneinu,", the "ch'moshie-" of "ch'moshienu.", the "baruch ado-" of "baruch adoneinu," and the "moshie-" of "moshienu.".

## Questions

### What does Ein Keloheinu mean?

"There is none like Hashem." The hymn's five verses say there is none like Hashem, our Lord, our King, our Saviour; ask who is like Hashem; give thanks to Hashem; bless Hashem; and end, "You are Hashem". The first letters of the first three verses spell Amen.[^je][^ej][^mjl]

### Who wrote Ein Keloheinu?

Nobody knows who wrote the words, or when. The oldest prayer book known to include them is Amram Gaon's, of the ninth century, and some scholars place them in the period of the Geonim, the early Middle Ages. The tune we sing is by Julius Freudenthal of Braunschweig, printed there in 1843; the 1932 Union Hymnal names him.[^ej][^milken][^huji][^idel29][^uh1932]

### When is Ein Keloheinu said?

At the end of the Sabbath and festival service in most Ashkenazi synagogues, right after Musaf. Sephardi Jews say it on weekdays too, at the close of the morning service, and Ashkenazi prayer books for use in Israel print it there as well. It has also been sung at home, at the Sabbath table.[^ej][^huji][^sefaria][^je]

### Is Ein Keloheinu sung in Ladino?

Yes. There is a Ladino, or Judeo-Spanish, version, sung in Sephardi communities. The HUC Jewish Language Project shows it printed in Ladino in two editions of a Sephardi songbook, from Los Angeles and Seattle, one of them beside the Hebrew script, and the Jewish Encyclopedia of 1903 already noted congregations that sang it in Hebrew and then in Spanish; the Hebrew University's Jewish Music Research Centre holds a recording of a Judeo-Spanish version from Gibraltar. Sephardi congregations also sing the Hebrew to traditional melodies of their own.[^jlliturgy][^jmrcladino][^je][^milken]

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings Freudenthal's tune throughout, up to its top E on the second "ke-" of "kemalkenu," in verse 1. With two singers it is soprano and alto, the alto mostly a third or sixth below; with three the bass joins, and with four the tenor fills the middle of each chord.
