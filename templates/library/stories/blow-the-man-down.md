Blow the Man Down is a sea shanty, a work song that sailors sang to pull together on a rope. It is a halyard shanty, for hoisting the heavy yards that carry a ship's sails: the shantyman sings a line alone, and the crew answer, "Way-ay, blow the man down" [^whallscan][^masefield][^bullen]. The master mariner W. B. Whall wrote that it came from the old Atlantic packet ships, and that "blow" then meant "knock" [^whallscan]. No one knows who made it. Laura Alexandrine Smith printed it with its tune in 1888 as one of the most well-known shanties, Cecil Sharp printed it in 1914 as Knock a Man Down, and in 1921 Richard Terry called it perhaps the shanty best known among landsmen [^smith][^sharp][^terry].

## Where it comes from

**The packet ships.** Packet ships sailed on set days each month, rain or shine [^amereras][^clark]. The Black Ball Line began regular sailings between New York and Liverpool in January 1818 [^amereras][^rpsl], though a history of 1910 dates the first of these packets to 1816 [^clark]. Its ships flew a crimson swallowtail flag with a black ball, and carried a black ball painted on the fore-topsail [^clark][^amereras]. They were known for hard discipline: "belaying pin soup" was first served on Black Ball liners, that history says, and Terry wrote that the packet mates, whose job was knocking the crew about, were called "blowers", or strikers [^clark][^terry]. Whall adds that the third mate was called the third "blower and striker" [^whallscan].

**The sailor who wrote it down.** Whall went to sea in 1861, in the old passenger-carrying East Indiamen, and in eleven years there, by his own account, wrote down the songs as sailors sang them. After 1872 he heard no shanty worth the name: "Steam spoilt them" [^whall]. In passenger ships, he explained, the crew had to keep their words decent or not sing at all, so his versions are the clean ones [^whall]. His *Ships, Sea Songs and Shanties* came out in 1910 [^whall][^terry]. By then, he noted, seamen had forgotten the original verses, though the tune and chorus remained [^whallscan].

![[1]]

**Where shanties came from.** Terry, in the words of 1921, wrote of the shanties: "Without doubt a number of them came from American negro sources", meaning African American ones [^terry]. Whall traced another shanty in his book, Lowlands, to the cotton ports of the old Southern States, and its verse asks about "screwing cotton" in Mobile Bay: packing bales into a ship's hold with jack-screws [^whall][^smyth]. A Library of Congress folklorist writes that the most valued singer on board was often a Black sailor, and that the collector Stan Hugill learned many of his songs from West Indian shipmates [^winick]. This shanty lived on in the Caribbean, where a review of 1935 recordings from the Bahamas calls it well known throughout the islands, in the days when sponging crews spent weeks at sea in small sailboats [^mustrad]. That year Alan Lomax and Mary Elizabeth Barnicle recorded David Pryor and a group from Andros Island singing its tune to other words, as Roll 'Im On Down [^crem][^balladindex]. The Traditional Ballad Index lists versions from the Bahamas, Tobago, Nevis and St Vincent [^balladindex].

**Myth and record.** Often told: "blow the man down" means a gust of wind knocking a ship over. What the record shows: the earliest writers we found who explained it, Whall, a master mariner, and Terry, raised among sailors, both said "blow" meant to knock a man down, and Terry added that it could mean to kick [^whallscan][^terry].

## What the words mean

It is a sailor's tale of a run-in with the law, told a line at a time between the crew's answers [^whallscan].

**The call.** The shantyman calls on the "bullies", his shipmates on the rope, to blow the man down in Liverpool town: to knock him down [^whallscan][^terry].

**Verse 1.** "As I was a-walking down Paradise Street, a saucy young p'liceman I happened to meet." Paradise Street is in Liverpool: Herman Melville's *Redburn* (1849) calls it "the well known street of that name" in a passage on sailors in that port [^melville], and the gates of the old Sailors' Home stand at its southern end today [^lss]. "P'liceman" is policeman, squeezed into two syllables.

**The verses we leave out.** The policeman takes him for a "Black Baller", a hand off the Black Ball packets, by his hair and clothes, and accuses him of robbing a poor Dutchman of his boots and clothes [^whallscan]. Whall wrote that such lines suited an old "packet rat" but meant nothing to seamen of his day [^whall].

**Verse 2.** "O p'liceman, O p'liceman, you do me great wrong, I'm a 'Flying Fish sailor' just home from Hong Kong." He swears he is no packet man but a sailor of the long voyages east. The Traditional Ballad Index describes this version as one where a sailor is mistaken for a Black Baller, whom clipper crews looked down on [^balladindex].

**The last verse.** Three months in Walton Jail "for booting and kicking and blowing him down": the man blown down was the policeman [^whallscan]. Walton was Liverpool's borough gaol, opened in 1855 [^mercury][^prisonorg].

**The second set of words.** A sailor who served on a Black Baller tells how, once clear of the land, the boatswain roars "Lay aft", come to the stern, "to the break of the poop", the front edge of the raised deck at the stern [^whallscan][^smyth]. "Larboard" is the old word for the left side [^smyth].

Rougher verses existed, sung where no passengers could hear; they are not in Whall's book and not given here [^whall][^balladindex].

### The full words

These are the words in W. B. Whall's *Ships, Sea Songs and Shanties*, third edition (1913), pages 92 and 93, with his spelling [^whallscan]. He prints each verse as a pair of lines; the crew answer after each line as in the call. The words are traditional, in print in 1913; Whall had died by 1919 [^mence], and the Smithsonian Libraries, whose copy we read, mark the book as public domain in the United States [^silib].

**The call**
O blow the man down, bullies, blow the man down!
Way-ay, blow the man down.
O blow the man down in Liverpool town!
Give me some time to blow the man down.

**Verse 1**
As I was a-walking down Paradise Street,
A saucy young p'liceman I happened to meet.

**Verse 2**
Says he, "You're a Black Baller by the cut of your hair,
I know you're a Black Baller by the clothes that you wear.

**Verse 3**
"You've sailed in a packet that flies the Black Ball,
You've robbed some poor Dutchman of boots, clothes and all."

**Verse 4**
"O p'liceman, O p'liceman, you do me great wrong,
I'm a 'Flying Fish sailor' just home from Hong Kong."

**Verse 5**
They gave me three months in Walton Jail
For booting and kicking and blowing him down.

**Another set of words, verse 1**
Come all you young fellows that follow the sea,
Now pray pay attention and listen to me.

**Verse 2**
T'was aboard a Black Baller I first served my time,
And in that Black Baller I wasted my prime.

**Verse 3**
'Tis when a Black Baller's preparing for sea,
You'd split your sides laughing the sights you would see.

**Verse 4**
At the tinkers and tailors and sodjers and all,
Who ship for prime seamen on board a "Black Ball."

**Verse 5**
'Tis when the Black Baller is clear of the land,
The boatswain then bawls out the word of command.

**Verse 6**
"Lay aft" is the cry, "to the break of the poop,
Or I'll help you along with the toe of my boot."

**Verse 7**
'Tis larboard and starboard on deck you will sprawl,
For kicking "Jack Williams" commands that "Black Ball."

## How it is sung

It was a hauling song for the halyards, the ropes that hoist a sail on its yard [^smyth][^winick]. Masefield, Bullen and Terry all file it with the halyard shanties, and Smith names the topsail-yards [^masefield][^bullen][^terry][^smith]. In a halyard shanty, Masefield wrote, the solo man began after the first two or three pulls, and the two choruses were of the same length [^masefield]: here, "Way-ay, blow the man down" and "Give me some time to blow the man down" [^whallscan].

Whall marks it "Brisk", in 3/8, with "Solo" over the calls and "Chorus" over the answers, and prints the tune alone, with no harmony and no marks for the pulls [^whallscan][^terry].

Our arrangement is read from his page, moved from D up to F and written in 3/4, a printed eighth to a beat. It sings the call and his first and fourth verses, for 2 to 4 singers, brisk and swinging. The soprano is the shantyman and sings every call alone, and everyone comes in on the answers. We keep his "Way-ay" as printed. The harmony is ours, not the ships'.

## Versions and other words

- **Hong Kong and Winchester Street.** Smith's own version opens "I'm a true English sailor, just come from Hong-Kong", then walks down Winchester Street [^smith]. So does Terry's, and he places the street in South Shields, then a street of shipowners and captains [^terry].
- **The Black Ball verses.** A correspondent sent Smith twelve verses following a Black Ball ship out of dock, across the ocean and home, close to Whall's second set [^smith][^whallscan].
- **The clipper to Sydney.** In Masefield's, a girl in Paradise Street steers the singer to a clipper bound for Sydney, where the mate knocks him down with a spar [^masefield].
- **Knock a Man Down.** Sharp's singer, John Short of Watchet, remembered two verses and sang "knock"; Sharp filled out the rest from other versions [^sharp]. Terry, without naming the book, wrote that "knock" was "too crudely realistic" for a sailor's chorus [^terry].

## Did you know?

- In a letter of April 1914 printed in Bullen's book, Arthur Conan Doyle, who had hauled whaling boats to shanties, named Blow the Man Down among the songs that still stirred his love of adventure [^bullen].
- An old sailor once told Laura Alexandrine Smith that "The Bay of Biscay" was a good song in its way, but not to be compared to Blow the Man Down [^smith].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/blow-the-man-down/soprano): you are the shantyman. Sing every call alone, then lead the crew's answers. "Way-ay" is two long notes, C and then your top D, a full bar each; the top D comes back, held, on "town", "meet" and "Kong", and as a quick passing note in the first line of the call and of each verse: on both "the"s of the call, on "was" and the "-a-" of "Paradise", and on the "-man" of the first "p'liceman" and on "me". Each "Give me some time" answer ends on your low F.
- [Alto](/songs/blow-the-man-down/alto): you come in on the answers, on just three notes, E, F and A: A and F on "Way-ay", E and F on "blow the man down", A all through "Give me some time". On the last "down" of each "Give me some time" you land on A, above the tune.
- [Tenor](/songs/blow-the-man-down/tenor): you come in with the crew on every answer, mostly holding C: C and B-flat on "Way-ay", G on "blow the man", back to C on "down", a dip to A on "time" and to B-flat on the "the" that follows.
- [Bass](/songs/blow-the-man-down/bass): you haul in on the roots. F on "Way-", down to a low B-flat on "ay", C through "blow the man down", then F through "Give me some time to blow the man down", with one C on "to".

## Questions

### What does "blow the man down" mean?

The earliest sailors we found who explained it said it means to knock a man down; the other reading often told, a gust of wind knocking a ship over, we found in no early source. W. B. Whall, a master mariner who learned the shanty at sea, wrote that "blow" in the packet-ship days meant "knock", and that the third mate was nicknamed the third "blower and striker" [^whallscan]. Richard Terry said "to blow" could also mean to kick [^terry]. In Whall's verses the man blown down is a Liverpool policeman [^whallscan].

### What is Blow the Man Down about, and where is it set?

It is a sailor's tale set in Liverpool. In Whall's version he walks down Paradise Street, is taken by a policeman for a hand off the Black Ball packets, swears he is a "Flying Fish sailor" home from Hong Kong, and gets three months in Walton Jail for knocking the policeman down [^whallscan]. Other versions move it: Terry's to South Shields, Masefield's onto a clipper bound for Sydney [^terry][^masefield].

### Is Blow the Man Down public domain?

Yes. The words and tune are traditional, and the printing we sing from, Whall's *Ships, Sea Songs and Shanties* of 1913, is over a century old; Whall had died by 1919, and the Smithsonian Libraries, whose copy we read, mark it public domain in the United States [^whallscan][^mence][^silib]. A later arrangement or recording can have its own copyright; ours is our own.

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano. In our arrangement the soprano is the shantyman, singing every call alone, as Whall marks it "Solo", and keeping the tune when everyone comes in on the answers; the alto, tenor and bass sing only the answers, in harmony [^whallscan].
