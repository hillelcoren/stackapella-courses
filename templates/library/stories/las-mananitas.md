Las Mañanitas, also known by its first line, Estas son las mañanitas, and as Las mañanitas del rey David, is the song Mexicans sing at dawn to wake someone on their birthday or saint's day [^toor1947][^manfred1932][^brenner1929]. It is also sung to the Virgin of Guadalupe at her basilica on 12 December, her day [^toor1947][^ewtn2022]. No one knows who made it. Its tune and Amapolita verse were printed in 1904 by Antonio García Cubas, remembering a dawn serenade [^garciacubas1904]; the earliest printing of the King David verse we have found is a Los Angeles songbook of 1921 [^laud1921].

## Where it comes from

**A serenade at a farmhouse door.** Antonio García Cubas, a Mexican mapmaker, joined the Scientific Commission of Pachuca, based at Real del Monte, in September 1864, and in El libro de mis recuerdos (1904) he tells of a night ride in that country [^garciacubas1904]. By moonlight his party reached the plain of Atotonilco el Grande and halted before a house among ash trees; as day came near, they began to sing the mañanitas. The windows opened, and soon the young women of the house began to sing, with two young men on guitars. On page 547 he prints the tune, in A-flat and triple time, with the Amapolita verse and the refrain "Despierta, mi bien despierta" [^garciacubas1904]. Earlier in the book, on page 374, he had already printed the same tune, remembering St John's Day in Mexico City in the years before the railway: from the first bell at dawn, people on their way to the baths went through the streets singing "las festivas mañanitas", and he gives the Amapolita verse, with "campos", fields, for "llanos" [^garciacubas1904].

![[1]]

**A folk song in Berlin.** In 1913 Albert Friedenthal printed the melody in a German book on Latin American music [^friedenthal1913]. He calls it one of Mexico's two best-known sentimental songs, "also called Amapola", says it comes from the south of the country, and calls it "halb Romanze, halb Serenade", in which a lover sings to a girl from the plains of Tepic. He marks it "Langsam", slowly, and adds that the distant time of such songs' making cannot be fixed at all [^friedenthal1913].

![[2]]

**A composer's word.** In an essay printed in 1917 (one modern article dates his words to 1914 [^quadratin2026]), the composer Manuel M. Ponce described songs in triple time with the air of a mazurka, and said that in one of them "the anonymous popular author" reached real beauty: "me refiero a las mañanitas, canto popular de melodía nobilísima y de corte beethoviano", a popular song with a most noble, Beethoven-like melody. It was so well known, he added, that he need not print it [^ponce1917].

**King David's verse.** The earliest printing we have found of the verse that opens the song today, "Estas son las mañanitas que cantaba el rey David", is in El laúd hispano-americano, a 160-page pocket songster published in Los Angeles in 1921 by its compiler, C. Santos, its cover striped green, white and red, with the Mexican eagle [^laud1921]. On pages 104 and 105 it prints "Las Mañanitas" as seven unbroken quatrains with no author [^laud1921]. A 2024 study by Ana Rosa Gómez Mutio finds the verse often, with changes, in prints from the Mexico City press of Antonio Vanegas Arroyo and his heirs; one, a booklet of 1935, turns it to the Virgin of Guadalupe [^gomez2024][^lacipi-diadoce]. One of his sheets, dated 1911, is titled "Unas lindas mañanitas a las muchachas bonitas", words from the verse's third line, and Gómez Mutio reads the title as a nod to it; but its twenty stanzas do not include King David's verse [^carter1911][^gomez2024]. A 2026 news article says that sheet already has the verse, and that El Colegio de México holds a copy dated 1918 [^quadratin2026]; the 1911 copy we have read does not have it, and we have not seen the 1918 one.

![[3]]

**Myth and record.** Often told: the song is very old, sung by King David himself [^hernandez-desdelafe], or, as Anita Brenner wrote in 1929, a chant handed down from before the Spanish came [^brenner1929]; or Ponce wrote it, or added King David, as a 2022 news article says without a source [^infobae2022]. What the record shows: the tune was in print as a folk song by 1904 [^garciacubas1904][^friedenthal1913], and Ponce credited an anonymous popular author [^ponce1917][^quadratin2026]. Gómez Mutio reports that Margit Frenk's Cancionero folklórico de México files King David's verse under "Las mañanitas I", and, in a note on another stanza of that song, "Levántate mamacita", that Frenk records an attribution to Ponce [^gomez2024]; that stanza is already on the 1911 sheet [^carter1911]. No source we have seen names Ponce as the author of King David's verse.

### Timeline

- 1904: García Cubas prints the tune twice in his memoir, with the Amapolita verse, the earliest printing we have found [^garciacubas1904]
- 1913: Friedenthal prints the melody in Berlin as a Mexican folk song [^friedenthal1913]
- 1917: Ponce, in print, calls it the work of an anonymous popular author [^ponce1917]
- 1921: El laúd hispano-americano, Los Angeles, prints King David's verse, the earliest printing we have found [^laud1921]
- 1935: A Vanegas Arroyo booklet for the twelfth day prints mañanitas to the Virgin of Guadalupe [^lacipi-diadoce][^gomez2024]
- 1947: Frances Toor prints it as the national birthday serenade at dawn [^toor1947]

## What the words mean

"Mañanitas" is the little form of "mañanas", mornings: the first hours of the day, and the songs sung then [^friedenthal1913][^gomez2024]. Our words are the first three of seven quatrains printed in 1921 [^laud1921].

1. These are the dawn songs King David used to sing, and we sing them here to the pretty girls. Why David is here, no early source says (see Questions).
2. "Amapolita morada", little purple poppy from the plains ("llanos") of Tepic, is a girl: if you are not in love, fall in love with me.
3. The refrain: wake up, my dear ("mi bien"), wake up. Day has dawned ("amaneció"), the little birds are singing, the moon has hidden.

The 1921 song goes on with the night watchman ("el sereno") and his lantern, happy mornings never forgotten, and a second refrain to a "Rosita de Jericó", little rose of Jericho.

![[4]]

On García Cubas's page 374 the refrain begins "Despierta divina aurora", wake, heavenly dawn. On his page 547 the Amapolita verse is followed by the lines that end the 1921 song, "Que amanece, que amanece, Rosita de Jericó"; the happy mornings will be remembered "En tanto que tenga vida", while I have life, followed by "Despierta, adorada mía", wake, my adored one, with "Ya los pajaritos cantan / Y la luna se metió"; and there is no watchman [^garciacubas1904].

### The full words

These are all seven quatrains as El laúd hispano-americano prints them (Los Angeles, 1921), pages 104 and 105, run together there without breaks; spelling and capitals as printed, including the old accent on "Á". They are traditional and the book names no author; no maker of the words or the tune is known, and the tune was in print by 1904 [^garciacubas1904]. Later arrangements and recordings are works of their own, with their own rights; our arrangement and its harmony are our own. Our arrangement sings the first three.

**Verse 1**
> Estas son las mañanitas
These are the dawn songs
> Que cantaba el rey David,
That King David used to sing,
> Á las muchachas bonitas
To the pretty girls
> Se las cantamos aquí.
We sing them here.

**Verse 2**
> Amapolita morada
Little purple poppy
> De los llanos de Tepic,
From the plains of Tepic,
> Si no estás enamorada
If you are not in love,
> Enamórate de mí.
Fall in love with me.

**Refrain**
> Despierta, mi bien, despierta,
Wake up, my dear, wake up,
> Mira que ya amaneció,
Look, the day has dawned,
> Ya los pajarillos cantan,
The little birds are singing now,
> La luna ya se escondió.
The moon has hidden away.

**Verse 3**
> Si el sereno de la esquina
If the night watchman on the corner
> Nos quisiera hacer favor
Would do us the favour
> Que apagara su linterna
Of putting out his lantern
> Mientras que pasa mi amor,
While my love goes by,

**Verse 4**
> Ahora sí, señor sereno,
Now then, Mister Watchman,
> Agradezco su favor,
I thank you for the favour,
> Encienda usted su linterna,
Light your lantern again,
> Que ya pasó mi amor.
For my love has gone by.

**Verse 5**
> Las mañanitas alegres
The happy early mornings
> Que contigo me pasé,
That I spent with you,
> Mientras Dios me preste vida
As long as God lends me life
> Nunca las olvidaré.
I will never forget them.

**Refrain**
> Despierta, mi bien, despierta,
Wake up, my dear, wake up,
> Mira que ya amaneció,
Look, the day has dawned,
> Que amanece, que amanece,
It is dawning, it is dawning,
> Rosita de Jericó.
Little rose of Jericho.

## How it is sung

It is sung at daybreak, or in the small hours before it, to wake the person whose birthday or saint's day it is [^toor1947][^manfred1932][^brenner1929]. Frances Toor (1947) says fiestas sometimes begin with mañanitas, their words fitted to the day [^toor1947]. She heard them sung to the Virgin at the Basilica of Guadalupe at dawn on 12 December, and Brenner describes the same serenade there [^toor1947][^brenner1929]; today they are sung at midnight, a high point of the feast [^ewtn2022].

The earliest accounts show a lovers' dawn serenade [^garciacubas1904][^friedenthal1913], and Gómez Mutio counts the song among the heirs of the old Hispanic albas and alboradas, dawn songs [^gomez2024].

Our arrangement is Friedenthal's melody of 1913, printed there without words under the notes or a time signature, here in G instead of A and in 3/4; every pitch is his, and every rhythm except the verse's last bar, completed with a rest, and five held notes shortened for breaths. It matches García Cubas's 1904 tune in rhythm and opening. We sing the 1921 words: verse 1, the refrain, verse 2 and the refrain, for 2 to 4 singers. Where vowels meet across words, we join them on one note, as Spanish singers do: "ba el", "no es", "ya a", "se es". Breaths are written in after "David", "Tepic", each "amaneció" and the first refrain's "escondió". The harmony is our own; neither print has any.

## Versions and other words

A Spanish textbook for American high schools, in its 1932 edition, prints the tune with "Amapola perfumada", fragrant poppy [^manfred1932].

Margit Frenk, as Gómez Mutio reports, counts ten different songs called Las mañanitas, made mostly of different stanzas, often in different metres [^gomez2024]. Eleanor Hague (1917) prints a "Las Mañanitas" from Oaxaca with other words and another tune, of the kind young people sang going home from an evening's entertainment [^hague1917]. A Jalisco birthday song beginning "El día en que tú naciste", the day you were born, is printed by Carl Sandburg in 1927 as "Mañanitas (de Jalisco)" and by Toor in 1947 [^sandburg1927][^toor1947]. Later songbooks, such as an undated one from Guadalajara, turn the second half of King David's verse to greet the person on their saint's day [^guadalajara-songbook][^gomez2024].

## Did you know?

- A Mexico City broadside of 1911 already sings mañanitas for a saint's day: its first long stanza opens "Por ser el día de tu santo / Te canto estas mañanitas", because it is your saint's day I sing you these mañanitas [^carter1911].
- In 1927 Jovita González wrote that she had heard Texas-Mexican vaqueros, saddling their horses on misty spring mornings, sing "Las Mañanitas de San Juan", with the same refrain, "Despierta, mi bien, despierta" [^gonzalez1927].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/las-mananitas/soprano): you sing the tune, in G and 3/4. Each verse opens with a leap, from the low D of "Es-tas" up a sixth to B on "son", and climbs to your top E on "Da-vid," ("Te-pic," in verse 2). In the refrain you leap again, from G on "Ya los" to four Es on "pa-ja-ri-llos". You end on B, not G.
- [Alto](/songs/las-mananitas/alto): you sing close under the tune, from low B up to B. In the refrain you climb F-sharp, G, A, B on "mi bien, des-pier-", a third under the soprano, then stay on G under the soprano's four high Es on "pajarillos". Your last note is D.
- [Tenor](/songs/las-mananitas/tenor): your part sits between G and D. You start on G, then sing eight notes on D through "-nitas que cantaba el rey Da-", and six on C for "Ya los pajarillos". Each second line ends stepping down C, A, G, as on "se es-con-dió"; solo it in the player to keep the repeated notes in tune.
- [Bass](/songs/las-mananitas/bass): you sing on G, A, C and D, the A under the D chords in the middle of each verse line. The verses stay mostly on low G. In the refrain you lift an octave, from low G on "Des-" through D to high G on "-pier-ta, Mi-" and "-ció". You end on low G.

## Questions

### Who wrote Las Mañanitas?

No one is known to have written it: it is a traditional Mexican song. Its tune and the Amapolita verse were printed in 1904 by García Cubas, from memory [^garciacubas1904]. Manuel M. Ponce, often named as its author, wrote in an essay printed in 1917 that it was the work of "the anonymous popular author" [^ponce1917]. The earliest printing of King David's verse we have found is a 1921 songbook that names no author [^laud1921].

### Why is Las Mañanitas sung, and since when?

To wake the person celebrated at dawn on their birthday or saint's day [^toor1947][^manfred1932]. Gómez Mutio counts it among the heirs of the old albas and alboradas, dawn songs [^gomez2024]. García Cubas, in a book of 1904, recalls singing mañanitas at daybreak in the 1860s [^garciacubas1904]; a broadside of 1911 sings them for a saint's day [^carter1911]; and in 1947 Frances Toor called it the national birthday serenade at dawn [^toor1947]. It is sung beyond Mexico too: it was printed in Los Angeles in 1921 [^laud1921].

### Why does Las Mañanitas mention King David?

No early source explains it. David is the Bible's singing king, and Psalm 5, headed "A Psalm of David", says "My voice shalt thou hear in the morning" [^kjv-ps5]. A priest writing in Desde la Fe reads the line as calling on David, the king who sang and danced for God [^hernandez-desdelafe]. That is a reading, not a record: in the prints we have found, the line appears without comment from 1921 on [^laud1921].

### What does Las Mañanitas mean in English?

The title means the early mornings, or the songs sung at dawn [^friedenthal1913][^gomez2024]. The first verse says these are the dawn songs King David sang, sung here to the pretty girls; the refrain says wake up, my dear, the day has dawned, the birds sing and the moon has gone [^laud1921].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, Friedenthal's melody of 1913, here in G [^friedenthal1913]. With two singers you have the tune and the alto, close under it; with three, add the bass, mostly on the roots of the chords, and the tenor, most often on D, makes four.
