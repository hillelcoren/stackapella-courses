Bully in the Alley is a sea shanty, a work song sailors sang to haul together on a rope [^sharp][^balladindex]. The shantyman sings "So help my bob I'm bully in the alley", and the crew answers "Way ay bully in the alley", pulling twice, on "Way" and on "bully" [^sharp]. No one knows who made it [^balladindex]. Its earliest known printing is the version John Short, an old shantyman of Watchet in Somerset, sang to the collector Cecil Sharp, who printed it in 1914 and knew no other [^sharp][^balladindex][^csp]. A second version, which the sailor Stan Hugill is reported to have picked up in the West Indies, sets the song in a Shinbone Alley, a name already in a song printed in 1833 for the blackface minstrel stage [^balladindex][^mainlynorfolk][^locodyssey][^beaman].

## Where it comes from

**The page we read.** Sharp printed the song as No. XXXV of *English Folk-Chanteys*, on pages 40 and 41, in G major and 4/4, with his own piano part and "Solo." and "Chorus." marked over the voice [^sharp]. In the verse, the shantyman sings "Bully down in our alley", the crew now takes up "So help my bob" too, and the shantyman's "Bully in Tinpot alley" brings the last answer. Over each "Way" and the "bully" after it stands a small arrowhead, Sharp's sign for the notes the men pulled on [^sharp]. Under the music he prints two more lines, numbered 2 and 3: "Have you seen our Sally?" and "She's the girl in the alley." His whole note on page 71 reads: "Sung by Mr. John Short, at Watchet. I have no variants of this nor do I know of any printed version of it." [^sharp]

![[1]]

**John Short of Watchet.** Short, baptised in Watchet in 1839, sailed deep-water as far as North America and the Far East [^csp][^halsway]. Sharp wrote that he had spent more than fifty years in sailing ships, mostly as a recognised chanteyman, the solo singer who led the work; at 76 he was Watchet's town crier [^sharp][^csp]. Sharp began taking down his shanties in April 1914 and printed 46 of them among the 60 in his book [^csp][^halsway][^sharp].

**Shinbone Alley ashore.** In 1833 a song called "Long Time Ago", also known as "Shinbone Alley", was printed in Baltimore as sung by Thomas Rice in his "Ethiopian Opera" [^locodyssey][^beaman]. Rice was a white blackface performer: in minstrel shows, white performers darkened their faces to mock Black people [^locodyssey][^beaman]. The Library of Congress counts the tune among the few published before the Civil War believed to be genuinely African American, though it was sold as part of Rice's act [^locodyssey]. A 2026 history of Alexandria, Virginia, gives its opening: a singer going down Shinbone Alley, answered "Long time ago" [^beaman]. In July 1836 the New York *Knickerbocker* magazine printed a traveller's journal whose New Orleans entry, arguing that enslaved people were content, told doubters to hear them singing at their work, with a verse of that song as the example [^knick][^beaman]. In 1850 Caroline Lee Hentz, a novelist who defended slavery, set a corn-husking near the Alabama River in her novel *Linda*: a leader sings out, everyone joins the chorus, and one verse goes down to Shinbone Alley "to buy a bonnet for my Sally", with the same answer [^hentz][^eoa][^utc]. Neither verse has "bully" in it. Whether Black singers made the song before the stage took it up or learned it there, the record does not say, and no source links these verses to the shanty.

**Black and Caribbean singers.** Joanna Colcord, a captain's daughter, wrote that a large class of shanties began with Black stevedores in the Gulf ports, singing as they rammed cotton bales into ships' holds, and that sailors carried them to sea [^colcord][^winick]. A Library of Congress folklorist calls African Americans and Afro-Caribbean people central to the shanty tradition [^winick]. Steam ended its working life: aboard a steamer, Terry wrote, there were practically no sails to work [^terry][^sharp].

**Hugill's version.** Stan Hugill, whom the same folklorist calls "the last professional British shantyman we know of", learned many of his songs from Black West Indian shipmates [^winick]. In *Shanties from the Seven Seas* (1961), which we have not been able to read, he printed a second Bully in the Alley. The Traditional Ballad Index sums up its verses as courting Sally, being turned down by her or leaving her, and reports Hugill's note that Shinbone Alley is a place name often found in African American songs. Sleeve notes collected on the Mainly Norfolk site say he picked the song up in the West Indies [^balladindex][^mainlynorfolk].

**Myth and record.** Often told, in sleeve notes to modern recordings collected on the Mainly Norfolk site: "bully" here means drunk, the sailor is left in the alley until his shipmates fetch him, and Shinbone Al is one particular street, perhaps the one in Bermuda [^mainlynorfolk]. What the record shows: no early source explains the line, and Sharp's 1914 words have no drink and no Shinbone in them [^sharp]. Slang dictionaries from 1874 to 1913 give "bully" as fine or first-rate, and one gives it as a sailors' word for a mate [^hotten][^farmer1912][^webster]. And Shinbone Alley was a name given to back streets in more than one place [^campbell][^royalgazette][^gotobermuda].

### Timeline

- 1833: "Long Time Ago", also called "Shinbone Alley", is printed in Baltimore as sung by the blackface performer Thomas Rice [^locodyssey][^beaman]
- 1836: the *Knickerbocker* prints a traveller's journal that quotes a verse of the same song as sung by enslaved workers in New Orleans [^knick][^beaman]

## What the words mean

Sharp's words are a scrap: one verse and two more lines, which was normal [^sharp]. He wrote that the chanteyman made up his solo lines as he went, while the crew's words were "little more than pegs upon which to hang the notes"; a chanteyman once told him, "It was only just a few words we had, but it made the thing come lighter." [^sharp]

**"So help my bob."** The street oath "s'help me Bob", a softened "so help me God" [^hotten][^farmer]. Hotten (1874) adds that street speech often had "so help my" for "so help me", as Sharp printed it [^hotten][^sharp].

![[2]]

**"I'm bully in the alley."** "Bully" meant fine or first-rate, and among sailors a mate, as in the shanty "Blow my bully boys" [^farmer1912][^webster][^hotten][^terry]. So the singer may be boasting that he is doing fine.

**"Bully in Tinpot alley."** "Tin-pot" was slang for shabby, mean or worthless [^hotten][^barrere]. Tinpot Alley sounds like a nickname for a poor street; Sharp does not say where it was.

**"Have you seen our Sally? She's the girl in the alley."** Sally is the singer's girl [^sharp].

**"Way ay."** The crew's cry as they pull, drawn out over two long syllables [^sharp].

### The full words

These are the words of No. XXXV in Cecil Sharp's *English Folk-Chanteys* (1914), pages 40 and 41, as John Short sang them, with Sharp's spelling. Sharp prints the refrain and the first verse under the tune, then only the lines numbered 2 and 3. Here, as we sing it, each of those lines takes the place of the verse's first solo line, and the rest of the verse is sung as before. The words are traditional, printed in 1914, and Sharp died in 1924, so they are in the public domain.

![[3]]

**Refrain**
So help my bob I'm bully in the alley;
Way ay bully in the alley,
So help my bob I'm bully in the alley;
Way ay bully in the alley.

**Verse 1**
Bully down in our alley;
So help my bob I'm bully in the alley,
Way ay bully in the alley;
Bully in Tinpot alley;
Way ay bully in the alley.

**Verse 2**
Have you seen our Sally?
So help my bob I'm bully in the alley,
Way ay bully in the alley;
Bully in Tinpot alley;
Way ay bully in the alley.

**Verse 3**
She's the girl in the alley.
So help my bob I'm bully in the alley,
Way ay bully in the alley;
Bully in Tinpot alley;
Way ay bully in the alley.

## How it is sung

Sharp sorts chanteys into capstan chanteys, for steady work like marching round the capstan or pumping, and pulling chanteys, for hard pulls as when the crew raised the yards or hoisted the sails. This is a pulling chantey [^sharp]. A pulling song, he wrote, must put every pull together, leave the men a rest between pulls to take a fresh grip and breathe, and hold their attention [^sharp]. Here the lines between the answers are the rests, and each answer carries two pulls [^sharp]. No early source names the exact job.

Our arrangement is read from Sharp's page, in G as printed, on the harmony of his piano part: the refrain and three verses for 2 to 4 singers. The soprano is the shantyman, alone on each solo line; the others join every answer, and in the verses "So help my bob" too, landing together on Sharp's pulls. Verses 2 and 3 are our fitting of Sharp's two extra lines, as he invited singers to make their own [^sharp].

## Versions and other words

- **Not in the other early books.** Whall (we read the 1913 edition) and Bullen and Arnold (1914) do not print it, which fits Sharp's note, nor do Terry (1921) or Colcord (1924) [^whall][^bullen][^terry][^colcord][^sharp].
- **Hugill's version (1961).** A longer song about Sally, set in Shinbone Alley; a 2012 sleeve note collected on Mainly Norfolk judges that all revival versions stem from it [^balladindex][^mainlynorfolk]. It is in copyright, so it is not given here.

## Did you know?

- In 1897 a book on New York's slums called Doyers Street "known locally as Shinbone Alley", where thieves robbed the countrymen and sailors lured in [^campbell].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/bully-in-the-alley/soprano): you are the shantyman. You sing the refrain's two "So help my bob" lines alone, and in each verse its first line and "Bully in Tinpot alley". Every "So" starts on your low D and every "I'm" jumps to your top D; you reach it again on "our" in verses 1 and 2, on the second "the" in "She's the girl in the alley." and on "in" in every "Bully in Tinpot alley".
- [Alto](/songs/bully-in-the-alley/alto): you answer "Way ay bully in the alley" with everyone, and in the verses sing "So help my bob" too, mostly on E, C and D. Each verse's "So" starts on your low A; your top G comes on "ay" in both answers of the opening refrain and in each verse's last answer.
- [Tenor](/songs/bully-in-the-alley/tenor): you answer with everyone and join "So help my bob" in the verses, most often on G. Your top D comes only once, on "ay" in the opening refrain's second "Way ay", and you drop to a low D on the last "alley." of each verse.
- [Bass](/songs/bully-in-the-alley/bass): you sing the bass line under the answers and the verses' "So help my bob", mostly on C and a low G, up to your top E only once, on "ay" in the opening refrain's first "Way ay". Land each "Way" and "bully" with everyone: those are the pulls.

## Questions

### What is Bully in the Alley about?

Not much, by design: it is a hauling shanty, and its words were mostly pegs for the pulls [^sharp]. In Sharp's 1914 version the shantyman swears he is "bully", doing fine, in his alley, and asks after his girl, Sally [^sharp][^hotten]. In Hugill's longer version he courts Sally in Shinbone Alley and is turned down by her or leaves her [^balladindex]. The tale of a drunk sailor left in the alley comes from modern sleeve notes, not the early record [^mainlynorfolk].

### What does "bully in the alley" mean?

No one knows for sure. Slang dictionaries from 1874 to 1913 give "bully" as fine or first-rate, and as a sailors' word for a mate, so the singer may be boasting that he is doing fine in his alley [^hotten][^farmer1912][^webster]. Modern sleeve notes often say it means drunk, but we found no early evidence for that, and Sharp's 1914 words have no drink in them [^mainlynorfolk][^sharp].

### What does "So help me Bob" mean?

It is a mild oath standing in for "so help me God". Slang dictionaries of the 1800s list "s'help me Bob" as a street oath, and Farmer and Henley give an example dated 1837 [^hotten][^farmer]. In Cecil Sharp's 1914 printing, the shantyman sings "So help my bob" [^sharp].

### Where is Shinbone Al?

No one can say which one the song means. Shinbone Al is Shinbone Alley, the setting of Stan Hugill's version; the name was in print by 1833 as the other title of "Long Time Ago", a song from the blackface minstrel stage, and real streets bore it too, in New York and in St. George's, Bermuda, among other places [^balladindex][^locodyssey][^beaman][^campbell][^royalgazette]. Sharp's version has no Shinbone at all [^sharp].
