Didn't My Lord Deliver Daniel is an African American spiritual, made by enslaved people, that asks a bold question. God saved Daniel from the lions, Jonah from the whale and the Hebrew children from the fiery furnace: "And why not every man?" [^seward1872][^kjv]. No maker's name is known. Its earliest known printing is on pages 16 and 17 of Jubilee Songs (1872), the first book of Fisk University's touring student singers, as a single tune headed "Sung in Unison" [^seward1872][^marsh1875]. In 1925 James Weldon Johnson wrote that the songs' makers sang their listeners into faith that God, who saved Daniel, would save them too [^johnson1925]. Choirs and soloists still sing it, in settings made since the 1920s [^burleigh1921][^johnson1925][^halleonard].

## Where it comes from

**The singers and their book.** Fisk University in Nashville, a school for freed people, was deep in debt in 1871. On 6 October that year its treasurer, George L. White, set out with a small company of students to sing for money for it [^pike1873][^fisk][^seward1872]. In 1872 Biglow & Main of New York printed their songs as Jubilee Songs. The copy we read lists 24, though the enlarged edition's introduction, later that year, counts twenty-nine in the first [^seward1872][^seward1872c]. White took the words down "from their lips", and Theodore F. Seward wrote down the music [^seward1872]. Its introduction says the singers learned most of the songs as children, that none had been printed before, as far as they knew, and that seven of the nine singers had been enslaved [^seward1872]. The song is not in the earlier Slave Songs of the United States (1867), compiled by William Francis Allen, Charles Pickard Ware and Lucy McKim Garrison, three white collectors [^slavesongs1867].

**What the 1872 page shows.** Under its black-letter title, page 16 reads "Sung in Unison": one line of melody, for everyone together, where Steal Away, later in the book, is printed in four parts [^seward1872]. The key is G minor and the time 4/8, four eighth notes to the bar. The refrain comes first, then the first verse with its own music, "He deliver'd Daniel from the lion's den". An asterisk over the return to the refrain points to a footnote: "Go on without pause, leaving out two beats of the measure", so the singers ran straight from the verse back into the refrain [^seward1872]. Page 17 prints the other three verses, each sending the singer back with "D. C. 'Didn't my Lord.'" [^seward1872][^seward1872c]. In the refrain the word is printed "D'liver", sung fast, and the last line reads "And why not a every man?", with an extra sung "a" that the verse leaves out [^seward1872].

![[1]]

**A growing book.** Later that year the publishers issued Jubilee Songs: Complete, a 64-page edition whose introduction is dated 9 October 1872, with the song on the same two pages [^seward1872c]. G. D. Pike's book of the singers' first campaign (1873) prints it on page 174, his book of their British tour (1875) on page 216, and J. B. T. Marsh's Story of the Jubilee Singers (London, 1875) as No. 10, on pages 134 and 135 [^pike1873][^pike1875][^marsh1875]. Marsh's pages repeat the 1872 tune and footnote, still "Sung in Unison", and add one heading, "1st Verse" [^marsh1875].

### Timeline

- 1871: The Fisk singers leave Nashville on 6 October [^pike1873][^fisk]

## What the words mean

- **The refrain.** "Didn't my Lord deliver Daniel?" is a question that expects the answer yes. Then comes the real question: "And why not every man?" If God saved Daniel, why not everyone?
- **Verse 1.** Three rescues from the Bible. King Darius has Daniel cast into the den of lions, and in the morning Daniel says, "My God hath sent his angel, and hath shut the lions' mouths" [^kjv]. In the book of Jonah, "the Lord had prepared a great fish to swallow up Jonah", and it put him out on dry land; Jesus, in Matthew's gospel, calls it "the whale's belly" [^kjv]. The Hebrew children are Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego, young men of the "children of Judah" in Babylon, thrown into a "burning fiery furnace" for refusing to worship the king's golden image, and seen walking in the fire unhurt [^kjv].
- **Verse 2.** The signs of the last day. "The moon run down in a purple stream": in Joel, "the moon into blood", and in Revelation, "the moon became as blood" and the stars fall [^kjv]. "The sun forbear to shine": the sun holds back its light. When all that passes, "King Jesus shall be mine."
- **Verse 3.** The wind blows like the judgment day, and everyone who never prayed will be glad to pray then. Our arrangement leaves this verse out.
- **Verse 4.** The singer steps onto "the Gospel ship", and it sails to "Canaan's shore", the promised land across the Jordan, here heaven. The enlarged 1872 book also holds "Old ship of Zion", the same picture of faith as a ship [^seward1872c].

Did the singers mean only heaven? Frederick Douglass wrote in 1855 that when he and his friends sang of reaching Canaan while planning their escape, "the north was our Canaan" [^douglass1855]. In a Fisk Day address printed in 1905, Mrs. Booker T. Washington spoke of the "faith songs" of the waiting enslaved and quoted this one as "the answer given to the cavilling soul" [^washington1905]. James Weldon Johnson wrote in 1925 that the Bible's story of the trials of the Jews fired the imaginations of "the Negro bards", who sang their hearers into faith "that as God saved Daniel in the lion's den, so would He save them" [^johnson1925]. In 1939 the scholar John Lovell Jr. read the question in the Hampton version of the song, why can't He deliver me, as a plain plea for freedom [^lovell1939].

### The full words

These are the words as Jubilee Songs prints them on pages 16 and 17, in its 1872 first edition and in the enlarged edition of the same year that we read, with their spelling [^seward1872][^seward1872c]. The refrain is sung first and after every verse.

**Refrain**
Didn't my Lord deliver Daniel,
D'liver Daniel, d'liver Daniel,
Didn't my Lord d'liver Daniel,
And why not a every man?

**Verse 1**
He deliver'd Daniel from the lion's den,
Jonah from the belly of the whale,
And the Hebrew children from the fiery furnace,
And why not every man?

**Verse 2**
The moon run down in a purple stream,
The sun forbear to shine,
And every star disappear,
King Jesus shall be mine.

**Verse 3**
The wind blows East and the wind blows West,
It blows like the judgment day,
And every poor soul that never did pray,
'll be glad to pray that day.

**Verse 4**
I set my foot on the Gospel ship,
And the ship it begin to sail,
It landed me over on Canaan's shore,
And I'll never come back any more.

## How it is sung

A folk spiritual was sung by enslaved people among themselves; a concert spiritual is arranged for the concert stage, for choirs or solo singers, and the Fisk Jubilee Singers were the first to make the choral spiritual widely known [^carnegie]. It is in church hymnals today, Songs of Zion (1981) and Lift Every Voice and Sing II (1993) among them [^hymnary][^commonprayer].

Our arrangement takes its tune and words from Jubilee Songs, a tone higher, in A minor, with the four parts our own [^seward1872c]. We sing the refrain, then verses 1, 2 and 4, each into the refrain. The soprano sings each verse alone, and everyone joins its last line: "And why not every man?", "King Jesus shall be mine." and "And I'll never come back any more." Unlike the 1872 footnote, we hold the verse's last word a beat and take a breath before the refrain. Two singers are soprano and alto; three add the bass, four the tenor.

## Versions and other words

- **Hampton, 1874.** Hampton and Its Students, a book by two teachers at Hampton Institute in Virginia, with fifty "Cabin and Plantation Songs" arranged by Thomas P. Fenner, prints My Lord Delivered Daniel to another tune, its refrain in four parts. The refrain asks why He can't deliver me, and the fourth verse is the Fisk song's first verse, in the book's dialect spelling [^hampton1874].
- **Burleigh, 1921.** Harry T. Burleigh's setting for solo voice and piano, from his "Jubilee Songs" of the United States of America, keeps the 1872 key of G minor and its 4/8 time, marked Andante [^burleigh1921][^imslp-burleigh].
- **The Johnsons, 1925.** In The Book of American Negro Spirituals, J. Rosamond Johnson arranged it for voice and piano, also in G minor, "Lively (With spirit)", dedicated to Robert Russa Moton [^johnson1925].
- **Moses Hogan.** Moses Hogan arranged it for unaccompanied mixed choir in eight parts, published by Hal Leonard [^halleonard][^musicanet].

## Did you know?

- Burleigh's 1921 page has a footnote on the very first word: pronounce "Didn't" as "dident" [^burleigh1921].
- The refrain's question became a book title: in 1961 the historian Herbert Aptheker called his documentary history of the fight against slavery And Why Not Every Man?, published in Berlin [^aptheker1961].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/didnt-my-lord-deliver-daniel/soprano): you carry the whole tune, built on only five notes, A, C, D, E and G, mostly on C and D. You sing every verse alone until the others join its last line. Your top E comes on "Daniel," after each opening "Didn't my Lord deliver" and on "why" in the refrain; your low G on the start of "deliver" after "Didn't my Lord".
- [Alto](/songs/didnt-my-lord-deliver-daniel/alto): you sing a steady low line, mostly on C, through the refrain and each verse's last line, and rest while the soprano sings the verse. You rise to your top E on the third "Daniel," of each refrain and on "why not", "Jesus" and "never come" in the verses' last lines, and dip to A on "every" in "And why not a every man?".
- [Tenor](/songs/didnt-my-lord-deliver-daniel/tenor): you sit mostly on A, then G, and join only the last line of each verse. Your low E comes on "a" and "man?" in "And why not a every man?", with F between them on "every": tune that half step carefully.
- [Bass](/songs/didnt-my-lord-deliver-daniel/bass): you sing the roots, mostly a low A, then C, with G under the second "Daniel". Your bottom F comes on "every" in each refrain's "And why not a every man?". You join each verse's last line on A, with G only on "every", "shall" and "back".

## Questions

### What does "and why not every man" mean?

It means: if God delivered Daniel from the lions, Jonah from the great fish and the Hebrew children from the furnace, He can deliver anyone [^seward1872][^kjv]. Mrs. Booker T. Washington called the song "the answer given to the cavilling soul", the fault-finder, the doubter [^washington1905]. Sung by enslaved people, it could carry hope of freedom in this world as well as the next, as Frederick Douglass said of Canaan [^douglass1855][^lovell1939].

### When was Didn't My Lord Deliver Daniel written?

No one knows. Its earliest known printing is in Jubilee Songs (New York, 1872), taken down from the Fisk Jubilee Singers, who said they had learned most of their songs as children. When and where it was made is not recorded [^seward1872].

### Is this Moses Hogan's arrangement?

No. Moses Hogan's well-known setting is for unaccompanied choir in eight parts [^halleonard][^musicanet]. Ours goes back to the 1872 page: the Fisk singers' own unison tune and words, a tone higher, with four simple parts of our own under it, and the verses sung by the soprano alone [^seward1872c].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano, all the way through. It is the tune of 1872, which the book printed for everyone to sing together, and it uses only five notes [^seward1872c]. The others sing the refrain in harmony under it and join each verse's last line.
