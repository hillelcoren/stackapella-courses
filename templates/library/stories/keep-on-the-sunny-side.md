Keep on the Sunny Side, printed in 1899 as "Keep On the Sunny Side of Life", is a gospel song with words by Ada Blenkhorn and music by J. Howard Entwisle [^bm1899][^hymnary-text]. Its earliest known printing is in Bright Melodies, a Philadelphia songbook made "for the Sunday School and Young People's Societies" [^bm1899][^hymnary-text]. In May 1928 the Carter Family, A. P., Sara and Maybelle Carter, recorded it for Victor in Camden, New Jersey [^ev-ap][^fw32431]. It became the trio's theme song, the song that often opened their radio shows [^cmhof][^fw32431][^delrio]. In 2000 it was heard again on the soundtrack of the film O Brother, Where Art Thou? [^umusic][^stinfo].

## Where it comes from

**Ada Blenkhorn.** She was born in 1858 in Cobourg, Ontario, the tenth of eleven children, and raised a Methodist; in 1884 her family moved to Cleveland, Ohio [^hymnary-blenkhorn][^ldsh]. She began writing hymns in 1892 [^gabriel1916][^hymnary-blenkhorn]. In her own account, which Charles H. Gabriel printed in 1916, she almost gave it up, until a woman at a convention in Ohio told her that a soul might be converted through a hymn she had not yet written. Those words, she wrote, "seemed an invisible but mighty chain that held me fast and would not let me give up" [^gabriel1916]. She never married, worked as secretary in her brother Henry's real estate business and became its president after he died in 1923 [^hymnary-blenkhorn][^ldsh]. She died in 1927 [^ldsh][^hymnary-blenkhorn].

**The sunny side of the street.** Gabriel's 1916 book sketched living gospel hymn writers, and Blenkhorn was still alive and writing. He named "Keep On the Sunny Side of Life" among her best-known hymns and, in his own words rather than hers, said where it came from: she got the idea while wheeling a nephew who was an invalid, and who always wanted to ride "on the sunny side of the street" [^gabriel1916]. Later tellings repeat it [^ldsh].

**The tune and the 1899 page.** J. Howard Entwisle was a musician and songbook editor in Philadelphia in the 1890s [^hymnary-entwisle][^bm1899]. With John R. Sweney he edited Bright Melodies, published in 1899 by John J. Hood of Philadelphia and Chicago, at thirty cents a copy [^bm1899][^hymnary-entwisle]. Entwisle died in 1901, two years after the song was printed [^showalter1904][^hymnary-entwisle]. The editors' preface says it was made for Sunday schools and young people's societies, mixing new songs by "the leading sacred song writers of the day" with favourites already tried in use [^bm1899]. The song is on page 128, its title in black-letter type, "Ada Blenkhorn." on the left and "J. Howard Entwisle." on the right [^bm1899]. It is set in four parts on two staves, in round notes, in A-flat and in four, with a dotted, skipping rhythm on almost every beat. Three verses sit between the staves, then a chorus, and at the foot is "Copyright, 1899, by J. Howard Entwisle" [^bm1899]. On the page before it is "Abide with Me"; on the page after, a song by Ida L. Reed beginning "Gladly the bells are ringing" [^bm1899]. The copy that was scanned carries the bookplate of the hymnologist Louis FitzGerald Benson, who left his library to Princeton Theological Seminary [^bm1899][^hymnary-benson].

**Heavenly Sunlight, 1900.** A year later Entwisle printed it again as No. 40 of Heavenly Sunlight, a book he compiled with others, published by MacCalla and Company of Dock Street, Philadelphia [^hs1900][^hymnary-entwisle]. The setting is the same, line for line, now under a plain capital heading, with the copyright date in Roman numerals and "His care" given a capital [^hs1900][^bm1899]. This is the printing our arrangement is read from.

**Into the hymnals.** The song spread through gospel songbooks: Pentecostal Hymns Number Three (Chicago, 1902) has it as No. 28, and Christ in Song (1908) as No. 243 [^ph1902][^cis1908]. Christ in Song heads it with a line from Psalm 36, "In thy light I shall see light", and its copyright line adds "Used by permission of John J. Hood, owner" [^cis1908].

**The Carter Family.** The trio was A. P. Carter, from Maces Spring in Scott County, Virginia, his wife Sara, and Sara's cousin Maybelle, who had married A. P.'s brother [^ev-ap][^ev-maybelle][^fw32431]. They made their first records for Victor at Bristol, Tennessee, on 1 and 2 August 1927 [^cmhof][^ev-ap]. Their second session, in Camden, New Jersey, in May 1928, included "Keep on the Sunny Side" [^ev-ap][^ev-maybelle][^fw32431]. Sara sang lead and played the autoharp, Maybelle played guitar and sang harmony, and A. P. joined in now and then with a bass part [^ev-ap][^cmhof]. The Country Music Hall of Fame lists it among their best-sellers of 1928, and it became their theme song [^cmhof][^fw32431]. In the late 1930s, broadcasting from the border station XERA at Del Rio, Texas, they generally opened with it, and it was heard across the country [^delrio][^cmhof].

**Myth and record.** Often told: A. P. Carter wrote it. Records credit the Carters: a Canadian Bluebird pressing of the Carter Family's record prints "(A. P. Carter)" under the title, and a 1979 album of Carter songs says "All words & music © The Carter Family" [^bluebird][^fw32431]. What the record shows: the song was printed in 1899 under Blenkhorn's and Entwisle's names, when A. P. Carter, born in December 1891, was a boy of seven or eight [^bm1899][^ev-ap][^cmhof]. The Country Music Hall of Fame counts him among the greatest song collectors and arrangers in country music; this song was not his own [^cmhof][^hymnary-text].

## What the words mean

1. Life has a dark, troubled side and a bright, sunny side. "Strife" is conflict, struggle. Even while we meet darkness and trouble, the sunny side is still there to see.
2. "Tho' the storm in its fury break to-day" means "even if the storm should break in all its fury today", crushing hopes we held dear. Storms and clouds pass in time, and the sun shines again.
3. The turn to faith: greet each day with a song of hope, whether its hours are cloudy or fair, and trust the Saviour "alway", an old form of always, who "keepeth", keeps and guards, everyone in his care.

The chorus is the advice itself: keep to the sunny side, every day, all the way. Gabriel's story gives it a plain picture, a nephew asking to be wheeled along the side of the street the sun is on [^gabriel1916]. Christ in Song's heading points to Psalm 36:9, "in thy light shall we see light" [^cis1908][^kjv].

### The full words

Ada Blenkhorn's three verses and chorus as printed in Heavenly Sunlight (Philadelphia, 1900), No. 40, the printing our arrangement is read from [^hs1900]. Bright Melodies (1899) has the same words, with "his care" in small letters [^bm1899]. All are in the public domain: they were printed in 1899, and Blenkhorn died in 1927.

**Verse 1**
There's a dark and a troubled side of life;
There's a bright and a sunny side, too;
Tho' we meet with the darkness and strife,
The sunny side we also may view.

**Chorus**
Keep on the sunny side,
Always on the sunny side,
Keep on the sunny side of life;
It will help us ev'ry day,
It will brighten all the way,
If we keep on the sunny side of life.

**Verse 2**
Tho' the storm in its fury break to-day,
Crushing hopes that we cherished so dear;
Storm and cloud will in time pass away,
The sun again will shine bright and clear.

**Verse 3**
Let us greet with a song of hope each day,
Tho' the moments be cloudy or fair;
Let us trust in our Saviour alway,
Who keepeth ev'ry one in His care.

## How it is sung

It is a gospel song: a verse and a chorus, easy to learn, made for Sunday schools, young people's societies and evangelistic services, and printed in four parts for singing in harmony [^bm1899][^hs1900][^ph1902]. The Carter Family sang it as a trio, "singing with auto-harp and guitar", as the label of their record says [^bluebird][^ev-ap].

Our arrangement sings verse 1 and the chorus, in A-flat as Entwisle printed it, for 2 to 4 singers, bright and bouncing. The soprano has the tune, and everyone sings the words. Two singers are soprano and alto; a third adds the bass, and a fourth the tenor. We keep Entwisle's own four parts, with the alto taking the chord's third from the tenor in a few places.

## Versions and other words

- A different song with the same title, "Keep on the Sunny Side" by Theodore F. Morse and Jack Drislane, was a popular song of 1906, recorded that year for both Victor and Columbia [^jukebox1906a][^jukebox1906b]. It has nothing to do with Blenkhorn and Entwisle's.
- Pentecostal Hymns (1902) spells "Savior", and Christ in Song (1908) prints "breaks to-day" for "break to-day" [^ph1902][^cis1908].
- The song is on the 2023 soundtrack album of The Hunger Games: The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes [^fmr2023][^holler2023].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/keep-on-the-sunny-side/soprano): you carry the bouncing tune and its dotted rhythm. Your top E-flat comes on "bright and a sunny" and on "darkness" in the verse, then on "Keep on the sunny" at the start of the chorus and on "help us" later in it.
- [Alto](/songs/keep-on-the-sunny-side/alto): you sing the words, most often a sixth or a third under the tune, mostly on E-flat and C. Hold G through "meet with the darkness and", and after the rest at the end of the verse, leap up an octave into the chorus.
- [Tenor](/songs/keep-on-the-sunny-side/tenor): you sing the words in the middle of the chord, mostly on A-flat and E-flat, with many repeated notes; keep the dotted rhythm crisp on them.
- [Bass](/songs/keep-on-the-sunny-side/bass): you sing the bass line, mostly on A-flat and E-flat, up to your high A-flat on "life" and "strife" in the verse, the chorus's opening "Keep on the sunny side", and "day, It will".

## Questions

### Who was the original artist of Keep on the Sunny Side?

No single artist: it began as a gospel song printed for Sunday schools. Ada Blenkhorn wrote the words and J. Howard Entwisle the music, and Bright Melodies printed it in Philadelphia in 1899 [^bm1899][^hymnary-text]. The record that made it famous is the Carter Family's, made for Victor in Camden, New Jersey, in May 1928 [^ev-ap][^fw32431]. Some later labels credit A. P. Carter, but the 1899 page names Blenkhorn and Entwisle [^bluebird][^bm1899].

### Is Keep on the Sunny Side a hymn?

It is a gospel song, the verse-and-chorus kind of hymn made for Sunday schools and evangelistic meetings. It was written for a Sunday-school songbook in 1899 and soon went into gospel hymnals such as Pentecostal Hymns (1902) and Christ in Song (1908), which sets it under a verse from Psalm 36 [^bm1899][^ph1902][^cis1908]. Hymnary.org indexes it in more than fifty hymnals [^hymnary-text].

### Is Keep on the Sunny Side in O Brother, Where Art Thou?

Yes. It is track 8 on the soundtrack album of the 2000 film, issued by Mercury Nashville [^umusic][^stinfo]. By then the song was a century old: Ada Blenkhorn and J. Howard Entwisle's gospel song of 1899, which the Carter Family's record of 1928 had carried into country music [^bm1899][^ev-ap][^cmhof].

### Is Keep on the Sunny Side public domain?

The song is: words and music were published in the United States in 1899, and works published there before 1931 are in the public domain [^bm1899][^cornell]. Recordings are separate. A US record of that era is protected for 100 years from its first publication, so the Carter Family's record, made in 1928 and issued in 1928 or 1929 (sources differ), stays protected until the end of 2028 or 2029 [^copyright-office][^cornell]. Our arrangement is read from the 1900 printing.

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano, as in Entwisle's printing, where the tune is the top line of the treble staff [^hs1900]. In our arrangement the alto sings mostly a sixth or a third under it, the tenor fills the middle of the chord and the bass sings the bass line, all four on the words together.
