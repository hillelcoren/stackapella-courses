Asleep in the Deep is a song of 1897, with words by Arthur J. Lamb and music by H. W. Petrie [^nypl1897][^ascap1948]. It tells of a ship in a storm, a lighthouse bell ringing its warning, and two lovers on deck who go down with the wreck [^levy1901][^nypl1897]. Its refrain ends on the line that names the song: "Many brave hearts are asleep in the deep, So beware! beware!" [^levy1901]. It was published as a bass solo with a male quartet, and in that edition the last "beware!" sinks to a low D, below the bass staff [^nypl1897]. Bass singers recorded it from its first years, and the Parlor Songs Academy, a site on the period's songs, calls it a "war horse" for basses [^ucsb-hooley][^dahr-hooley][^parlorsongs].

## Where it comes from

**The writers.** Arthur J. Lamb was born in Somerset, England, in 1870, and made his career as a songwriter in America [^ascap1948][^parlorsongs][^ingeb]. A few years later he wrote the words of another lasting hit, A Bird in a Gilded Cage [^spaeth1926][^ascap1948]. Henry W. Petrie was born in Bloomington, Illinois, in 1857 [^ascap1948][^parlorsongs][^loc-petrie]. He also wrote a children's song of the 1890s, I Don't Want to Play in Your Yard, which the song historian Sigmund Spaeth counted among the most popular of its kind [^spaeth1927][^parlorsongs].

**The bass edition.** A copy in the New York Public Library shows the song as it was sold to basses [^nypl1897]. Its red cover reads "Song for Baritone or Bass Voice", with a photograph of a singer, Geo. H. Carr, "As sung with success by", and a dedication "to Mr. W. Nicholson, Corning, N.Y." [^nypl1897]. At the foot, the publisher F. A. Mills of 48 West Twenty-ninth Street, New York, lists the bass edition in D and a baritone or contralto edition in F, at 50 cents each, and an orchestra arrangement [^nypl1897]. Inside, under the title "(Bass Solo and Male Quartette Chorus.)", the first page is marked "Copyright, 1897, by Julie C. Petrie. English copyright secured" [^nypl1897]. The voice part is written in the bass clef, in 12/8, after a piano introduction [^nypl1897].

**A quartet like a bell.** The chorus is marked "Solo voice loud. Chorus very soft and stac.": under "Loudly the bell in the old tower rings", two tenors and two basses answer in short, soft chords, "Loud the bell in the old tow'r rings, yes!", like a bell striking [^nypl1897]. At the end the soloist falls from A to the low D, held under a pause, while the quartet sings its last "Beware!" ppp, as softly as possible [^nypl1897].

**On the minstrel stage.** The song was soon sung in minstrel shows. In December 1898 the New York Journal and Advertiser gave it away as a music supplement, in F, under Julie C. Petrie's 1897 copyright, and its cover reads "Sung by John W. Early with J. H. Haverly's American-European Minstrels" [^nypl1898]. A history of minstrelsy published in 1911 says Early, a baritone, joined Haverly's company in 1898 [^rice1911], and the Parlor Songs Academy also names Early and Haverly's minstrels as the song's singers that year [^parlorsongs]. A minstrel show was a stage entertainment in which white performers darkened their faces to mock Black people. The supplement's cover shows no performers at all, only ships, sea horses and a lighthouse in a storm [^nypl1898].

**The copy we read.** Our arrangement is read from a later printing in the Lester S. Levy Sheet Music Collection at Johns Hopkins University [^levy1901]. Its cover is the same design, with a photograph of another singer, Harry Tanner, and Mills at 721 Seventh Avenue [^levy1901]. Inside it is headed "(Baritone or Contralto.)", notes that the song is also published in D as a bass solo with male quartet, and carries the line "Copyright, 1901, by F. A. Mills" [^levy1901]. Its music is the engraving the New York Journal printed in 1898, down to the same misprint, "tqe key of D"; only the copyright line has changed, from Julie C. Petrie's of 1897 to Mills's of 1901 [^levy1901][^nypl1898]. Mills was advertising from that address in 1914, so this copy was printed years after the date on it [^billboard1914]. Here the voice is printed in the treble clef, in F (our bass sings it an octave lower), with both verses under the one tune, the refrain printed once, and "ad lib." over the first "Many brave hearts" [^levy1901].

**Myth and record.** Taken for: a traditional sea song. Kurt Weill, who quoted its German version in an opera, seems to have thought it a traditional German sea song [^drew]. What the record shows: it is a song for voice and piano by two professional songwriters, copyrighted in 1897 and sold as sheet music [^nypl1897][^nypl1898][^ascap1948]. Its covers name the stage singers who sang it "with success" [^nypl1897][^levy1901].

## What the words mean

1. **Verse 1.** A storm, high waves, and a ship that rides them bravely ("doth ride": does ride). Hark, listen: a lighthouse bell rings its solemn warning over the sullen tide, the gloomy, threatening sea [^webster-sullen]. On deck two lovers stand hand in hand. Though death is near, she is not afraid, because the one she loves most of all is beside her.
2. **Verse 2.** The morning after. The storm has left no trace, except the wreckage it has strewn, scattered, along the shore, and the sun shines peacefully. When the storm ended, the two lovers found peace under the billows, the great waves of the sea [^webster-billow]: they drowned together. They will never be parted and never feel pain again, and the bell may toll, ring slowly as for a funeral [^webster-toll], but its warning comes too late for them.
3. **Refrain.** The bell in the old tower rings out, bidding us list, listen [^webster-list], to its warning. "Sailor, take care!" Danger is near. The "brave hearts asleep in the deep" are the drowned, lying at the bottom of the sea, and the song ends by warning the living to beware [^levy1901][^nypl1897].

### The full words

These are the two verses and the refrain as printed in the Mills edition in F (copyright 1901), the printing our arrangement is read from. The verses share one tune, and the refrain, printed once, follows each verse; its last line is sung twice. Lamb died in 1928 and Petrie in 1925, and the song was copyrighted in 1897, so all of it is in the public domain.

**Verse 1**
Stormy the night and the waves roll high,
Bravely the ship doth ride;
Hark! while the light-house bell's solemn cry
Rings o'er the sullen tide.
There on the deck see two lovers stand,
Heart to heart beating and hand in hand,
Tho' death be near, she knows no fear,
While at her side is one of all most dear.

**Verse 2**
What of the storm when the night is o'er?
There is no trace or sign!
Save where the wreckage hath strewn the shore,
Peaceful the sun doth shine.
But when the wild raging storm did cease,
Under the billows two hearts found peace,
No more to part, no more of pain,
The bell may now toll its warning in vain.

**Refrain**
Loudly the bell in the old tower rings,
Bidding us list to the warning it brings.
Sailor, take care! Sailor, take care!
Danger is near thee, Beware! Beware!
Beware! Beware!
Many brave hearts are asleep in the deep,
So beware! beware!
Many brave hearts are asleep in the deep,
So beware! beware!

## How it is sung

Asleep in the Deep is a parlour song of the 1890s, written for one voice and piano, in 12/8, and sold as sheet music [^levy1901][^nypl1897]. Mills sold it in D for a bass, with a male quartet joining in the chorus, and in F for a baritone or contralto [^nypl1897][^levy1901]. The Discography of American Historical Recordings lists the bass William F. Hooley recording it for Victor in 1901 and 1903, and the bass Wilfred Glenn in 1926 [^dahr-hooley][^dahr-glenn].

Our arrangement is read from the Mills printing in F, checked against M. Witmark and Sons' edition in D [^levy1901][^imslp]. We sing the first verse and the refrain, for 2 to 4 singers, slow and rolling, with the 12/8 written as 6/8. The bass sings Petrie's tune and all the words, as he wrote it, with breaths marked for beginners, and leaves out the small slide note into the last "ware!". The alto, soprano and tenor hold soft chords on "oo" built on Petrie's piano harmony, a little like the quartet in his bass edition, which sings very softly under the soloist in the chorus [^nypl1897]. With two singers, the alto joins the bass; with three, the soprano; with four, the tenor.

## Versions and other words

- **In German.** A German version begins "Stürmisch die Nacht" ("Stormy the night") [^drew][^mahagonny1929]. The Parlor Songs Academy gives it the title Des Seemanns Los, The Sailor's Lot [^parlorsongs].
- **In an opera.** In Act 2, No. 16 of Bertolt Brecht and Kurt Weill's opera Aufstieg und Fall der Stadt Mahagonny (Rise and Fall of the City of Mahagonny), the drunken men in a bar build a "ship" out of a billiard table, and Jim, Jenny and Bill pretend to sail back to Alaska. When the men act out a storm, Jenny says the best thing is to sing "Stürmisch die Nacht" to keep their courage up, and the three sing the opening of the German version [^mahagonny1929]. The Weill scholar David Drew wrote that the quotation is exact, a merry tune recalled by drunken singers [^drew].
- **Other keys.** M. Witmark and Sons later published it too: IMSLP holds their editions of 1920 in D for low voice, in B-flat and in F [^imslp].

## Singing it

- [Bass](/songs/asleep-in-the-deep/bass): you carry Petrie's tune and all the words, from a low F to a high D, so take the top and bottom notes gently. "Stormy the night" climbs the chord from C an octave up and back down. Your high D comes on "lovers stand", on "-or" of the first "Sailor" and on "Danger". After "Danger", the last two "Beware!"s walk down, D, C, B-flat, A. The first "Many brave hearts are asleep in the deep" climbs a scale from low G to B-flat; the second walks down to the last "beware!", held on the low F.
- [Alto](/songs/asleep-in-the-deep/alto): you hold soft chords on "oo" in the middle, mostly on A and E, between a low A and a C. In "Tho' death be near, she knows no fear" you rock between G-sharp and C under the tune; tune the G-sharp high. Your low A comes near the end, under the last "ware!" of the first "So beware! beware!".
- [Soprano](/songs/asleep-in-the-deep/soprano): you float soft "oo" chords high above the bass, mostly on C, with B-flat, D and A. Your one top E comes early, under "ship" in "Bravely the ship doth ride". Under the last of the falling "Beware!"s you hold a C-sharp, the bright note of an A major chord.
- [Tenor](/songs/asleep-in-the-deep/tenor): you sing close to the bass's tune on "oo", mostly on C. As the refrain begins you rise to F and hold it under "Loudly the bell"; under "Sailor" you touch an E-flat, and under the first of the falling "Beware!"s you drop to your low F.

## Questions

### What is the lowest note in Asleep in the Deep?

It depends on the edition. In Petrie's bass edition in D (copyright 1897), the last "Beware!" falls from A to the low D below the bass staff, held under a pause [^nypl1897]. The edition in F, for baritone or contralto, ends the same phrase on a low F [^levy1901]. Our arrangement follows the F edition, so our bass ends on the low F.

### What does Asleep in the Deep mean?

"Asleep in the deep" means drowned and lying at the bottom of the sea. In Lamb's words two lovers stand together on a ship in a storm, and by morning only wreckage is left on the shore: "Under the billows two hearts found peace" [^levy1901]. The New York Public Library's catalogue sums it up the same way, with the lovers at rest [^nypl1897]. The refrain turns their story into a warning to every sailor: beware.

### Was Asleep in the Deep written for a bass?

Mostly, yes. Its covers call it a "Song for Baritone or Bass Voice", and Mills sold it in two keys: in D as a bass solo with a male quartet, and in F for baritone or contralto [^nypl1897][^levy1901]. Bass singers recorded it from its first years, and the Parlor Songs Academy calls it a "war horse" for basses [^ucsb-hooley][^dahr-hooley][^parlorsongs].

### Which part sings the tune?

The bass. Because its covers call it a song for baritone or bass voice, our bass sings the tune and all the words, from "Stormy the night" to the last "beware!" on the low F [^levy1901]. The alto, soprano and tenor hold soft chords on "oo" above it, on the harmony of Petrie's piano part.
