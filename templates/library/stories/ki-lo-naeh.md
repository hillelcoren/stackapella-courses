Ki Lo Na'eh is a Hebrew hymn sung at the Passover seder, the home service and festive meal on the first nights of the festival.[^je][^ej] It comes near the end, after the Haggadah's formal service has closed, among the songs that finish the night, and it is sung mainly in Ashkenazi homes.[^ej][^sefaria][^bogomilsky] Its verses run through the Hebrew alphabet. Each one calls God mighty, chosen, holy or strong in His kingship, then names a company of His servants who answer: Yours, and Yours, Lord, is the kingdom.[^ej][^sefaria] The table has sung it for more than seven centuries. A London scholar copied it into his book of Jewish law in 1287, and the Reform movement's Haggadah still printed it with music in 1908 and 1923.[^kaufmann][^jejacob][^uh1908][^uh1923]

## Where it comes from

No one knows who wrote it, or when. The Jewish Encyclopedia of 1904 dated it to the fifteenth century, and the Union Haggadah of 1923 says that in that century "the two anonymous ditties 'Addir Hu' and 'Ki Lo Noeh' were added".[^je][^uh1923] The record is older than that. In 1892 the scholar David Kaufmann published the seder service from the manuscript of Etz Hayyim, a book of Jewish law by Jacob ben Judah of London, dated 1287. The Encyclopaedia Judaica puts it at about 1286.[^kaufmann][^jejacob][^ejroth] Kaufmann notes that of the free poems that graced the seder in English homes, Jacob's book keeps only this one. Kaufmann noted that the scholar Zunz had dated it to the fifteenth century. Yet here it was, in Kaufmann's words, already 'a firmly established portion of the English Ritual' before the Jews were expelled from England in 1290.[^kaufmann][^jeengland]

His copy also has extra lines whose first letters spell the name Jacob.[^kaufmann][^ej][^davidson] Kaufmann read them as Jacob's addition to a poem that already stood, and thought Jacob's text the original form, changed since in both the German and the Roman rites. The Encyclopaedia Judaica allows that Jacob may have written the whole poem, and Rabbi Jonathan Sacks called it perhaps Britain's one contribution to the Haggadah.[^kaufmann][^davidson][^ej][^sacks]

The hymn was once paired with Adir Hu, which follows it in the Haggadah and runs through the alphabet the same way. The Jewish Encyclopedia says Ki Lo Na'eh was first meant for the first night of Passover, with Adir Hu chosen for the second, and that for more than two centuries both had been sung on each night.[^je][^jeaddir][^niv]

Johann Stephan Rittangel, a Christian Hebraist at Königsberg, published a Haggadah with Latin and German translations there in 1644, the first Haggadah printed with musical notation. He gave tunes for two songs, Ki Lo Na'eh and Adir Hu.[^brown][^niv][^je][^idel29] The Jewish Encyclopedia reprinted his Ki Lo Na'eh tune in 1904 as the most appealing of the several airs the hymn had been sung to. It is a tune in 3/4 time, quite unlike ours.[^je] Ours is the one the Union Haggadah printed, marked "Traditional" with no source named.[^jeaddir][^je][^uh1923]

### Timeline

- 1287: Jacob ben Judah of London copies the hymn into his Etz Hayyim (about 1286, says the Encyclopaedia Judaica). [^kaufmann][^jejacob][^ejroth]
- 1644: Rittangel's Haggadah, printed at Königsberg, gives a tune for it, in the first Haggadah printed with music. [^brown][^niv]
- 1904: The Jewish Encyclopedia reprints Rittangel's tune with an English paraphrase. [^je]
- 1908: The Union Haggadah, from the Central Conference of American Rabbis, prints our tune with its first verse. [^uh1908][^uh1923]

## What the words mean

The title is the hymn's last line: ki lo naeh, ki lo yaeh. Ki means for, lo means to Him, and both naeh and yaeh mean fitting or due. "For to Him it is fitting, to Him it is due."[^sefaria][^baruch] The words echo Jeremiah 10:7, "ki lecha yaata", for to You it is due.[^tanakh][^baruch][^sacks] The hymn is also called Adir Bimlucha, after its first words.[^ej][^davidson]

The full hymn has eight stanzas, each the same except for three words whose first letters march through the alphabet. The last stanza uses the final letter, tav, three times.[^je][^sefaria] The Union Haggadah prints three of them, and we sing those: the first, seventh and eighth.[^uh1923][^sefaria]

**Verse 1.** Adir bimlucha: mighty in kingship. Bachur kahalacha: chosen, as is right, truly. Gedudav yomru lo: His troops say to Him.[^sefaria][^baruch]

**Verse 2.** Kadosh bimlucha: holy in kingship. Rachum kahalacha: compassionate, as is right. Shinanav yomru lo: His angels say to Him.[^sefaria][^lissa]

**Verse 3.** Takif bimlucha: powerful in kingship. Tomech kahalacha: upholding, as is right. Temimav yomru lo: His perfect ones, the wholehearted, say to Him.[^sefaria]

**The answer, after every verse.** Lecha ulecha: Yours and Yours. Lecha ki lecha: Yours, for it is Yours. Lecha af lecha: Yours, yes, Yours. Lecha Adoshem hamamlacha: Yours, Lord, is the kingdom. Then the title line again.[^sefaria] The answer is built from Bible phrases: "Yours, Lord, is the kingdom" is from 1 Chronicles 29:11, and "the heavens are Yours, the earth too" from Psalm 89:12 gives the pattern of "lecha af lecha".[^tanakh][^baruch][^sacks] Listen for the turn: the hymn speaks about God, "to Him", but the hosts speak to God, "Yours".[^sefaria]

Who are the speakers? The Encyclopaedia Judaica reads them all as kinds of angels.[^ej] Jacob of Lissa, in his Haggadah commentary Maaseh Nissim, splits them: the troops and the angels are angels, but the righteous and the perfect ones are Israel. He reads the names through midrash too. The midrash says God appeared at the Red Sea as a warrior and at Sinai as an elder full of compassion, and he and other teachers read bachur, which can also mean a young man, as that young warrior. Rachum recalls the Talmud's story that God stopped the angels singing as the Egyptians drowned, because they too were the work of His hands.[^lissa][^ejlissa][^bogomilsky][^rashi][^megillah]

Where the Hebrew has the four-letter name of God, which the Union Haggadah spells "Adonoi", we sing Adoshem. Many Jews change God's names when saying them outside prayer, out of reverence and to avoid taking the name in vain; our songs, sung outside prayer, use the changed form, and the meaning is the same.[^ohr][^outorah]

## How it is sung

At a traditional Ashkenazi seder, after the closing wish "Next year in Jerusalem" and two longer poems come Ki Lo Na'eh, Adir Hu, the counting of the Omer, Echad Mi Yodea and Chad Gadya.[^sefaria][^ej] The Union Haggadah puts it after the fourth cup of wine and after Adir Hu, the reverse of the traditional order.[^uh1923][^sefaria]

Our arrangement is read from pages 84 and 85 of the Union Haggadah, revised edition, published by the Central Conference of American Rabbis in 1923. There it is "Ki Lo Noeh", "Traditional", Andante con moto, in C major and 4/4, for voice and piano, in the music as edited by the Society of American Cantors.[^uh1923][^uh1908] It opens with the chorus "Ki lo noeh, ki lo yoeh", then gives each verse as a SOLO and the answer as a CHORUS. The 1908 first edition headed it only "Chorus", with one verse, and put it in the Miscellany, the part kept for the lighter, more joyous side of the evening.[^uh1908]

We sing it a tone higher, in D, with four parts of our own; the piano part was used only to check the chords. The book spells the Hebrew in the Ashkenazi way, "noeh", "bim'lucho", "Adonoi". We respell it in modern Israeli pronunciation, syllable for syllable as the notes carry it.[^uh1923] We sing the chorus, then the three printed verses: the soprano sings each verse alone, as the book's solo, and everyone sings the answer and "Ki lo nae, ki lo yae." The last "yae" is held, as the book's pause sign asks. It is for 2 to 4 singers: soprano and alto, then the bass, then the tenor.

## Versions and other words

The 1923 Union Haggadah prints an English hymn to the same tune two pages before the Hebrew, "Our Souls We Raise In Fervent Praise", with no author named. Like the Hebrew, it has solo verses and a chorus, "To Thee, O to Thee", which echoes "lecha ulecha".[^uh1923]

There are other tunes. The Jewish Encyclopedia mentions several, and in 1929 the music historian A. Z. Idelsohn printed one whose shifting sharps and flats he likened to Ukrainian and Romanian folk song.[^je][^idel29]

## Did you know?

- The answer says "lecha", Yours, seven times. Commentators have read meanings into the number, among them the seven heavens, the seven days of the week and the seven thousand years of the world.[^sacks][^marbeh]
- The Jewish Encyclopedia's English paraphrase of 1904 plays the alphabet game in English, from "Awe by His throne is" to "His zealous ones".[^je]
- The phrase "it is fitting for You" has an old echo. A midrash in Genesis Rabbah reads Psalm 74:16, "Yours is the day, Yours also the night", as: it is fitting for You that we sing by day and by night.[^berrab][^tanakh][^sacks]

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/ki-lo-naeh/soprano): the tune, and every verse alone, mostly on A and F-sharp. In each verse the low D of "gedudav", "shinanav" or "temimav" leaps straight up a sixth to B, and the G-sharp on "yomru" leans up into "lo:". The top D rings on every "yae." and, in each answer, on the first "lecha" and the "lecha" before "af".
- [Alto](/songs/ki-lo-naeh/alto): rests through the solo verses and joins for "Ki lo nae, ki lo yae." and every answer, mostly a third or a sixth below the tune. Its top F-sharp comes on every "yae." and, in each answer, on the first "lecha", "ki lecha," and "lecha af lecha,"; its low A only on the "Ki lo" that opens every "Ki lo nae,".
- [Tenor](/songs/ki-lo-naeh/tenor): rests in the verses, then holds the middle of the chord on F-sharp, G and A nearly all the time. Its top C-sharp comes on the first "lecha" of every answer, and it drops to its low D on every "nae,".
- [Bass](/songs/ki-lo-naeh/bass): rests in the verses, then sings the bass line, mostly on D and B. It falls to its low F-sharp on every "nae," and on "af" and "hamamlacha," in each answer, and climbs to its top F-sharp on the "lecha" before "Adoshem".

## Questions

### What does Ki Lo Na'eh mean in English?

"For to Him it is fitting." Ki is for, lo is to Him, and naeh is fitting; the line goes on "ki lo yaeh", to Him it is due. It closes every verse, after His hosts answer: Yours, Lord, is the kingdom.[^sefaria][^baruch]

### When is Ki Lo Na'eh sung at Passover?

Near the end of the seder, after the meal, the Hallel psalms and the closing wish "Next year in Jerusalem", among the songs that finish the night. The Jewish Encyclopedia says it was once meant for the first night only, with Adir Hu for the second, but both nights have long sung both.[^sefaria][^ej][^je]

### How do you pronounce Ki Lo Na'eh?

Kee, loh, then na'eh as two syllables, nah and eh. The apostrophe shows that the two vowels are said separately. The h only spells the Hebrew letter that ends the word and is not sounded, so our sung words write it "nae". Older Ashkenazi books, like the Union Haggadah, spell it "noeh", with an o. Our tune puts the long note on the second syllable, "e".[^sefaria][^uh1923]

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano, throughout, and it sings each verse alone, as the Union Haggadah's solo. The others join for "Ki lo nae, ki lo yae" and every answer. Two singers make soprano and alto; a third adds the bass, a fourth the tenor.[^uh1923]
