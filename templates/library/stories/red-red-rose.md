A Red, Red Rose, also known by its first line, My Love Is Like a Red, Red Rose, or in Burns's Scots spelling, O My Luve's Like a Red, Red Rose, is a love song by Robert Burns [^smm1796][^smith]. A lover compares his love to a June rose and a sweet tune, swears to love her till the seas run dry, and promises to come back though it were ten thousand miles [^smm1796][^smith]. Its earliest known printing is in Peter Urbani's Selection of Scots Songs of 1794 [^urbani][^dick][^balladindex]. By the 1820s it had been printed to five tunes [^urbani][^smm1796][^dick][^smith]. Today's tune, Low down in the Broom, is the one R. A. Smith set it to in his Scotish Minstrel in the 1820s, its earliest known printing with that air [^smith][^graham][^balladindex].

## Where it comes from

**Burns and the Museum.** Robert Burns (1759 to 1796) wrote and collected many of the songs in The Scots Musical Museum, a book of Scots songs with their tunes, engraved and sold in Edinburgh by James Johnson [^smm1796][^hh3]. He sent this one to Johnson with a note, quoted by William Stenhouse, who had the manuscript before him, and by James C. Dick, who places it in the British Museum: the tune "is in Niel Gow's first collection, and is there called Major Graham" [^stenhouse][^dick]. It is silent on the words.

**Urbani's printing.** Pietro (Peter) Urbani was a singer from Milan who settled in Edinburgh and sang Scots songs at its concerts [^graham][^stenhouse][^urbani-uwo]. Burns met him in 1793 and told the publisher George Thomson he was "a narrow, conceited creature; but he sings so delightfully" [^dick][^gebbie5][^gebbie4]. The second book of Urbani's Selection, entered at Stationers' Hall in 1794, prints "The red red Rose" on pages 16 and 17, headed "The Music by P. Urbani": a slow air in 3/4 time, marked "Largo con molta Espressione", for voice, two violins, viola and harpsichord [^urbani][^graham][^stenhouse]. No poet is named on the page, and some words differ from Burns's later text: "O my love's like the red, red rose", "And I can love thee still", "my dearest Love" [^urbani].

**The Museum, 1796.** The fifth volume of the Museum is undated; the National Library of Scotland and Henley and Henderson date it 1796 [^smm1796][^hh3]. Its preface mourns "the late celebrated Scottish Bard", so it came out after Burns died, and its index gives the song to Burns [^smm1796]. On page 415, No. 402, "A red red Rose" is set in F, "Slow", to the strathspey Major Graham, signed with the letter R [^smm1796][^hh3][^dick]. That setting holds only three stanzas [^smm1796][^dick]; Stenhouse quotes a note by Stephen Clarke, who arranged the music, that once through the tune took in all the words "except the last four lines" [^stenhouse]. On the next page, No. 403, the "Old Set", gives all four stanzas to an older air of one strain, which Stenhouse calls Mary Queen of Scots and G. F. Graham calls Queen Mary's Lament [^smm1796][^stenhouse][^graham].

**The older songs behind it.** Graham, writing in the late 1840s, calls it an old song that Burns revised and extended, and its lines echo cheap chapbook songs [^graham][^hh3]. Graham names, and Dick quotes, a stall ballad, The Turtle Dove, or True Love's Farewell: "O fare you well, my own true love, O farewell for a while ... If I go ten thousand miles" [^graham][^dick]. Graham and Henley and Henderson point to a loyal lover's farewell in a chapbook, The Horn Fair Garland, with "And the Rocks melt with the Sun" [^graham][^hh3]. Burns's name is written in that copy in a boyish hand; Graham reported that it was believed to be Burns's own copy, while Henley and Henderson said the writing could no longer be proved genuine [^graham][^hh3]. Henley and Henderson, following the ballad editor J. W. Ebsworth, also hear the opening in an old black-letter ballad, The Wanton Wife of Castle Gate, whose cheeks "are like the Roses That blossom fresh in June" [^hh3]; Graham found the first six lines in none of the old versions he knew [^graham].

William Scott Douglas, in a note reprinted in an American edition of Burns in 1886, wrote that no editor had shown the lines in print before the Museum; in 1897 Henley and Henderson answered him with a chapbook dated 1792 [^gebbie4][^hh3]. Graham also printed a family memory: Burns's sister, Mrs Begg, told Captain Charles Gray that it was one of the old songs their mother sang, and recalled lines such as "Your friends they are displeased wi' me" [^graham][^hh1][^dick].

**Myth and record.** Often told in the 19th century: the song that gave Burns his ideas was a farewell written by a Lieutenant Hinches, a claim made by Peter Buchan [^graham][^hh3]. What the record shows: Buchan gave nothing more about Hinches, not even when or where he lived, as Graham noted, and Henley and Henderson dismissed Buchan's text as a poor patchwork of a chapbook song [^graham][^hh3].

**The tune we sing.** Low down in the Broom is in the first volume of the Museum (1787), No. 90, "Andante", in C, with an older song, "My Daddy is a canker'd carle", whose refrain runs "For he's low down, he's in the broom, That's waiting on me" [^smm1787][^stenhouse]. Robert Archibald Smith (1780 to 1829), who became precentor, the leader of the singing, at St George's Church, Edinburgh, in 1823, published The Scotish Minstrel in six volumes between 1821 and 1824 [^dnb][^graham][^smith-nls]. Its undated third volume prints the song on page 85, in C for voice and piano, and its index names the words as Burns's and the air as Low down in the Broom [^smith]. Smith's page adds a four-line refrain to each verse by repeating its closing lines [^smith]. Graham called the tune "a modern version" of the old air, and warned that some airs Smith gave as ancient were his own [^graham][^dnb].

## What the words mean

The song is in English with a few Scots words. We sing two verses as Smith printed them, each made of two of Burns's four stanzas and a refrain [^smith].

**Stanza 1.** My love is like a red rose just opened in June, and like a melody ("melodie") played sweetly and in tune.

**Stanza 2.** You are as lovely ("bonnie") as I am deep in love, and I will love you "'till a' the seas gang dry": until all the seas go dry, that is, forever. A' means all and gang means go [^dick][^hh4].

**Stanza 3.** Until the seas go dry and the rocks melt with ("wi'") the sun, I will love you while the sands of life run: the sand in the hourglass, the time left to live [^dick][^hh4].

**Stanza 4.** Now he must go. Fare thee weel means farewell, weel being well, and awhile means for a while. He will come again, "Tho' 'twere ten thousand mile", even if it were ten thousand miles [^dick][^hh4].

Smith's words differ from the Museum's: "love" for its "luve" in places, "a melodie That sweetly play'd" for "the melodie That's sweetly play'd", "sands of life" for "sands o' life", "But, fare thee weel ... O fare thee weel awhile!" for "And fare thee weel ... And fare thee weel, a while!", and "'twere" for "it ware" [^smith][^smm1796].

### The full words

These are the words as R. A. Smith printed them in The Scotish Minstrel, vol. 3, page 85, with spelling and punctuation as printed. The first verse is printed under the music and the second below it.

**Verse 1**
O, my love is like a red, red rose,
That's newly sprung in June!
O, my love is like a melodie
That sweetly play'd in tune!
As fair art thou, my bonnie lass,
So deep in love am I;
And I will love thee still, my dear,
'Till a' the seas gang dry.
'Till a' the seas gang dry my dear,
'Till a' the seas gang dry,
And I will love thee still, my dear,
'Till a' the seas gang dry.

**Verse 2**
Till a' the seas gang dry, my dear,
And the rocks melt wi' the sun;
And I will love thee still, my dear,
While the sands of life shall run.
But, fare thee weel, my only love!
O fare thee weel awhile!
And I will come again, my love,
Tho' 'twere ten thousand mile.
Tho' 'twere ten thousand mile, my love,
Tho' 'twere ten thousand mile;
And I will come again, my love,
Tho' 'twere ten thousand mile.

## How it is sung

It is often sung at Burns Suppers, held on or near 25 January, Burns's birthday [^wbc][^lfs]. The early printings ask for a gentle pace: the Museum marks both settings "Slow", Urbani "Largo con molta Espressione", and Graham's edition, arranged by T. M. Mudie, "Moderato" [^smm1796][^urbani][^graham]. Smith's page gives no speed [^smith].

Our arrangement takes the melody and words from Smith's page, in C and 4/4 as printed, without his grace notes [^smith]. We sing both his verses, slowly, for 2 to 4 singers: the soprano sings the tune, and the alto, tenor and bass sing the words beneath it. The parts are our own, written over Smith's piano chords, with breaths written in for beginners.

## Versions and other words

In 1799 George Thomson printed it in his Scotish Airs to a longer tune and stretched the words to fit, giving "And fare thee weel a little while", which Dick called "a water-logged addition" [^dick][^balladindex]. Smith's tune followed in the 1820s [^smith][^balladindex].

Smith's same volume has a different red-rose song on page 74, "O gin my love were yon red rose", by an unknown writer, to an air it calls The Red, Red Rose [^smith]. In June 1793 Burns had sent that older fragment to Thomson, calling its thought "inexpressibly beautiful" [^stenhouse][^dick][^gebbie5].

## Singing it

- [Soprano](/songs/red-red-rose/soprano): you sing Smith's tune, middle C up to G. Practise the second line: on "O, my" you run up C, D, E, then drop a tenth to middle C for "love". "So deep in love" leaps from G up to F, and the next line reaches your top G on "And I will". You end on a long C.
- [Alto](/songs/red-red-rose/alto): you start on middle C, and on "love is" you share the tune's note. Then you move mostly a third or sixth below it, from the B below middle C to the B above. On the "I;" of "am I;" you rise to that B, breathe, and sing it again for a full beat on "And" while the tune sings G and F above. You end on E.
- [Tenor](/songs/red-red-rose/tenor): your line lives on G and the C above it, within F to D. You step up to A on "bonnie lass" and "only love", and to D on the "I;" of "am I;" and the "-while!" of "awhile!", holding D a full beat on the "And" after. The low F comes once in each verse, on "seas" and on "thou-". You end on G.
- [Bass](/songs/red-red-rose/bass): you sing from low G to the G above. As the tune climbs back from middle C on "is like a", you walk down E, D, C beneath it. Lines end D, low G, C on "seas gang dry". The big moment is the "I;" of "am I;" on high G, then an octave drop to low G held for "And". You end on C.

## Questions

### Who did Robert Burns write A Red, Red Rose for?

No early printing names anyone, and Burns's note on his manuscript, as Stenhouse and Dick quote it, is only about the tune [^stenhouse][^dick]. The record points to older songs instead: his lines echo chapbook farewells, and his sister said their mother sang an older form [^graham][^hh3]. The story that a Lieutenant Hinches wrote the original for his sweetheart came from Peter Buchan, and Graham, Henley and Henderson all doubted it [^graham][^hh3].

### Is A Red, Red Rose a poem or a song?

A song first: its earliest printings, Urbani's in 1794, the Museum's in 1796 and Thomson's in 1799, all give it with music, and Burns sent it to Johnson with a note naming its tune [^urbani][^smm1796][^dick][^stenhouse]. It is read as a poem too; the critic William Hazlitt praised it among Burns's love songs [^graham][^gebbie4].

### Which part sings the tune?

The soprano sings the tune, Smith's Low down in the Broom [^smith]. With two singers you have the tune and the alto; with three, add the bass, which mostly sings the roots of the chords; the tenor makes four.
