# Decisions row (text for docs/decisions.md, append only)

- 2026-11-18, the arranger (Hillel, after his Reddit answer on which prayers Reform and Conservative shuls sing: "Yes, add the Shema"): **Shema Yisrael joins the Jewish songs, to the familiar congregational tune, which is not Sulzer's own.** The tune known as "Sulzer's Shema" is a different melody from the Shema Salomon Sulzer (died 1890) printed in Schir Zion I (Nos. 28, 70 and 104 in Joseph Sulzer's revised edition, read on the shulmusic.org scans): the two share only the 3/4 time, the first note and the closing "Adoshem echad". Geoffrey Goldberg (Journal of Synagogue Music 44/1, 2019, pp. 44 to 45 and 52) argues the familiar tune should be attributed to Cantor Jacob Beimel (died 1944), from his manuscript (an older manuscript by Alois Kaiser shares its opening only), and calls its earliest printing Israel and Samuel E. Goldfarb, Synagogue Melodies for the High Holy Days (Brooklyn, 1926), No. 18 "Sh'ma Yisroel" with No. 19 "Boruch Shem" ("On Yom Kippur add"), p. 17: one unison line for the congregation, G major, 3/4, marked "Traditional". No earlier printing was found (Union Hymnal 1897 and 1914: Alois Kaiser's Shema; Goldfarb's Friday Evening Melodies 1918 and the Society of American Cantors' Shir Zion 1904: other tunes; Idelsohn's Jewish Song Book of 1928 could not be read online).
  - **Rights (Hillel, 2026-11-19: "Melody only, our voicing"):** the 1926 book is melody only throughout (every number one line for the cantor or the congregation, no harmony or accompaniment; read on the title page and pp. 8 to 19 of the scan), and it names the Goldfarbs where the work is theirs ("I. Goldfarb" on No. 8, "Adapted by I. Goldfarb" on No. 26); this Shema it marks "Traditional". So we take only the melody line, attributed to Beimel, and our SATB is our own, written to the voicing rules (there was no printed harmony to copy). Makers: Jacob Beimel (`music`, died 1944) alone; the Goldfarbs (died 1967 and 1978) are credited in the story and licence as the earliest known printing, not as makers. Printed in the US in 1926, so public domain there; elsewhere free where life plus 70 from 1944 has run (geo-rights reads the makers). Prepared alternative, not used: the Goldfarbs as `edition` makers (parked build history).
  - **What we sing:** the page as printed, in G, at 72: the Shema, then Baruch shem to the same six bars, as the page sets it for Yom Kippur (the story says it is said quietly on other days). Fermatas held into an added bar ("nu," of "Elokeinu," and "to" of "malchuto", so "A-do-" and "le'-o-" stay upbeats; the end of the Shema before Baruch shem); breaths of 0.75 beat after "Yisrael," and "Baruch shem,"; the print's two G eighths on "k'-" sung as one G on "ke-". Words in modern Israeli pronunciation from the print's Ashkenazi, the names sung as Adoshem and Elokeinu. 2 to 4 singers, beginner, genre jewish and traditional (its festival tag: every day).
  - **Our own SATB** (the print has one line): G, E minor 7, C, A minor 7, G over D, D (the half close on "Elokeinu"), E minor 7 over D, D, G over D, D7, G; the alto mostly a third or a sixth under the tune, D, C, B under the close.
  - Choral review (an independent reviewer, the scan read again): the tune and underlay match the print note for note; no parallels or crossings at any onset; no doubled F-sharp. Fixes: chord labels added at 14 and 38 (E minor 7 over D, then D at 14.5 and 38.5); four wording fixes in the licence and description (the print has no slurs; "manuscript", singular; no claim that "most" synagogues sing it). Accepted exceptions, all judged sound by the reviewer: the alto's two-note slur G to F-sharp on "to" (a held note would clash or leave a bare fourth); the third of A minor 7 (two eighths on "E-lo-" and "mal-chu-") and of D7 ("e-", "va'-") only in the tenor, so 2 and 3 singers hear those chords without it, with the alto walking D, C, B in sixths under the tune into a complete last chord.
