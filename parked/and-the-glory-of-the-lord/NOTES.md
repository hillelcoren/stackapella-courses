## The source (read from the page images)

1. **The Messiah, a sacred oratorio, composed in the year 1741 by G. F. Handel. Edited, and the additional accompaniments largely re-written, by Ebenezer Prout. Full score.** "Ent. Sta. Hall. Price 31/6. London: Novello and Company, Limited, and Novello, Ewer and Co., New York. Copyright, 1902, by Novello and Company, Limited. The right of Public Representation and Performance is reserved." (title page, leaf n6, read from the image). Plate 11570. The only date in the printing is the 1902 copyright line; the library catalogues it as 1902; the vocal score's later printing (below) names H. W. Gray as the American agents instead of Novello, Ewer and Co. University of Toronto, Faculty of Music copy (stamped June 18, 1976; call number M2000 H22M4 1902): archive.org/details/messiahsacredora00hand_0.
   - The archive's OCR page index is the image leaf plus one (title page: image n6, OCR index 7). Preface signed "EBENEZER PROUT. London, April, 1902." (leaf n22).
   - No. 4, Chorus, "And the glory of the Lord": pp. 19 to 35, leaves n44 to n60. Allegro, crotchet = 100 (printed three times on p. 19). A major, 3/4, 138 bars, every tenth bar numbered. Rehearsal letters A (bar 33), B (51), C (68), D (84), E (102), F (124). Adagio from bar 136; fermata on the last chord. Soprano, alto and bass in treble and bass clefs, the tenor in the tenor clef. The voices rest in bars 1 to 10, 39 to 42 and 74 to 75 (the orchestra alone).
   - Preface, p. vi (leaf n11): Handel sets English words "the Italian method", two vowels in two words on one note; "The first occurs in the chorus 'And the glory of the Lord,' which the composer sets thus" (And the glo-ry, the glo-ry of the Lord, with "ry of" under one crotchet). "Most modern editions spoil the passage, either by printing two quavers on the first beat of the bar ... or by so dividing the words that 'of the' is to be sung on the third crotchet; both readings are equally bad ... It is just as easy to sing 'glo-ryof' or 'glo-ryand' in two syllables as 'glo-rious'; I have therefore had no hesitation in restoring Handel's reading, to the great benefit of the musical effect." The other places: the Hallelujah (above) and "Worthy is the Lamb".
   - Preface, p. viii (leaf n13), No. 4: "The division of the syllables in the opening subject has been already spoken of (p. vi). In this edition the phrase is printed throughout as Handel wrote it. In one place near the end of the movement (bar 107), where the composer had introduced an elision" (and the glo-ry, and the glo-ry, the glo-ry of the Lord, printed in music: our soprano's bars 106 to 110 note for note) "the word 'and' is omitted, as in all the printed editions, the sense being quite complete without it." Bars 12 and 13: the printed (Mozart-Hiller) accompaniment of the alto's first entry is "quite contrary to the spirit of the music"; Prout puts back "the simpler harmonic progression which Handel's bass naturally suggests" (his example's bass, rest, C sharp, A | D, B, E | A, is the Bassi line read for the chords here). "In two places (bars 17 to 29, and 63 to 68), in which no accompaniment but that of the basses is given to the chorus, I have doubled the voice parts in unison" (organ, or wind).
   - Preface, pp. vii (leaf n12): the sources Prout collated: the facsimile of Handel's autograph (Sacred Harmonic Society, 1868; Chrysander, 1892, the one he used), the "Dublin score" in Christopher Smith's hand from which Handel conducted the first performance (then at St. Michael's College, Tenbury, the Rev. Sir Frederick Ouseley's bequest), Otto Goldschmidt's Smith copy (once Dr. William Hayes's), the Hamburg score (through Goldschmidt), Dr. W. H. Cummings's volume of transposed songs; printed full scores of Walsh ("or, more accurately, Randall and Abell") and Arnold; Mozart-score editions (Breitkopf, Addison, Novello, the London Handel Society, Peters, Robert Franz).
2. **Prout's vocal score** (Novello's Original Octavo Edition, "Copyright, 1902"; preface London, October 1902; New York agents The H. W. Gray Co., so a later printing), Coventry University copy: archive.org/details/bwb_KT-315-920. Same text as the full score (its preface says so). Not used: its printing date is unknown.
3. **The words in 1750:** "Messiah. An Oratorio. As it is perform'd at the Theatre-Royal in Covent-Garden. Set to musick by Mr. Handel." (1750), wordbook, p. 4 (leaf n3): "CHORUS. And the Glory of the Lord shall be revealed, and all Flesh shall see it together, for the Mouth of the Lord hath spoken it." archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_messiah-an-oratorio-as_jennens-charles_1750. A fact for the story: the words as the London audience read them eight years after Dublin.

## People and dates

- **George Frideric Handel**, music: Grove's Dictionary, 2nd edition, vol. II (archive grovesdictionary0002jafu_n7d2, OCR text in ~/Code/arranger-keep/carols/ocoe-build/search), "Handel Festival" article: Handel died "on Saturday, April 14, 1759" (against Dr. Warren's Good Friday, April 13). Nationality left out of makers (born in Halle; not checked on a source here).
- **Charles Jennens**, words: Dictionary of National Biography, vol. 29 (Smith, Elder, 1892), archive.org/details/dictionaryofnati0029sidn_p5j0, leaf 332: "JENNENS, CHARLES (1700-1773), friend of Handel ... He also wrote the words for Handel's 'Saul' (1735), 'Messiah' (1742), and 'Belshazzar' (1745) ... Jennens died unmarried on 20 Nov. 1773". Also: of Gopsall, Leicestershire; "nicknamed by his neighbours 'Solyman the Magnificent'"; Handel left him two pictures by Denner; Lord Aylesford inherited his music, with Handel autographs and Smith's scores.
- **Ebenezer Prout**, editor: Grove, 2nd edition, vol. III (Macmillan, 1907, this printing 1910), addenda: "PROUT, EBENEZER ... Hackney, Dec. 5, 1909." (archive grovesdictionary0003jafu_t6h5; OCR text as above.)
- Grove vol. III, "MESSIAH" (a lead for the story, from the OCR text, not seen on the page image): "Oratorio by Handel; libretto from Holy Scripture by Charles Jennens. Composition commenced August 22, 1741; first part completed August 28; second part, Sept. 6; third part, Sept. 12; instrumentation, etc., filled in, Sept. 14; in all twenty-four days only. First performed ... in the Music Hall, Fishamble Street, Dublin, for the benefit of the Society for relieving Prisoners, the Charitable Infirmary, and Mercer's Hospital, April 13, 1742."
- The words are Isaiah 40:5 in the King James Version (1611); not checked on a scan of a Bible here.

Every maker died by 1925 (Handel 1759, Jennens 1773, Prout 1909) and the printing is 1902: the rights bar is met. The King James text's UK Crown letters patent is the open question already with the lawyer (The Lord Bless You).

## What is sung

Handel's four voice parts, every pitch, rhythm and word as Prout prints them, from the alto's entry in bar 11 to the end, written a major third lower (F major). The orchestra is left out; its interludes become a bar's rest each; the Adagio is written at 1.5 times its values; breaths are written in. All in the licence and DECISION.md.

## Reading method

Every bar of every voice read by eye from crops of the full-resolution page images (leaves n44 to n60), every pitch measured against the staff lines with headat.py (a notehead's centre against the five lines found in the same column), the rhythms and words by eye. Checks on the reading: every bar sums to three crotchets (trans.py refuses one that doesn't); with all four voices singing, every beat but three makes a chord, and those three are Handel's suspensions (bars 37 and 100); Prout's printed example of bars 106 to 110 (p. viii) matches the soprano note for note, and his example of the bass under bars 12 and 13 matches the Bassi as read.

## Uncertain

- The tenor's crotchet D sharp on "the" in bar 77 is printed with a hollow-looking head in this copy (ink); read as a crotchet, as the bar's count needs.
- The chords in the soprano's unaccompanied bars (58, 108, 109) and the soprano and alto's duet (84 to 88, the Bassi resting) are set by hand from Handel's harmony for the same notes elsewhere in the chorus.
